A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 39 of 39

The prolongation of commands made Rome servile Discourses III.24, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that the prolongation of commands in time made Rome servile, because fewer men were trained in command and a long-serving commander won his army to himself. Guicciardini has no doubt that prorogation was a great occasion for anyone wishing to seize the republic, but holds that the chief foundation of the ills was the city's corruption, which bred the bloody divisions that lead free peoples to tyranny, and the extraordinary prorogations themselves; Sulla's command had not been prorogued when he first fought Marius. He therefore concludes that prorogation was useful and holy while Rome was uncorrupt, and that once it was corrupt civil wars arose even without it; and, it can be concluded, without it Caesar and the others would not have lacked the will or the means to trouble the republic another way.

3.24.014 notes2 variants
Se si considera bene il procedere della Repubblica romana, si vedrà due cose essere state cagione della risoluzione di quella Repubblica; l'una furono le contenzioni che nacquero dalla legge Agraria; l'altra la prolungazione degl'Imperj: le quali cose se fussero state conosciute bene da principio, e fattivi debiti rimedj, sarebbe stato il vivere libero più lungo, e per avventura più quieto.
If one considers well the proceeding of the Roman republic, one will see two things to have been the cause of the dissolution of that republic; the one was the contentions that arose from the Agrarian law; the other the prolongation of commands: which things, if they had been known well from the beginning, and due remedies made for them, free living would have been longer, and, it may be, more quiet.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The chapter opens on a diagnosis, conditioned: "if one considers well" the proceeding of the Roman republic, two things will be seen to have been the cause of its dissolution, the contentions that arose from the Agrarian law and the prolongation of commands. The rubric names only the second, and the chapter treats only the second; the first is the subject of an earlier chapter (I.37, the editor's cross-reference). The counterfactual that closes the sentence is bounded twice: had the two things been known well from the beginning and remedied, free living would have been longer, and, "it may be", more quiet. Machiavelli does not say that the republic would have lasted, only that its free living would have been longer (the editor's reading).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not mention the Agrarian law. He grants the prorogation of commands to have been a great occasion to whoever wished to occupy the republic, and sets the principal foundation of the ills elsewhere, in the corruption of the city Guicciardini .01.

3.24.023 notes1 variant
E benché quanto alla prolungazione dell'Imperio, non si vegga che in Roma nascesse mai alcuno tumulto, nondimeno si vede in fatto, quanto nuocé alla Città quella autorità che i cittadini per tali deliberazioni presero. E se gli altri cittadini, a chi era prorogato il Magistrato, fussero stati savj e buoni, come fu L. Quinzio, non si sarebbe incorso in questo inconveniente.
And albeit, as to the prolongation of command, one does not see that any tumult ever arose in Rome, nonetheless one sees in fact how much that authority which the citizens took through such deliberations did harm to the city. And if the other citizens to whom the magistracy was prorogued had been wise and good, as L. Quinctius was, one would not have incurred this inconvenience.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The second cause is set apart from the first by its effect: one does not see that any tumult ever arose in Rome over the prolongation of command, where the Agrarian contentions, by implication, made tumult (the editor's reading). Its harm is seen "in fact", in the authority that the citizens took through such deliberations. The paragraph's conditional names the remedy that was lacking: had the other citizens to whom the magistracy was prorogued been wise and good, as Lucius Quinctius was, the inconvenience would not have been incurred. The fault is placed in the men who accepted prorogation, not in the institution alone (the editor's reading).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's first sentence names what prorogation gave the ambitious: it was an instrument for making friends of the soldiers and a following with kings and in foreign nations or provinces Guicciardini .01. That it answers Machiavelli's harm without tumult is the editor's reading.

His goodness is of a notable example, because, a convention of accord having been made between the plebs and the Senate, and the plebs having prolonged the command to the tribunes for a year, judging them apt to be able to resist the ambition of the nobles, the Senate, out of rivalry with the plebs, and so as not to seem less than it, wished to prolong the consulship to L. Commentary · 3

Argument. Quinctius' goodness is the example. The plebs prolonged the command to the tribunes for a year, judging them able to resist the ambition of the nobles; the Senate, out of rivalry with the plebs and so as not to seem less than it, wished to prolong his consulship; he wholly denied it, on the ground that bad examples are to be extinguished, not increased with a worse one, and wished that new consuls be made. The paragraph closes on a second counterfactual, wider than the first: had that goodness and prudence been in all the Roman citizens, the usage of prolonging magistracies would not have been introduced, and from it the prolongation of commands would not have followed, which in time ruined the republic. The chain runs from magistracies prolonged at Rome to commands prolonged in the field (the editor's reading).

History. Livy places the episode in the year of Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus' consulship, he having been made consul in place of the dead Valerius (Livy 3.19); the next year's consuls are named at 3.22. He reports a compromise, the tribunes promising to bow to the senate's authority, after which the senate resolved that for the future the continuing of magistrates and the re-election of the same tribunes was against the public interest; the tribunes were re-elected over the consuls' protests; the senate, so as not to yield to the plebs, re-elected Quinctius; he refused, in a speech that ironically bids the fathers imitate the thoughtless crowd and not yield to the multitude's rashness, and the consuls by a joint edict forbade any vote for him (Livy 3.21; the editor's references, checked against the Latin and Roberts's public-domain English). Machiavelli's "convention of accord" may answer to that compromise, but he does not say that the tribunes' re-election broke it, and he gives the plebs a reason of their own (the editor's reading). What the plebs prolonged is the tribunes' office; Machiavelli calls it Imperio, the chapter's word, though the tribunes of the plebs held no imperium (the editor's note).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not take up Quinctius; his example of a command not prorogued is Sulla's Guicciardini .01. Quinctius' poverty is the subject of the next chapter, III.25, which Guicciardini does not answer.

The first to whom command was prorogued was P. Philo, who, being in camp at the city of Palaeopolis, and the end of his consulship coming, and it seeming to the Senate that he had that victory in hand, they did not send him the successor, but made him proconsul; so that he was the first proconsul. Which thing, even though moved by the Senate for public utility, was that which in time made Rome servile.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The first prorogation of command is named and explained: Publilius Philo, at the end of his consulship, before Palaeopolis, with the victory, as it seemed to the Senate, in his hand, was not sent a successor but made proconsul. The decision was "moved by the Senate for public utility"; the paragraph grants the motive and still names the thing as that which "in time" made Rome servile. The judgment is of consequence, not of intention (the editor's reading).

History. Quintus Publilius Philo, consul for the second time with Lucius Cornelius Lentulus, was given the war against Palaeopolis, the Greek city not far from where Neapolis now stands (Livy 8.22); as the elections approached, with the siege near its end, an arrangement was made with the tribunes of the plebs to propose to the people that, his consulship ended, he should act as proconsul until the war with the Greeks was finished (Livy 8.23). Livy notes that two things then befell him first, a prorogation of command never before made, and a triumph after his term of office had ended (Livy 8.26, acto honore) (the editor's references, checked against the Latin and Roberts's public-domain English; traditionally 327-326 BC). Machiavelli's P. Filone gives the praenomen P., where Livy has Q., Quintus; and in Livy the prorogation passes by a vote of the people, proposed through the tribunes, the senate not named there as its mover, where Machiavelli gives the judgment and the act to the Senate (the editor's reading).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's list of what did not move Caesar's command begins with "the utility of the republic" Guicciardini .01; that it answers Machiavelli's "public utility" is the editor's reading.

3.24.056 notes
Perché quanto più i Romani si discostarono con le armi, tanto più pareva loro tale prorogazione necessaria, e più l'usarono. La qual cosa fece due inconvenienti. L'uno, che meno numero di uomini si esercitarono negl'Imperj, e si venne per questo a ristringere la riputazione in pochi; l'altro, che stando un cittadino assai tempo Comandatore d'uno esercito, se lo guadagnava, e facevaselo partigiano; perché quello esercito col tempo dimenticava il Senato, e riconosceva quello Capo. Per questo Silla e Mario poterono trovare soldati che contro al bene pubblico li seguitassero; per questo Cesare potette occupare la patria.
For the more the Romans went to a distance with arms, the more such prorogation seemed necessary to them, and the more they used it. Which thing made two inconveniences. The one, that a lesser number of men were exercised in commands, and one came through this to restrict reputation to few; the other, that a citizen, staying much time commander of an army, gained it for himself, and made it his partisan; because that army in time forgot the Senate, and acknowledged that head. Through this Sulla and Marius could find soldiers who would follow them against the public good; through this Caesar could occupy the fatherland.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The mechanism. The further the Romans carried their arms, the more necessary prorogation seemed to them, and the more they used it; and it made two inconveniences. The first: fewer men were exercised in commands, so that reputation came to be restricted to few. The second: a citizen who long commanded an army gained it for himself and made it his partisan, because the army in time forgot the Senate and acknowledged that head. The conclusions are drawn twice by per questo: through this Sulla and Marius could find soldiers to follow them against the public good; through this Caesar could occupy the fatherland. The seeming necessity grows with distance, and the use grows with it; the chapter does not blame the Romans for extending their arms (the editor's reading).

History. Sulla and Marius fought the first civil war of 88-87 BC; Caesar crossed the Rubicon with the army of his Gallic command in 49 BC (the editor's references).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini says that the command had not been prorogued to Sulla when he first came to blows with Marius, the cause being the division between nobility and plebs Guicciardini .01; that this contests Machiavelli's first example is the editor's reading, Machiavelli not having said that Sulla's command was prorogued at that clash. He grants that the long command of Gaul increased Caesar's wealth and that Caesar's was an extraordinary prorogation, but gives the prorogation itself another cause, the conspiracy with Pompey and Crassus to occupy the republic, and concludes, as what can be concluded, that without prorogations Caesar would not have lacked the thought or the capacity to trouble it another way (the editor's reading).

3.24.061 note1 variant
Che se mai i Romani non avessero prolungati li Magistrati e gl'Imperj, se non venivano sì tosto a tanta potenza, e se fussero stati più tardi gli acquisti loro, sarebbero ancora venuti più tardi nella servitù.
That if the Romans had never prolonged the magistracies and the commands, if they had not come so soon to so much power, and if their acquisitions had been later, they would also have come later into servitude.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The chapter ends on a third counterfactual, its terms stacked: had the Romans never prolonged magistracies and commands, had they not come so soon to so great a power, and had their acquisitions been later, they would also have come later into servitude. The conclusion is a delay, not an escape: servitude would have come later, not never. The conditions also widen the cause: power reached so soon and acquisitions made earlier stand beside prolongation as conditions of the timing of servitude, so that the closing sentence makes prolongation one cause among others, not the only one (the editor's reading). The 1554 edition, without the second se, makes the coming so soon to power a consequence of prolongation rather than a further condition (NM-DISC-III24-D004; D509).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's close is also counterfactual, and turned the other way: therefore, he says, it can be concluded that, had there not been the prorogations as well, neither Caesar nor the others who occupied the republic would have lacked the thought or the capacity to trouble it by another path Guicciardini .01. Where Machiavelli delays servitude by removing prolongation, Guicciardini concludes that its removal would not have taken from such men the thought or the means of troubling a corrupted city (the editor's reading).

Guicciardini

Considerazione sul capitolo XXIV

Considerazioni III.24

La prolungazione degl'imperii fece serva Roma.

The prolongation of commands made Rome servile.

Italian · 1857
English
3.24.0117 notes8 variants
Non è dubio che la prorogazione degli imperii fu occasione grande a chi volle occupare la republica; perché era instrumento da farsi amici i soldati e séguito co' re, e nelle nazioni o provincie forestiere, e a' capitani accresceva ricchezza con la quale potevano corrompere gli uomini, come fece a Cesare il lungo imperio di Gallia. Ma il fondamento principale de' mali fu la corruzione della città, la quale datasi alla avarizia, alle delizie, era in modo degenerata dagli antichi costumi, che ne nacquono le divisioni sanguinose della città, dalle quali sempre ne' populi liberi si viene alle tirannidi. Di quivi nacque la facilità di corrompere e' cittadini e' soldati, di qui potette sperare uno Catilina sanza imperio e sanza eserciti occupare la republica, di qui conjurazione di più potenti di dividersi fra loro gli imperii e gli eserciti, e con queste forze tenere bassi gli altri; di qui le prorogazioni estraordinarie degli imperii, come fu quella di Cesare, al quale non la utilità della republica, non la necessità della guerra, non la ammirazione della sua virtù, ma la conjurazione con Pompeo e Crasso di occupare la republica, fece imperio decemvirale. Non era stato prorogato lo imperio a Silla, quando la prima volta venne alle mani con Mario, ma ne fu causa la divisione tra la nobilità e la plebe, e avendo la plebe per capo Mario, fu forzata la nobilità cercarsi uno capo. Però conchiuggo che quando Roma non fu corrotta, le prorogazioni degli imperii e la continuazione del consulato, la quale ne' tempi difficili usorono molte volte, furono cosa utile e santa; ma corrotta la città, sursono le battaglie civili, e i semi delle tirannidi, etiam sanza la prorogazione degli imperii. E però si può conchiudere, che se non fussino state anche le prorogazioni, non sarebbe mancato né a Cesare né agli altri che occuporono la republica, né pensiero né facultà di travagliarla per altra via.
There is no doubt that the prorogation of commands was a great occasion to whoever wished to occupy the republic; because it was an instrument for making friends of the soldiers, and a following with kings and in foreign nations or provinces, and for the captains it increased wealth, with which they could corrupt men, as the long command of Gaul did for Caesar. But the principal foundation of the ills was the corruption of the city, which, having given itself to avarice, to delights, had degenerated from the ancient customs in such a way that there arose from it the sanguinary divisions of the city, from which in free peoples one always comes to tyrannies. From there arose the facility of corrupting the citizens, the soldiers; from here one Catiline could hope, without command and without armies, to occupy the republic; from here [a] conspiracy of several powerful [men] to divide among themselves the commands and the armies, and with these forces to hold the others low; from here the extraordinary prorogations of commands, as was that of Caesar, for whom not the utility of the republic, not the necessity of war, not the admiration of his virtù, but the conspiracy with Pompey and Crassus to occupy the republic, made [a] decemviral command. The command had not been prorogued to Sulla when he came to blows with Marius the first time, but the cause of it was the division between the nobility and the plebs, and, the plebs having Marius for head, the nobility was forced to seek a head for itself. Therefore I conclude that when Rome was not corrupted, the prorogations of commands and the continuation of the consulship, which in difficult times they used many times, were a useful and holy thing; but, the city corrupted, there rose the civil battles and the seeds of tyrannies, even without the prorogation of commands. And therefore one can conclude that, if there had not been the prorogations as well, there would not have failed, either to Caesar or to the others who occupied the republic, either the thought or the capacity to trouble it by another path.
Commentary · 5

Argument. The reply has the shape of a grant followed by a relocation of the principal ground. It opens on assent, "there is no doubt": the prorogation of commands was a great occasion to whoever wished to occupy the republic, because it was an instrument for making friends of the soldiers and a following with kings and in foreign nations or provinces, and it increased the captains' wealth, with which they could corrupt men, as the long command of Gaul did for Caesar. Guicciardini's word is prorogazione, the second of Machiavelli's two words, and an occasion is not a cause (the editor's reading).

Note. The principal foundation follows at once, introduced by Ma: the principal foundation of the ills was the corruption of the city, which, given to avarice and delights, had degenerated from its ancient customs, so that the sanguinary divisions arose from which free peoples always come to tyranny. Four consequences are drawn, from the corruption or from the divisions it bred, by the anaphora "from there ... from here": the facility of corrupting citizens and soldiers; one Catiline's hope of occupying the republic without command or armies; the conspiracy of several powerful men to divide the commands and armies among themselves; and the extraordinary prorogations, Caesar's among them, whose cause was not the utility of the republic, the necessity of war or the admiration of his virtù, but the conspiracy with Pompey and Crassus (the editor's reading). Prorogation thus appears twice, first as an occasion to those who wished to occupy the republic, then as an effect of corruption (the editor's reading).

Note. Of Sulla, Guicciardini says that the command had not been prorogued to him when he first came to blows with Marius; the cause was the division between nobility and plebs, the plebs having Marius for head, so that the nobility was forced to seek one too. That this turns Machiavelli's example against him is the editor's reading. The conclusion is introduced by Però, therefore: while Rome was not corrupted, the prorogations of commands and the continuation of the consulship, used many times in difficult times, were "a useful and holy thing"; once the city was corrupted, civil battles and the seeds of tyranny rose even without them. A second conclusion, introduced by E però and impersonal, closes the reply: one can conclude that, had there not been the prorogations as well, neither Caesar nor the others who occupied the republic would have lacked the thought or the capacity to trouble it by another path. The reply does not deny that prorogation did harm; it denies that it was the principal foundation of the ills, and concludes that without it such men would not have lacked the thought or the means to trouble the republic another way (the editor's reading).

History. Caesar held his Gallic command from 58 BC; it was extended for five years by the agency of Pompey and Crassus, consuls in 55 BC (Suetonius, Divus Iulius 24; the editor's reference). Pompey, Crassus and Caesar made their compact in 60-59 BC (Suetonius 19). Catiline's conspiracy is of 63 BC, Catiline holding no magistracy; Sulla's first march on Rome, against Marius and the tribune Sulpicius, is of 88 BC, Sulla then consul (the editor's references, against standard chronology). Canestrini prints decemvirale, decemviral, where the 1933 edition prints decennale, decennial; the ten years of Caesar's command support the 1933's reading (the editor's reading), and the edition keeps Canestrini's provisionally (O50).

Cross-reference. Machiavelli's two causes, the Agrarian contentions and prolongation, and his diagnosis "if one considers well" Machiavelli .01; the harm without tumult, which Guicciardini's opening may be read as taking up (the editor's reading) Machiavelli .02; the "public utility" of the first prorogation, which "not the utility of the republic" may be read as answering (the editor's reading) Machiavelli .04; Sulla and Marius, and Caesar, "through this" Machiavelli .05; and the closing counterfactual, which Guicciardini turns: Machiavelli's servitude would have come later without prolongation; Guicciardini's republic, as one can conclude, would not have lacked men with the thought and the capacity to trouble it by another path Machiavelli .06. The reply names corruption, a word that does not occur in Machiavelli's chapter. This is the last of the Considerazioni; nothing in the reply marks it as an end (the editor's note).
