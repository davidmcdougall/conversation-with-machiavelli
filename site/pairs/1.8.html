A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 8 of 39

Natural in a free city Discourses I.8, and Guicciardini's reply

Machiavelli tells how Manlius Capitolinus, envying Camillus, sowed calumnies among the plebs and was imprisoned, and draws the rule: calumnies are detestable, and the remedy is to open many places to accusation and punish the calumniators; Florence, which had no such order, was ruined by them, as the calumny against Giovanni Guicciardini showed. Guicciardini grants the conclusion and denies the remedy. Calumnies are so natural in a free city that it is difficult and perhaps impossible to remove them: false charges spread in a multitude that believes ill sooner than good, Rome with its easy accusations was full of them, and no way of republic, except on paper, provides against every disorder. But they often die of themselves, a grave citizen calumniated does not turn on the republic, and the scandalous ones are put down. Machiavelli's unnamed citizen is Cosimo de' Medici, and his example is a dream: prudence and excessive wealth, not calumnies, opened Cosimo's path. As for Giovanni Guicciardini, the example was begged for want of others: he was calumniated unjustly, but his case did not divide the city; the city's discords made his case.

NM-DISC.1.8

Capitolo VIII

Quanto le accuse sono utili alle Repubbliche, tanto sono perniziose le calunnie.

As much as accusations are useful to republics, so much are calumnies pernicious.

Italian · 1824
English
1.8.01
Nonostante che la virtù di Furio Camillo, poi ch'egli ebbe liberato Roma dalla oppressione dei Francesi, avesse fatto che tutt'i cittadini romani, senza parer loro torsi reputazione o grado, cedevano a quello, nondimeno Manlio Capitolino non poteva sopportare che gli fusse attribuito tanto onore e tanta gloria; parendogli quanto alla salute di Roma, per avere salvato il Campidoglio, aver meritato quanto Camillo; e quanto all'altre belliche laudi, non essere inferiore a lui. Dimodoché carico d'invidia, non potendo quietarsi per la gloria di quello, e veggendo non potere seminare discordia infra i Padri, si volse alla Plebe, seminando varie opinioni sinistre tra quella. E intra l'altre cose che diceva, era come il tesoro, il quale si era adunato insieme per dare ai Francesi, e poi non dato loro, era stato usurpato da privati cittadini; e quando si riavesse si poteva convertirlo in pubblica utilità, alleggerendo la Plebe dai tributi, o da qualche privato debito. Queste parole poterono assai nella Plebe, talché cominciò avere concorso, e a fare a sua posta tumulti assai nella città: la qual cosa dispiacendo al Senato, e parendogli di momento e pericolosa, creò un Dittatore, perché e' riconoscesse questo caso, e frenasse l'impeto di Manlio. Ondeché subito il Dittatore lo fece citare, e condussonsi in pubblico all'incontro l'uno dell'altro, il Dittatore in mezzo de' Nobili, e Manlio in mezzo della Plebe. Fu domandato Manlio che dovesse dire, appresso a chi fusse questo tesoro che si diceva, perché n'era così desideroso il Senato d'intenderlo come la Plebe; a che Manlio non rispondeva particolarmente, ma andando fuggendo, diceva come non era necessario dire loro quello che e' si sapevano, tantoché il Dittatore lo fece mettere in carcere.
Notwithstanding that the virtù of Furius Camillus, after he had freed Rome from the oppression of the French, had made all the Roman citizens, without its seeming to them that they took from themselves reputation or degree, yield to that man, nonetheless Manlius Capitolinus could not bear that so much honour and so much glory should be attributed to that man; it seeming to him that, as to the safety of Rome, through having saved the Capitol, he had merited as much as Camillus; and as to the other praises of war, he was not inferior to him. So that, laden with envy, not being able to quiet himself because of the glory of that man, and seeing that he could not sow discord among the Fathers, he turned to the plebs, sowing various sinister opinions among it. And among the other things that he said, was how the treasure which had been gathered together to give to the French, and then not given to them, had been usurped by private citizens; and if it were had back, it could be converted to public utility, lightening the plebs of the tributes, or of some private debt. These words could do much with the plebs, so that he began to have concourse, and to make many tumults at his pleasure in the city: which thing displeasing the senate, and it seeming to it of moment and dangerous, it created a dictator, that he might take cognizance of this case and rein in the impetus of Manlius. Whereupon the dictator at once had him summoned, and they betook themselves in public to meet one another, the dictator in the midst of the nobles, and Manlius in the midst of the plebs. Manlius was asked to say with whom this treasure was that was spoken of, for the senate was as desirous to understand it as the plebs; to which Manlius was not answering particularly, but, going about evading, was saying that it was not necessary to tell them what they themselves knew, so that the dictator had him put in prison.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The story first. Camillus' virtù had made the citizens yield to him without feeling diminished; Manlius, who had saved the Capitol, could not bear it. Unable to sow discord among the Fathers, he turned to the plebs and spread the charge that the gold gathered to ransom Rome from the French had been taken by private citizens, and could be recovered to relieve the plebs of tributes and debts. He gathered a following and made tumults; the senate made a dictator; summoned and asked in public who held the treasure, Manlius would only say that they knew already, and the dictator put him in prison. He would not bring the charge to proof. That is what makes it, in .05, a calumny.

History. Livy 6.11–16, under 385 BC. The dictator was Aulus Cornelius Cossus, named ostensibly for a war with the Volsci, which he won, and turned on Manlius on his return; the gold was the ransom being weighed for the Gauls when Camillus arrived (5.48–49). Manlius was released (6.17), and in 384 was accused of aiming at kingship, condemned, and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (6.18–20).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini has named Manlius twice already: as one whom the consuls and dictators put down in defence of liberty (his reply to I.5), and as a favourite the people would not condemn until his design was out (his reply to I.7). Here he takes Machiavelli's example and turns it: the scandalous calumny is put down without the order Machiavelli asks for Guicciardini .01.

1.8.02
È da notare per questo testo, quanto siano nelle città libere e in ogni altro modo di vivere, detestabili le calunnie, e come per reprimerle si debbe non perdonare a ordine alcuno, che vi faccia a proposito. Né può essere migliore ordine a torle via, che aprire assai luoghi alle accuse, perché quanto le accuse giovano alle Repubbliche, tanto le calunnie nuocono: e dall'altra parte è questa differenza, che le calunnie non hanno bisogno di testimoni, né d'alcun altro particolare riscontro a provarle, in modoché ciascuno da ciascuno può esser calunniato; ma non può già essere accusato, avendo le accuse bisogno di riscontri veri, e di circostanze, che mostrino la verità dell'accusa. Accusansi gli uomini ai Magistrati, ai Popoli, ai Consigli; calunniansi per le piazze, e per le logge. Usasi più questa calunnia, dove si usa meno l'accusa, e dove le città sono meno ordinate a riceverle. Però uno ordinatore di una Repubblica debbe ordinare che si possa in quella accusare ogni cittadino, senz'alcuna paura, o senz'alcun sospetto; e fatto questo e bene osservato, debbe punire acremente i calunniatori; i quali non si possono dolere quando siano puniti, avendo i luoghi aperti a udire le accuse di colui che gli avesse per le logge calunniato. E dove non è bene ordinata questa parte, seguitano sempre disordini grandi; perché le calunnie irritano, e non gastigano i cittadini; e gl'irritati pensano di valersi, odiando più presto che temendo le cose che si dicono contro di loro.
It is to be noted, by this text, how detestable calumnies are, in free cities and in every other way of living, and how, to repress them, one must spare no order that may serve the purpose. Nor can there be a better order to take them away than to open many places to accusations, for as much as accusations benefit republics, so much do calumnies do harm: and on the other hand there is this difference, that calumnies have no need of witnesses, nor of any other particular corroboration to prove them, in such a way that each can be calumniated by each; but cannot, however, be accused, accusations having need of true corroborations, and of circumstances, that show the truth of the accusation. Men are accused to the magistracies, to the peoples, to the councils; they are calumniated about the squares and about the loggias. This calumny is used more where accusation is used less, and where cities are less ordered to receive them. Therefore an orderer of a republic must order that in it any citizen can be accused without any fear or without any suspicion; and, this done and well observed, he must punish the calumniators sharply; who cannot complain when they are punished, having the places open to hear the accusations of him whom he might have calumniated about the loggias. And where this part is not well ordered, great disorders always follow; for calumnies irritate and do not chastise the citizens; and the irritated think of availing themselves, hating rather than fearing the things that are said against them.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The rule. Calumnies are detestable in any form of living, and no order that serves to repress them should be spared. The best is to open many places to accusation: an accusation needs witnesses and corroboration, a calumny needs nothing, so anyone can calumniate anyone, and calumny is used most where accusation is used least. The orderer of a republic should let any citizen be accused without fear, and then punish calumniators sharply; they have no grievance, since the courts were open to them. Where this part is not ordered, calumny irritates without chastising, and the irritated look to their own remedies, hating rather than fearing what is said against them.

Cross-reference. The courts are I.7's — "to the people, or to any magistracy or council" — now in the plural; the irritated who "avail themselves" are I.7's citizens who, without an ordinary outlet, turn to extraordinary ways. Guicciardini grants the conclusion in Machiavelli's own word, detestabili Guicciardini .01.

1.8.03
Questa parte, come è detto, era bene ordinata in Roma, ed è stata sempre male ordinata nella nostra città di Firenze. E come a Roma questo ordine fece molto bene, a Firenze questo disordine fece molto male. E chi legge le Istorie di questa città, vedrà quante calunnie sono state in ogni tempo date a' suoi cittadini, che si sono adoperati nelle cose importanti di quella. Dell'uno dicevano, ch'egli aveva rubati danari al Comune; dell'altro, che non aveva vinto una impresa, per essere stato corrotto, e che quell'altro per sua ambizione aveva fatto il tale e tale inconveniente. Del che ne nasceva che da ogni parte ne surgeva odio, d'onde si veniva alla divisione, dalla divisione alle Sette, dalle Sette alla rovina. Che se fusse stato in Firenze ordine d'accusare i cittadini, e punire i calunniatori, non seguivano infiniti scandali che sono seguìti; perché quelli cittadini, o condannati o assoluti che fussero, non arebbono potuto nuocere alla città, e sarebbono stati accusati meno assai, che non ne erano calunniati, non si potendo, come ho detto, accusare come calunniare ciascuno. E intra l'altre cose, di che si è valuto alcuno cittadino, per venire alla grandezza sua, sono state queste calunnie, le quali venendo contra a' cittadini potenti, che allo appetito suo si opponevano, facevano assai per quello; perché pigliando la parte del Popolo, e confermandolo nella mala opinione ch'egli aveva di loro, se lo fece amico.
This part, as has been said, was well ordered in Rome, and has always been ill ordered in our city of Florence. And as at Rome this order did much good, at Florence this disorder did much ill. And whoever reads the histories of this city will see how many calumnies have been given in every time to its citizens who have been employed in its important things. Of one they said that he had robbed money from the Commune; of another, that he had not won an enterprise, through having been corrupted; and that that other, through his ambition, had done such and such an inconvenience. From which there arose that from every part hatred rose, whence one came to division, from division to sects, from sects to ruin. For if there had been in Florence an order for accusing the citizens and punishing the calumniators, there would not have followed the infinite scandals that have followed; for those citizens, whether condemned or acquitted, would not have been able to do harm to the city, and they would have been accused much less than they were calumniated, it not being possible, as I have said, to accuse each as one can calumniate each. And among the other things of which some citizen has availed himself to come to his greatness have been these calumnies, which, coming against powerful citizens who were opposing his appetite, did much for him; for, taking the part of the people, and confirming it in the ill opinion that it had of them, he made it his friend.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The part was well ordered in Rome and has always been ill ordered in Florence, whose histories are full of calumnies against citizens in public charge: one robbed the commune, another lost an enterprise for a bribe, a third did this or that from ambition. From them came hatred, division, sects and ruin, the chain of I.7 shortened. An order of accusation would have prevented it, since every citizen, condemned or acquitted, would have been unable to harm the city, and far fewer would have been accused than were calumniated. And among the other means some citizen used to come to greatness were calumnies against the powerful who stood in his way, taking the people's side and confirming its ill opinion of them.

Cross-reference. Machiavelli does not name the citizen. Guicciardini does, and calls the example a dream Guicciardini .01.

1.8.04
E benché se ne potesse addurre assai esempj, voglio essere contento solo d'uno. Era l'esercito Fiorentino a campo a Lucca, comandato da messer Giovanni Guicciardini, Commissario di quello. Vollono o i cattivi suoi governi, o la cattiva sua fortuna, che la espugnazione di quella città non seguisse. Pur comunque il caso stesse, ne fu incolpato messer Giovanni, dicendo com'egli era stato corrotto da' Lucchesi; la quale calunnia sendo favorita da' nimici suoi, condusse messer Giovanni quasi in ultima disperazione. E benché per giustificarsi ei si volesse mettere nelle mani del Capitano, nondimeno non si potette mai giustificare, per non essere modi in quella Repubblica da poterlo fare. Di che ne nacque assai sdegno tra gli amici di messer Giovanni, che erano la maggior parte degli uomini grandi, e infra coloro che desideravano fare novità in Firenze. La qual cosa, e per queste e per altre simili cagioni, tanto crebbe, che ne seguì la rovina di quella Repubblica.
And albeit many examples of it could be adduced, I wish to be content with one alone. The Florentine army was in the field before Lucca, commanded by messer Giovanni Guicciardini, its commissary. Either his bad governing or his bad fortune willed that the taking of that city by storm did not follow. Yet, however the case stood, messer Giovanni was blamed for it, it being said that he had been corrupted by the Lucchese; which calumny, being favoured by his enemies, brought messer Giovanni almost to the last desperation. And albeit, to justify himself, he wished to put himself in the hands of the Captain, nonetheless he could never justify himself, there not being ways in that republic by which to be able to do it. From which there arose much indignation among the friends of messer Giovanni, who were the greater part of the great men, and among those who desired to make novelty in Florence. Which thing, both for these and for other like causes, grew so much that the ruin of that republic followed from it.
Commentary · 3

Argument. One example. The Florentine army failed before Lucca under the commissary Giovanni Guicciardini; whether by his bad management or bad fortune, he was blamed and said to have been bribed by the Lucchese, and his enemies kept the charge alive. He offered to put himself in the hands of the Captain, and could not clear himself, because the republic had no way of doing it. Indignation arose among his friends, most of the great men, and among those who wanted change, and that, with other such causes, grew until it ruined the republic.

History. The war against Lucca of 1429–1433, in which Florence failed to take the city, with Milanese help for Lucca. The accusations against Giovanni Guicciardini, commissary in 1430, are told in Machiavelli's Istorie fiorentine (book 4), among the quarrels of the years before the change of regime of 1433–1434. Giovanni was of Francesco Guicciardini's own family.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini grants the want of a way to clear himself, which is Machiavelli's diagnosis, and affirms the injustice, which Machiavelli left open ("however the case stood"); and he reverses the cause: the city's discords made the case, not the case the discords Guicciardini .01.

1.8.05
Era adunque Manlio Capitolino calunniatore, e non accusatore; e i Romani mostrarono in questo caso appunto, come i calunniatori si debbono punire. Perché si debbe fargli diventare accusatori, e quando l'accusa si riscontri vera, o premiarli, o non punirli; ma quando la non si riscontri vera, punirli come fu punito Manlio.
Manlius Capitolinus was, then, a calumniator, and not an accuser; and the Romans showed in this very case how calumniators must be punished. For one must make them become accusers, and, when the accusation is corroborated true, either reward them or not punish them; but when it is not corroborated true, punish them as Manlius was punished.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The verdict on Manlius: a calumniator, not an accuser, and the Romans showed how calumniators are to be punished. The remedy turns them into accusers. If the charge is corroborated, reward or at least do not punish them; if not, punish them as Manlius was.

Cross-reference. The text shows only the prison of .01; in Livy the later death (6.20) was for aiming at kingship, and Machiavelli does not say which punishment he means. The chapter closes I.7's pairing of accusation and calumny (I.7 .07), and I.9 turns to the founder alone.

Variants

poi ch'egli ebbe liberato Roma dalla oppressione dei Francesi — 1824. The 1554 witness reads poi che egli hebbe libera Roma dallo assedio, & dalla oppressione de Franciosi. Giglio names the siege as well as the oppression.

quanto all'altre belliche laudi ... lo fece mettere in carcere — 1824. The 1554 witness reads quanto all'altre laudi, della guerra ... lo fece mettere in prigione. The same praises of war, and prison for gaol; no change of sense.

detestabili le calunnie — 1824. The 1554 witness reads dannose & pessime le calunnie. Giglio judges calumnies by their effects (damaging and very bad); the 1824 text says they are to be detested, the word Guicciardini's reply takes up (le calunnie sono detestabili).

in modoché ciascuno da ciascuno può esser calunniato ... Usasi più questa calunnia — 1824. The 1554 witness reads in modo che ciascuno puo essere calunniato ... Vsasi questa calunnia. Giglio has neither da ciascuno (anyone by anyone) nor the comparative più.

punire acremente ... le calunnie irritano ... e gl'irritati — 1824. The 1554 witness reads punire aspramente ... le calunnie commouono ... & gli commossi. Harshly for sharply; calumnies stir men up rather than irritate them.

contra a' cittadini potenti, che allo appetito suo si opponevano, facevano assai per quello; perché pigliando la parte del Popolo — 1824. The 1554 witness reads contro a Cittadini potenti, all'appetito suo si opponeuano, et faceuano assai per quello, perche pigliando la parte del gran popolo. Giglio has no relative che, so the calumnies themselves oppose his appetite; and il gran popolo, the great body of the people.

comandato da messer Giovanni Guicciardini, Commissario di quello — 1824. The 1554 witness reads commandato da Messer Giouanni Guicciardoni buō commissario di quello. Giglio misprints the name and calls him a good commissary.

Orthography, capitalization, word division and punctuation follow the governing witness throughout; no reading is hybridized between witnesses.

Notes

calunnie, calunniare, calunniatori. "Calumnies", "calumniate", "calumniators": the private charge, made without proof and without a court, set against accuse, accusare, accusatori, "accusations", "accuse", "accusers", the public charge before a court. One root each, held.

la virtù di Furio Camillo. "The virtù of Furius Camillus" (D16): the capacity that made the citizens yield to him. Camillus, dictator, recovered Rome after the Gallic sack of about 390 BC (Livy 5.49).

dei Francesi. "Of the French": Machiavelli's name for the Gauls, as in I.7. The 1554 edition has him free Rome "from the siege and from the oppression" (NM-DISC-I8-D001).

avesse fatto che ... cedevano a quello. "Had made all the Roman citizens ... yield to that man": the indicative cedevano states the yielding as fact. Quello is Camillus throughout the period, "that man", so that the English "him" cannot be taken for Manlius.

torsi reputazione o grado. "Took from themselves reputation or degree": yielding did not feel like self-deprivation.

reputazione o grado. Glossary, grado, "degree".

Manlio Capitolino. Marcus Manlius Capitolinus, who held the Capitol against the Gauls. Livy 6.11–20, for 385–384 BC: he took up the cause of debtors, accused the senators of keeping back the Gallic gold, and was imprisoned by the dictator Aulus Cornelius Cossus. Guicciardini named him in his replies to I.5 and I.7.

belliche laudi. "Praises of war" (glossary, laude).

i Padri. "The Fathers": the senators, patres.

opinioni sinistre. "Sinister opinions": opinione (D02).

il tesoro. "The treasure": the gold collected to ransom Rome from the Gauls, which Camillus' arrival made unnecessary.

dai tributi, o da qualche privato debito. "Of the tributes, or of some private debt".

poterono assai. "Could do much": potere absolute, as the people of I.7 "could" (D78 for the noun).

tumulti assai. "Many tumults" (glossary, assai of number).

cominciò avere concorso. "He began to have concourse": a crowd running together to him. Concorso is concorrere's noun, and the currere family is held in it (D01, D63).

a sua posta. "At his pleasure".

di momento. "Of moment": of weight. Guicciardini's reply ends on the same phrase (di più momento).

perché e' riconoscesse questo caso. "That he might take cognizance of this case": examine it as a judge. "Recognise" now means identify.

frenasse l'impeto. "Rein in the impetus": the bridle, as a freno (D88).

condussonsi ... all'incontro l'uno dell'altro. "They betook themselves ... to meet one another": condursi, reflexive.

andando fuggendo. "Going about evading": the progressive andare with the gerund.

non rispondeva ... diceva. "Was not answering ... was saying": the imperfects are durative; he went on evading until the dictator jailed him.

quello che e' si sapevano. "What they themselves knew": si is an ethic dative, not a reflexive object.

detestabili. "Detestable". The 1554 edition reads dannose & pessime, "damaging and very bad" (NM-DISC-I8-D003). Guicciardini's reply opens on the 1824 word: le calunnie sono detestabili.

si debbe non perdonare a ordine alcuno. "One must spare no order": perdonare a, to spare, leave unused. Debbe is "must", as in I.4 and I.5 (D120).

torle via. "Take them away" (D131).

giovano / nuocono. "Benefit" / "do harm" (D106).

dall'altra parte. "On the other hand" (D24).

riscontro, riscontri, si riscontri vera. "Corroboration", "corroborations", "is corroborated true": a matching of the charge against other evidence. One root, held to the last sentence, where the reward or the penalty turns on it.

ciascuno da ciascuno. "Each ... by each": everyone can be calumniated by everyone, but not everyone accused. Già is "however". The same ciascuno closes .03: "to accuse each as one can calumniate each". The 1554 edition has neither this nor più in "is used more" (NM-DISC-I8-D004).

ai Magistrati, ai Popoli, ai Consigli. "To the magistracies, to the peoples, to the councils" (D93): I.7's three courts, al Popolo, o a qualunque Magistrato o Consiglio, now in the plural.

per le piazze, e per le logge. "About the squares and about the loggias": the open places where Florentines met and talked.

di colui che gli avesse ... calunniato. "Of him whom he might have calumniated": gli is the subject (egli). The sentence slides from the calumniators, plural, to one calumniator, and the English follows it.

a riceverle. "To receive them": the pronoun is plural, though the nearest noun, l'accusa, is singular; it takes accusations in general.

Però. "Therefore" (D22).

uno ordinatore. "An orderer" (glossary).

senz'alcuna paura, o senz'alcun sospetto. "Without any fear or without any suspicion": of the accuser, who need fear nothing in accusing.

acremente. "Sharply". The 1554 edition reads aspramente, "harshly" (NM-DISC-I8-D005).

irritano, e non gastigano. "Irritate and do not chastise": the calumny wounds without punishing. Gastigare as in I.7.

pensano di valersi. "Think of availing themselves": of their own force, to get their own back. The verb returns of the citizen who "availed himself" of calumnies (.03), and of I.7's valersi di forze esterne.

odiando più presto che temendo. "Hating rather than fearing" (D135).

le Istorie. "The histories": the chronicles of Florence. Machiavelli's own Istorie fiorentine were later.

non aveva vinto una impresa, per essere stato corrotto. The comma is kept, so that the corruption is the alleged cause of the failure and not a manner of winning.

il tale e tale inconveniente. "Such and such an inconvenience": fare is "do" here, as "did much good" just before.

la mala opinione. "The ill opinion": male is "ill" (glossary), and "bad" is kept for cattivo (D03), which follows in .04.

il Comune. "The Commune": the commonwealth of Florence as owner of public money.

inconveniente. "Inconvenience" (D133): a harm done.

da ogni parte ne surgeva odio, d'onde si veniva alla divisione, dalla divisione alle Sette, dalle Sette alla rovina. "From every part hatred rose, whence one came to division, from division to sects, from sects to ruin": the chain of I.7 .04 (offence, fear, defence, partisans, parties, ruin), shortened. Setta is "sect", a faction, as in I.7.

non seguivano. "There would not have followed": the imperfect in a contrary-to-fact condition (D47, D130).

o condannati o assoluti. "Whether condemned or acquitted".

si è valuto alcuno cittadino. "Some citizen has availed himself": unnamed. Guicciardini's reply names him.

la parte del Popolo. "The part of the people": its side. The 1554 edition reads del gran popolo and has no relative che before all'appetito (NM-DISC-I8-D006).

se lo fece amico. "He made it his friend": the people.

a campo a Lucca. "In the field before Lucca": besieging it. The war of 1429–1433, in which Florence failed to take Lucca.

messer Giovanni Guicciardini. Florentine commissary with the army before Lucca in 1430, and a member of Guicciardini's own family; messer marks a knight or doctor. The 1554 edition misprints the name and calls him buon commissario (NM-DISC-I8-D007).

ei si volesse mettere. "He wished to put himself": volere is "wish" (D117).

i cattivi suoi governi. "His bad governing": the management of the siege. Governo is "government" (D64); the plural is of acts, not of states, and "his bad governments" would read as regimes (D158).

la espugnazione. "The taking by storm".

corrotto da' Lucchesi. "Corrupted by the Lucchese": bribed.

del Capitano. "The Captain": presumably one of the foreign magistrates with criminal jurisdiction in Florence, such as the Captain of the People. Guicciardini says he went voluntarily to prison.

sdegno. "Indignation", as I.7.

gli uomini grandi. "The great men" (D118).

fare novità. "Make novelty": change the state, as I.7 quale novità.

ne seguì la rovina di quella Repubblica. "The ruin of that republic followed from it": the quarrels that ended in the change of regime of 1433–1434, which Machiavelli writes as that republic's end.

in questo caso appunto. "In this very case": appunto goes with the case.

fargli diventare accusatori. "Make them become accusers": the remedy is to turn the calumniator into an accuser, so that his charge goes to a court.

la non si riscontri vera. "It is not corroborated true": la is the accusation.

come fu punito Manlio. "As Manlius was punished": the text has shown only the prison of .01. In 384 BC, accused before the people by tribunes, he was condemned and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (Livy 6.20), but for aiming at kingship, and Machiavelli does not say which punishment he means. Machiavelli calls him calumniator and not accuser: he made the charge in the crowd, and would not bring it to proof.

FG-CONS.1.8

Considerazione sul capitolo VIII

Quanto le accuse sono utili alle repubbliche, tanto sono perniziose le calunnie

As much as accusations are useful to republics, so much are calumnies pernicious Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply, with lower-case repubbliche and without a full stop.

Italian · 1857
English
1.8.01
[È] vera conclusione che le calunnie sono detestabili, ma tanto naturali in una città libera, che è difficile e forse impossibile il levarle; perché quando nasce uno carico falso contro a uno cittadino, che può nascere per malignità di chi ne è autore e anche per errore, come si può provedere che non si allarghi nella moltitudine, la quale è più inclinata a credere il male che il bene? E anche non mancano molti che per odio o per invidia fomentino questi romori, e però a Roma, nella quale la via dello accusare era sì facile e larga, quanti furono e' carichi dati falsamente a cittadini? In esemplo ci è Fabio Massimo, e molti altri, né si può sempre accusare o punire chi calunnia a torto, né si può altrimenti che per scrittura formare modo di republica che proveda così prontamente a tutti e' disordini. Però in ogni populo libero fu e sarà sempre abondanza di calunniatori, basta, che le calunnie false col tempo e con la verità si spengono spesso per sé stesse. Né lo sdegno di essere calunniato trasporterà mai uno cittadino grave a fare disordine contro alla republica, e se bene arà sdegno contro a chi pensa che sia stato autore della calunnia, ha anche sdegno, e molto maggiore, contro a chi l'ha accusato falsamente. Ma questi sdegni particulari non fanno mai disordine importante in una città che per altro sia bene regolata, come né anche fanno le calunnie, le quali quando sono scandalose, come fu quella di Manlio Capitolino che tendeva a sollevare la plebe contro al senato, si opprimono, se non sono scandalose, si lasciano andare, perché da sé medesime caggiono. E lo esemplo di Cosimo figurato nel Discorso suo, sanza nominarlo, è un sogno, perché a lui aperse la via alla grandezza non le calunnie, ma la prudenza, e principalmente la ricchezza eccessiva, con le quali, essendo il governo di Firenze disordinatissimo e pieno per sua natura di sedizione, gli fu facile corrompere e' cittadini, e fomentando le divisioni della città, camminare, col farsi capo di una parte, alla tirannide. E perché questa materia a provare la conclusione del Discorso è stretta di esempli, fu mendicato quello di messer Giovanni Guicciardini, il quale è vero che fu calunniato ingiustamente, e che per essere e' giudicii disordinati non ebbe modo per mezzo di quegli giustificare la innocenza sua, ancora che ne facessi ogni opera, insino a rapresentarsi volontariamente in carcere, ma dalla calunnia sua non nacque la divisione della città, né da questo si augumentorono, anzi pel contrario le discordie dei cittadini fomentorono e feciono di più momento questo caso suo, che per lo ordinario non sarebbe stato
It is a true conclusion that calumnies are detestable, but so natural in a free city that it is difficult and perhaps impossible to remove them; for when a false charge arises against a citizen, which can arise from the malignity of whoever is its author and also from error, how can one provide that it does not spread in the multitude, which is more inclined to believe the ill than the good? And also there is no lack of many who for hatred or for envy foment these uproars, and therefore at Rome, in which the path of accusing was so easy and broad, how many were the charges given falsely to citizens? For an example there is Fabius Maximus, and many others, nor can one always accuse or punish whoever calumniates wrongly, nor can one, otherwise than in writing, form a way of republic that provides so promptly against all the disorders. Therefore in every free people there was and will always be abundance of calumniators, suffice it, that false calumnies, with time and with the truth, are often extinguished of themselves. Nor will the indignation of being calumniated ever carry a grave citizen away to make disorder against the republic, and although he will have indignation against him who he thinks was the author of the calumny, he has indignation also, and much greater, against him who has accused him falsely. But these particular indignations never make important disorder in a city that is otherwise well regulated, as neither do calumnies, which, when they are scandalous, as was that of Manlius Capitolinus, which tended to raise the plebs against the senate, are oppressed, if they are not scandalous, are let go, for they fall of themselves. And the example of Cosimo, figured in his Discourse without naming him, is a dream, for to him the path to greatness was opened not by calumnies, but by prudence, and principally by excessive wealth, with which, the government of Florence being most disordered and full by its nature of sedition, it was easy for him to corrupt the citizens, and, fomenting the divisions of the city, to walk, by making himself head of a party, to tyranny. And because this matter, for proving the conclusion of the Discourse, is narrow of examples, that of messer Giovanni Guicciardini was begged, of whom it is true that he was calumniated unjustly, and that, through the judgments' being disordered, he had no way by means of them to justify his innocence, even though he made every effort, as far as presenting himself voluntarily in prison, but from his calumny the division of the city did not arise, nor from this were [they] augmented, but rather, on the contrary, the discords of the citizens fomented this case of his and made it of more moment than by the ordinary it would have been
Commentary · 3

Argument. He grants the conclusion in Machiavelli's word — calumnies are detestable — and doubts they can be removed: difficult, and perhaps impossible. They are natural in a free city: a false charge, born of malice or error, spreads in a multitude readier to believe ill than good, and there are always men to foment it; and therefore even Rome, where accusation was easy and open, was full of false charges, as Fabius Maximus found. The calumniator cannot always be punished, and no way of republic, except on paper, provides so promptly against every disorder. Therefore free peoples will always have calumniators. Then he lowers the stakes. False calumnies often die of themselves; a grave citizen calumniated does not turn on the republic, whatever his anger at the author and, more, at whoever accused him falsely; private resentments make no important disorder in a city otherwise well regulated; the scandalous calumnies, like Manlius', are put down, and the rest fall of themselves. Then the two examples. The unnamed citizen is Cosimo de' Medici, and the example is a dream: prudence and, above all, excessive wealth opened his path, with which, the government of Florence being most disordered and by nature full of sedition, he corrupted citizens and, fomenting division, walked to tyranny as head of a party. And Giovanni Guicciardini's case was begged for want of examples: true, he was calumniated unjustly and had no way to clear himself, but the division of the city did not arise from his calumny nor grow by it; the city's discords made his case of more moment than it would have been by the ordinary. He ends without a stop.

History. Fabius Maximus, dictator in 217 BC, was decried at Rome for cowardice, and made suspect of collusion when Hannibal spared his estate; his master of the horse was given equal command (Livy 22.12–26). Cosimo de' Medici, exiled in 1433, returned in 1434 and governed Florence until his death in 1464 through his wealth, his bank and his party; Guicciardini's charge here, corruption and a walk to tyranny, is the conventional one against a rule kept under republican forms. Canestrini's note on Cosimo, which is his and is not carried, glosses Machiavelli's unnamed citizen the same way. Giovanni Guicciardini was commissary at Lucca in 1430; Canestrini's other note says the calumny was favoured by nobili venduti a Cosimo, "nobles sold to Cosimo".

Cross-reference. Detestabili is Machiavelli's Machiavelli .02; his rule was to open many places to accusation, and Guicciardini's answer is that Rome, which had them, had calumnies anyway. Manlius is the case Machiavelli told Machiavelli .01, now the type of the scandalous calumny that is put down. The citizen who used calumnies among other means Machiavelli .03 becomes Cosimo, whose path was opened not by calumnies but by prudence and money. Machiavelli said many examples could be adduced and gave one Machiavelli .04; Guicciardini says the matter is narrow of them, and that one was begged. He grants Giovanni Guicciardini the want of a way to clear himself, which is Machiavelli's diagnosis, affirms the injustice, which Machiavelli left open, and refuses the cause; di più momento turns Machiavelli's phrase for Manlius' tumults (Machiavelli .01) on the case itself. His point that Rome's easy accusation did not stop false charges keeps the caution of his reply to I.7, where the ways of accusing were to be ordered so that the innocent are not readily vexed.

Notes

The paragraph. Canestrini prints one, and it is kept.

[È] vera conclusione. "It is a true conclusion": assent to Machiavelli's detestabili le calunnie (.02), in his own word. The witness prints E without the accent; as printed the period has no main verb, and it is emended as his I.3 and I.7 openings were (D114, D144, D157). The assent is to the conclusion; the reply is against the remedy and the examples.

tanto naturali in una città libera. "So natural in a free city": the 1933 edition reads the singular naturale (FG-CONS-I8-D003).

il levarle. "To remove them": a nominal infinitive in the Italian, which the English does not show. Machiavelli asked for the order "to take them away" (torle via, .02); Guicciardini doubts it can be done.

uno carico falso. "A false charge": carico, a burden laid on a man's name.

malignità. "Malignity" (glossary).

provedere. "Provide": take measures. Proveda returns of the way of republic that "provides so promptly".

il male che il bene. "The ill than the good" (glossary, male).

romori. "Uproars" (D155): public clamour, here stirred up around a false charge.

e però a Roma. "And therefore at Rome" (D22). The consequence is in the argument, not the history: many foment false charges, and so even Rome, with its easy path of accusing, had many.

la via dello accusare. "The path of accusing" (D131): Machiavelli's open places for accusation (.02) were Rome's, and did not stop calumny there.

e carichi. "The charges": the plural article (D35).

Fabio Massimo. Quintus Fabius Maximus, the Delayer, whose caution against Hannibal was decried at Rome as cowardice and worse; Hannibal spared his estate to make him suspect, and his command was split with his master of the horse in 217 BC (Livy 22.12–26).

a torto. "Wrongly".

altrimenti che per scrittura. "Otherwise than in writing": only on paper can a republic be framed to provide against every disorder.

modo di republica. "Way of republic" (glossary, modo).

Però. "Therefore" (D22), again: the second consequence, that free peoples always have calumniators.

basta, che. "Suffice it, that": it is enough. Canestrini's commas are kept; the 1933 edition has calunniatori; basta che.

si spengono ... per sé stesse. "Are extinguished of themselves" (as spegnere, I.7). The calumnies "fall of themselves" too (da sé medesime caggiono).

trasporterà. "Will carry ... away": as a passion does. The 1933 edition spells it traporterá (FG-CONS-I8-D004).

uno cittadino grave. "A grave citizen": of weight and judgment.

sdegno, sdegni particulari. "Indignation", "particular indignations" (particulare, D93): private resentments, as against Machiavelli's "indignation" of Giovanni's friends, which he made a public cause.

contro a chi l'ha accusato falsamente. "Against him who has accused him falsely": two objects of indignation, the presumed author of the calumny and, much more, whoever accused him falsely. The word is accusato, not calunniato; whether the false accuser is the calumniator, or a formal accuser under Machiavelli's remedy, the Italian does not say, and the English does not either.

regolata. "Regulated": ordered.

come né anche fanno le calunnie. "As neither do calumnies". The 1933 edition prints neanche (FG-CONS-I8-D005).

si opprimono. "Are oppressed": put down (glossary, opprimere). Manlius is Machiavelli's example and Guicciardini's: the scandalous calumny, put down when the dictator imprisoned Manlius.

si lasciano andare. "Are let go": the sentence runs on by commas, without a conjunction, as the Italian does.

lo esemplo di Cosimo figurato nel Discorso suo, sanza nominarlo. "The example of Cosimo, figured in his Discourse without naming him": Machiavelli's "some citizen" who rose by calumnies against the powerful (.03). Guicciardini names him: Cosimo de' Medici. Canestrini's note, which is his and is not carried, says the same.

è un sogno. "Is a dream": the 1933 edition prints uno sogno.

a lui aperse la via alla grandezza non le calunnie, ma la prudenza. "To him the path to greatness was opened not by calumnies, but by prudence": the subjects come last in the Italian, and the passive keeps them last (D18).

con le quali. "With which": the plural takes both prudence and wealth.

prudenza. "Prudence" (glossary): practical judgment, not caution.

disordinatissimo. "Most disordered", in the ordine family.

corrompere e' cittadini. "To corrupt the citizens": Machiavelli's corrotto (.03, .04), the charge in the calumnies, is here what Cosimo did.

camminare, col farsi capo di una parte, alla tirannide. "To walk, by making himself head of a party, to tyranny": the Italian puts the means between the verb and its goal, and the English keeps it there. Capo as in I.12 (glossary).

è stretta di esempli. "Is narrow of examples": short of them. The purpose phrase stays between subject and predicate, as in the Italian. Rome's path of accusing was "broad" (larga); Machiavelli's matter is narrow.

fu mendicato. "Was begged": sought for want of better. Giovanni Guicciardini was of his own family.

il quale è vero che ..., e che. "Of whom it is true that ..., and that": one concession governing two facts. He grants Machiavelli both, the unjust calumny and the want of a way to clear himself, which is Machiavelli's diagnosis (per non essere modi in quella Repubblica, .04).

per essere e' giudicii disordinati. "Through the judgments' being disordered": the courts (glossary, giudicii). The 1933 edition reads giudici, judges (FG-CONS-I8-D006).

insino a rapresentarsi volontariamente in carcere. "As far as presenting himself voluntarily in prison": where Machiavelli has him willing to put himself in the Captain's hands.

né da questo si augumentorono. "Nor from this were [they] augmented": da questo repeats dalla calunnia sua, "from his calumny". The verb is plural and has no subject until the next clause names the discords; the English supplies "[they]" (D53). Augumentare, "augment" (D69).

anzi pel contrario. "But rather, on the contrary": anzi corrects the negative (D90), and pel contrario reverses.

fomentorono e feciono di più momento questo caso suo. "Fomented this case of his and made it of more moment": both verbs take questo caso suo. The e prints a-shaped in Canestrini; read e (D156). Fomentare is the verb of the many who "foment these uproars" and of Cosimo "fomenting the divisions". Di momento is Machiavelli's phrase for Manlius' tumults (.01). The causation runs the other way from Machiavelli's: the discords made the case, not the case the discords.

per lo ordinario. "By the ordinary", as Machiavelli's per l'ordinario in I.7 (D151). It can mean "in the ordinary course", as things would have gone; it can also mean by the ordinary way of the orders, which would make Guicciardini answer Machiavelli's argument in Machiavelli's phrase. The English keeps the phrase so that both stay open.

No full stop. Canestrini prints none after stato; the 1933 edition does. The English ends as the Italian does (D40).

What this is

A free, openly licensed, bilingual edition of Guicciardini's 39 Considerazioni and the Discorsi chapters they answer — 78 text units, Italian and English facing, every reading traceable to a named public-domain witness.

It is a reading edition with a transparent apparatus, not a text of record. It does not adjudicate the critical text against Bausi 2001 or the manuscript tradition, and does not claim to.

On the title

Machiavelli had been dead two years when Guicciardini wrote; the Discorsi were unpublished, and the Considerazioni stayed unprinted until 1857. Machiavelli never answers.

“Conversation” is a quotation, not a description. It invokes Machiavelli's letter to Vettori of 10 December 1513, where he changes into court dress at evening, enters the antique courts of ancient men, and reports that they, in their humanity, answer me. Guicciardini does to Machiavelli what Machiavelli did to Livy — and is denied the reply Machiavelli claimed.

Method

No image-derived reading enters the text on a single reading. Each unit is transcribed twice, independently, by readers who have not seen each other's work; disagreements are adjudicated against the page images and recorded.

Existing English translations of the Considerazioni — Grayson (1965), Atkinson & Sices (2002) — are never used as translation inputs.
