A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 33 of 39

Men deceive themselves many times, believing with humility to vanquish pride Discourses II.14, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that humility often harms; therefore a prince with one enemy must never yield a thing by agreement unless he can, or believes he can, hold it, since yielding from fear usually does not avert the war, and should arm at once even when weaker. Guicciardini finds this in part contrary to Machiavelli's own counsel to temporize in dangers, and therefore distinguishes. With forces not equal to the enemy's, it is better to come to terms, even giving something up, than to court ruin at once, since time can bring events that meet the danger. When they are equal or almost equal, even though war is dangerous and difficult, the first loss of reputation matters so much that one should be loath to yield, as Thucydides' Pericles argues.

Capitolo XIV

Discorsi II.14

Ingannansi molte volte gli uomini, credendo con la umiltà vincere la superbia.

Men deceive themselves many times, believing with humility to vanquish pride.

Italian · 1824
English

One sees many times how humility not only does not help but does harm, most of all using it with insolent men, who either from envy or from another cause have conceived hatred toward you. Of which our Historian bears witness in this cause of war between the Romans and the Latins. Because, the Samnites complaining to the Romans that the Latins had assailed them, the Romans did not wish to prohibit the Latins such a war, desiring not to irritate them; which not only did not irritate them, but made them become more spirited against them, and they uncovered themselves sooner as enemies. Of which the words used by the aforesaid Annius, Latin praetor, in the same council bear witness, where he says: Tentastis patientiam negando militem: quis dubitat exarsisse eos? Pertulerunt tamen hunc dolorem. Exercitus nos parare adversus Samnites foederatos suos audierunt, nec moverunt se ab urbe. Unde haec illis tanta modestia, nisi a conscientia virium, et nostrarum, et suarum? One knows, on that account, most clearly by this text how much the patience of the Romans increased the arrogance of the Latins.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The chapter opens on what "one sees many times": humility not only does not help but does harm, most of all when it is used with insolent men who, from envy or another cause, have conceived hatred toward you. The claim is general, and "most of all" marks where it holds most strongly. Livy is the witness. When the Samnites complained that the Latins had assailed them, the Romans did not wish to prohibit the war, desiring not to irritate the Latins; the forbearance did not irritate them but made them more spirited against Rome, and they uncovered themselves sooner as enemies. The words of Annius, the Latin praetor, in the Latins' council are the second witness: the Romans bore the refusal of soldiers and did not move when the Latins armed against the Samnites, and Annius asks whence such modesty, if not from a consciousness of strength, both ours and theirs. From this text, Machiavelli concludes, one knows how much the patience of the Romans increased the arrogance of the Latins. The rubric's humility and pride are here patience and arrogance.

History. Livy tells of the Samnites' complaint at Rome in 341 BC against the Latins and Campanians who were making war on them, and of the senate's answer that nothing in the treaty forbade the Latins to make war on whom they wished (8.1-2); the motive, a desire not to irritate them, is as Machiavelli gives it. Annius of Setia's speech to the Latin council, from which Machiavelli quotes, is 8.4; the Latin War that followed ran from 340 to 338 BC (the conventional dates). In Livy, Annius puts the Roman restraint down to awareness of strength, both sides'; Machiavelli takes the speaker's reading as evidence of what the restraint produced. The chapter continues II.13, where the same war is caused by the Latins' perceiving Rome's deceit; here it is given a second cause, Rome's patience. The references, the dates and these readings are the editor's.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not mention the Livy case. He takes up the word "insolent" Guicciardini .01, but where Machiavelli's men are insolent before anyone yields, Guicciardini's enemy is made insolent by the yielding.

2.14.0212 notes1 variant
E però mai un Principe debbe volere mancare del grado suo, e non debbe mai lasciare alcuna cosa d'accordo, volendola lasciare onorevolmente, se non quando e' la può, o e' si crede che la possa tenere; perché egli è meglio quasi sempre (sendosi condotta la cosa in termine che tu non la possa lasciare nel modo detto) lasciarsela torre con le forze, che con la paura delle forze; perché se tu la lasci con la paura, lo fai per levarti la guerra, e il più delle volte non te la lievi; perché colui a chi tu arai con una viltà scoperta concesso quella, non starà saldo, ma ti vorrà torre delle altre cose, e si accenderà più contro di te stimandoti meno; e dall'altra parte in tuo favore troverai i difensori più freddi, parendo loro che tu sia o debole o vile.
And therefore a prince must never wish to lack his degree, and must never leave any thing by accord, wishing to leave it honourably, except when he can [hold] it, or he believes that he can hold it; because it is better almost always (the thing having been conducted to [such a] term that you cannot leave it in the said way) to let it be taken away from you with forces than with the fear of forces; because if you leave it with the fear, you do it to remove the war from yourself, and most times you do not remove it from yourself; because he to whom you will have conceded it with an uncovered baseness will not stand firm, but will wish to take away other things from you, and will be kindled more against you, esteeming you less; and on the other hand, in your favour, you will find the defenders colder, it seeming to them that you are either weak or base.
Commentary · 2

Argument. "And therefore" the counsel: a prince must never wish to lack his degree, and must never leave a thing by accord, if he would leave it honourably, except when he can hold it or believes he can. The condition governs the period. The first "because" gives the ground: where the thing has been conducted to such a term that it cannot be left that way, it is better almost always to let it be taken away with forces than with the fear of forces. The next two "because" form a chain, each explaining the one before: he who leaves a thing with the fear does so to remove the war from himself, and most times does not remove it, because the one to whom he has conceded it with an uncovered baseness will not stand firm, but will wish to take other things, and will be kindled more against him, esteeming him less. "On the other hand" adds a further cost: the defenders in his favour will be colder, thinking him weak or base. "Almost always" and "most times" are Machiavelli's own qualifications, and they stay with the counsel.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini answers this counsel. He reads it as contrary in part to what Machiavelli said in another place, and distinguishes by the measure of forces: where yours are not even with the enemy's, it is better to come to an accord, even leaving something; where they are even or almost even, the beginning of a loss of reputation matters so much that unwillingly one must yield Guicciardini .01. "Reputation" is his word for what Machiavelli calls degree and esteem; "base" is this paragraph's word.

2.14.034 notes
Ma se tu subito scoperta la voglia dello avversario prepari le forze, ancora che elle siano inferiori a lui, quello ti comincia a stimare; stimanti più gli altri Principi allo intorno; e a tale viene voglia di ajutarti, sendo in su l'arme, che abbandonandoti non ti ajuterebbe mai.
But if you at once, the longing of the adversary uncovered, prepare the forces, even though they are inferior to him, he begins to esteem you; esteem you more [do] the other princes round about; and to such a one comes the longing to aid you, [you] being in arms, who, [you] abandoning yourself, would never aid you.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The other course. If, at once, the adversary's longing uncovered, you prepare the forces, even inferior ones, the adversary begins to esteem you, the other princes round about esteem you more, and to such a one comes the longing to aid you, you being in arms, who would never aid you had you abandoned yourself (the gerund could also be read of the helper abandoning you, which the sense does not favour). Arming even with inferior forces makes you esteemed and brings aid; Machiavelli does not say that the inferior forces win.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's first case is forces "not even" with the enemy's; there he holds that it is better to come to an accord, since time can bring accidents enough to provide against your future danger Guicciardini .01.

2.14.046 notes1 variant
Questo s'intende quando tu abbia un inimico; ma quando ne avessi più, rendere delle cose, che tu possedessi, ad alcuno di loro per riguadagnarselo, ancora che fusse già scoperta la guerra, e per smembrarlo dagli altri confederati tuoi inimici, fia sempre partito prudente.
This is understood when you have one enemy; but when you had more, to render some of the things that you possessed to one of them to regain him [for yourself], even though the war were already uncovered, and to dismember him from the other confederates your enemies, will always be a prudent course.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The last period limits the counsel of the two before: "this is understood when you have one enemy". With more than one, to render some of the things you possessed to one of them, to regain him and dismember him from the other confederates your enemies, even after the war is already uncovered, will always be a prudent course. The counsel against leaving a thing by accord is thus confined to the single enemy, and the chapter ends by admitting a cession, made for a purpose, against several.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not take up this limit; his distinction is drawn on the measure of forces, not on the number of enemies Guicciardini .01.

Notes

¶ 01 Vedesi molte volte come la umiltà non solamente non giova ma nuoce. "One sees many times how humility not only does not help but does harm": giovare, help (D334); nuocere, do harm (D106). come, how, introducing what is seen.

¶ 01 massimamente usandola con gli uomini insolenti. "Most of all using it with insolent men": massimamente, most of all; the gerund is left without a subject, as in the Italian; insolente, insolent, with insolenza (glossary), the word Guicciardini takes up of the enemy (insolente lo inimico, Guicciardini .01).

¶ 01 hanno concetto odio teco. "Have conceived hatred toward you": concepire, conceive; teco, with you, rendered "toward you". The ti of the chapter's advice begins here, before any prince is named.

¶ 01 Di che ne fa fede lo Istorico nostro. "Of which our Historian bears witness": fare fede, bear witness (D464), here and at Di che ne fanno fede below; Istorico, Historian, Livy, capitalised as printed. The episode is Livy 8.1-2 (the editor's reference): in 341 BC the Samnites complained at Rome that the Latins and Campanians were making war on them, and the senate answered, of the Latins, that nothing in the treaty forbade them to make war on whom they wished. The motive, desiderando non gli irritare, is as Machiavelli gives it, not a word of Livy's that the note reports.

¶ 01 in questa cagione di guerra tra i Romani e i Latini. "In this cause of war between the Romans and the Latins": the war of II.13, whose cause there is the Latins' recognition of the Roman deceit (Machiavelli II.13); here the same war is given a second cause, Roman humility. cagione, cause (glossary).

¶ 01 dolendosi i Sanniti con i Romani. "The Samnites complaining to the Romans": dolersi, complain; the gerund's subject stated, as printed.

¶ 01 i Romani non vollono proibire ai Latini tal guerra, desiderando non gli irritare. "The Romans did not wish to prohibit the Latins such a war, desiring not to irritate them": volere, wish (D117); proibire, prohibit (glossary); desiderare, desire (glossary); irritare, irritate.

¶ 01 ma li fece diventare più animosi contro a loro. "But made them become more spirited against them": animoso, spirited, with animosamente, spiritedly (glossary); the first "them" is the Latins, the second the Romans, as in the Italian li and loro.

¶ 01 e si scopersono più presto inimici. "And they uncovered themselves sooner as enemies": scoprirsi, uncover oneself, of scoprire, uncover (D452), the root of the chapter's scoperta (.02, .03, .04); più presto without che is temporal here, "sooner", and D135's "rather ... than" stays for the comparison; inimico, enemy (D334).

¶ 01 le parole usate dal prefato Annio Pretore Latino nel medesimo Concilio. "The words used by the aforesaid Annius, Latin praetor, in the same council": prefato, aforesaid: Annius Setinus, named in Machiavelli II.13 (names.md); Concilio, council, here the same body as II.13's Consiglio (D452), a collapse now recorded (D464). The 1554 reads Pretorio (NM-DISC-II14-D001, D459).

¶ 01 Tentastis patientiam ... et nostrarum, et suarum?. Livy 8.4.6-7, as Machiavelli quotes it, left in Latin as he leaves it: "You tried [their] patience by refusing soldiers: who doubts they were inflamed? Yet they bore this pain. They heard that we were preparing armies against the Samnites, their confederates, and did not move from the city. Whence this so great modesty in them, if not from a consciousness of strength, both ours and theirs?" (the editor's rendering). In the 1554 the quotation lacks Livy's a before conscientia and is followed by an Italian rendering, which the 1824 does not print (NM-DISC-II14-D002, D459). patientia is the chapter's pazienza; modestia, the Romans' restraint, Annius puts down to awareness of strength (conscientia virium), the Latins' as well as their own. Tentastis as the 1824 prints it.

¶ 01 Conoscesi pertanto chiarissimo per questo testo. "One knows, on that account, most clearly by this text": conoscere, know (D374); pertanto, on that account (D98), kept apart from però, therefore, which opens the next period; chiarissimo, most clearly, the superlative kept; testo, text, the passage of Livy.

¶ 01 quanto la pazienza dei Romani accrebbe l'arroganza de' Latini. "How much the patience of the Romans increased the arrogance of the Latins": pazienza, patience; accrescere, increase (D69); arroganza, arrogance. The rubric's humility and pride, in the case.

¶ 02 E però mai un Principe debbe volere mancare del grado suo. "And therefore a prince must never wish to lack his degree": però, therefore (D22); dovere, must (D274), here and in the next member; mancare di with a noun, lack (D312), here in the sense of falling short of, or letting go, the rank he holds; grado, degree (glossary), as II.13's gradi grandi. The 1554 doubles the negative, mai ... non debbe, with the same sense (NM-DISC-II14-D003, D459).

¶ 02 e non debbe mai lasciare alcuna cosa d'accordo, volendola lasciare onorevolmente. "And must never leave any thing by accord, wishing to leave it honourably": lasciare, leave, the chapter's verb of giving up, held through .02; d'accordo, by accord, by agreement rather than by force; accordo, accord (D452); onorevolmente, honourably. The gerund states the condition: to leave a thing honourably, leave it by accord only when the case below holds.

¶ 02 se non quando e' la può, o e' si crede che la possa tenere. "Except when he can [hold] it, or he believes that he can hold it": the first la può leaves tenere to the second member, and the English supplies it in brackets (D53); tenere, hold (D242). The construction is compressed; the sense is that the prince should leave a thing by accord only while he still could hold it, or thinks he could. e' si crede may also be impersonal, e' for egli as in egli è meglio just after: "or it is believed that he can hold it", the belief of others, whose esteem the chapter goes on to weigh; the English keeps the personal reading and records the other.

¶ 02 perché egli è meglio quasi sempre. "Because it is better almost always": egli the impersonal subject, not rendered.

¶ 02 (sendosi condotta la cosa in termine che tu non la possa lasciare nel modo detto). "(The thing having been conducted to [such a] term that you cannot leave it in the said way)": sendosi condotta, a perfect gerund, the point already reached; condursi, of a thing, be conducted (D444, D452, D464); termine, term, a point reached: a third sense beside D325's termini, bearings, of conduct, and D401's terms of a comparison (D464); nel modo detto, in the said way, honourably, by accord. The parenthesis is printed and kept.

¶ 02 lasciarsela torre con le forze, che con la paura delle forze. "To let it be taken away from you with forces than with the fear of forces": -se-, from you, the chapter's person; torre, take away (D131); forze, forces (glossary); paura, fear (D313); con la paura, "with the fear", in the next member, points back to it. The 1554 omits the article before paura (NM-DISC-II14-D003).

¶ 02 lo fai per levarti la guerra, e il più delle volte non te la lievi. "You do it to remove the war from yourself, and most times you do not remove it from yourself": levarsi with an object, remove from oneself (D373), held in both members; il più delle volte, most times.

¶ 02 colui a chi tu arai con una viltà scoperta concesso quella. "He to whom you will have conceded it with an uncovered baseness": viltà, baseness (D444); scoperto, uncovered, the participle of scoprire (D452), the baseness shown openly; concedere, concede; quella, it, the thing (la cosa). The 1554 reads quello (NM-DISC-II14-D003, D459).

¶ 02 non starà saldo, ma ti vorrà torre delle altre cose. "Will not stand firm, but will wish to take away other things from you": saldo, firm; delle altre cose, other things, partitive.

¶ 02 e si accenderà più contro di te stimandoti meno. "And will be kindled more against you, esteeming you less": accendersi, be kindled, as Livy's exarsisse; stimare, esteem (D452), held at .03.

¶ 02 e dall'altra parte in tuo favore troverai i difensori più freddi. "And on the other hand, in your favour, you will find the defenders colder": dall'altra parte, on the other hand (D24); the fronted in tuo favore is kept where it stands, so that it may go with the defenders or with their coldness; difensore, defender; freddo, cold.

¶ 02 parendo loro che tu sia o debole o vile. "It seeming to them that you are either weak or base": parere, seem; debole, weak; vile, base, the adjective of viltà (D444, D464), the word Guicciardini takes up (a fare vile te, Guicciardini .01).

¶ 03 Ma se tu subito scoperta la voglia dello avversario prepari le forze. "But if you at once, the longing of the adversary uncovered, prepare the forces": subito, at once (D177), placed between tu and the absolute participle, so that it may go with either, and the English keeps it there; le forze, the forces, your own. voglia, longing (D117); avversario, adversary (D334), here, beside inimico, enemy (.04); scoperta, uncovered, as above.

¶ 03 ancora che elle siano inferiori a lui. "Even though they are inferior to him": ancora che, even though (D31 extended); inferiore, inferior. elle, the forces.

¶ 03 quello ti comincia a stimare; stimanti più gli altri Principi allo intorno. "He begins to esteem you; esteem you more [do] the other princes round about": the second member fronts its verb to answer the first (stimare, stimanti), a contrast in which the order does the work, so it is kept (D263), the auxiliary supplied (D53); allo intorno, round about, as II.13's all'intorno.

¶ 03 e a tale viene voglia di ajutarti, sendo in su l'arme, che abbandonandoti non ti ajuterebbe mai. "And to such a one comes the longing to aid you, [you] being in arms, who, [you] abandoning yourself, would never aid you": aiutare, aid (D348); voglia, longing, as above; in su l'arme, in arms, under arms. The two gerunds, sendo and abbandonandoti, have the same unexpressed subject, you, set against each other: armed, you are aided; given up, you would never be. English needs the subject supplied (D53). abbandonandoti could also be read "abandoning you", of the helper, which the sense of the period does not favour.

¶ 04 Questo s'intende quando tu abbia un inimico. "This is understood when you have one enemy": intendere, understand (D374); un, one, the number that the next member sets against più.

¶ 04 ma quando ne avessi più. "But when you had more": avessi may be second or, in Florentine, third person; the second is taken from tu possedessi in the next clause, and the bare subjunctive is not given "should" (D348). The 1554 reads haveſſe (NM-DISC-II14-D004, D459).

¶ 04 rendere delle cose, che tu possedessi, ad alcuno di loro per riguadagnarselo. "To render some of the things that you possessed to one of them to regain him [for yourself]": rendere, render, give back (D464); possedere, possess; riguadagnare, regain, of guadagnare, gain (glossary); se, for yourself, bracketed because the English needs the person. The 1554 reads riſguardarſelo, which barely construes (NM-DISC-II14-D004, D459).

¶ 04 ancora che fusse già scoperta la guerra. "Even though the war were already uncovered": the last of the chapter's four uses of scoprire; a war uncovered is a war declared, in the open. The 1554 adds di (NM-DISC-II14-D004).

¶ 04 e per smembrarlo dagli altri confederati tuoi inimici. "And to dismember him from the other confederates your enemies": smembrare, dismember, detach a member from the body of the league; confederato, confederate (D464), with Guicciardini's confederazione at II.13 (D452) and Livy's foederatos. Without a comma, as printed.

¶ 04 fia sempre partito prudente. "Will always be a prudent course": partito, course (D236); prudente, prudent (glossary); fia, will be, the future. The 1554 reads ſia, be, with the long s for the f (NM-DISC-II14-D004, D459). The chapter ends on the counsel for many enemies, which the Italian sets after the counsel for one; neither is subordinated to the other.

Considerazione sul capitolo XIV

Considerazioni II.14

Ingannansi molte volte gli uomini, credendo con la umiltà vincere la superbia

Men deceive themselves many times, believing with humility to vanquish pride

Italian · 1857
English
2.14.0114 notes3 variants
La conclusione del Discorso è in parte contraria a quello che lui disse in altro luogo, che è più prudenza temporeggiarsi ne' casi periculosi che urtare, e però bisogna distinguere che quando le forze tue non sono pari a quell[e] dello inimico, meglio sia accordare, etiam lasciando qualche cosa, che tirarsi subito addosso la ruina; perché il tempo può portare degli accidenti che bastino a provedere al tuo futuro periculo. Ma quando tu hai forze pari o quasi pari allo inimico, ancora che lo entrare in guerra sia con periculo e con difficultà, importa tanto il cominciare a tòrti la riputazione, a fare vile te, insolente lo inimico, che mal volentieri si debbe cedere. La quale ragione largamente discorre Tucidide nella persona di Pericle, quando consigliò agli Ateniesi più presto il pigliare la guerra co' Lacedemonii, benché difficile e periculosa, che accettare le condizioni proposte da loro, ancora che per sé stesse le paressino di poco momento.
The conclusion of the Discourse is in part contrary to that which he said in another place, that it is more prudence to temporize in dangerous cases than to strike, and therefore it is required to distinguish that, when your forces are not even with those of the enemy, it is better to come to [an] accord, even leaving something, than to pull ruin upon yourself at once; because time can bring accidents that are enough to provide against your future danger. But when you have forces even or almost even with the enemy, even though entering into war is with danger and with difficulty, the beginning to take away your reputation from you, to make you base, the enemy insolent, matters so much that unwillingly one must yield. Which reason Thucydides discourses at large in the person of Pericles, when he counselled the Athenians rather to take the war with the Lacedaemonians, albeit difficult and dangerous, than to accept the conditions proposed by them, even though in themselves they seemed of little moment.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The reply is one paragraph and makes one move: a distinction. Guicciardini first finds the conclusion of the Discourse "in part" contrary to what Machiavelli said in another place, that it is more prudence to temporize in dangerous cases than to strike; "and therefore", because of that partial contradiction, it is required to distinguish. When your forces are not even with those of the enemy, it is better to come to an accord, even leaving something, than to pull ruin upon yourself at once, because time can bring accidents enough to provide against your future danger. When they are even or almost even, then, even though entering into war is with danger and with difficulty, the beginning to take away your reputation from you, to make you base, the enemy insolent, matters so much that unwillingly one must yield: one must be loath to do it. That reason, he says, Thucydides discourses at large in the person of Pericles, who counselled the Athenians rather to take the war with the Lacedaemonians, albeit difficult and dangerous, than to accept their conditions, even though these seemed in themselves of little moment. The test is Guicciardini's own, the measure of forces, not Machiavelli's, whether the thing can be held. In the first case he answers Machiavelli's counsel to arm even with inferior forces the opposite way; in the second he keeps Machiavelli's reasons, base and insolent, and puts "unwillingly" where Machiavelli says "never".

History. The "other place" is Discourses I.33, whose rubric holds that when an inconvenience has grown in or against a state it is a more salutary course to temporize with it than to strike at it; Canestrini's footnote names the chapter, to which the Considerazioni have no reply. The speech is Pericles' first in Thucydides (1.140-144), urging the Athenians not to yield to the Spartan demands, among them the repeal of the Megarian decree; Pericles tells them not to think they would be going to war for a small thing, since a concession would be taken for fear and bring greater demands. The identifications are the editor's, and so is the observation that Guicciardini's "dangerous cases" is wider than I.33's grown inconvenience.

Cross-reference. The contradiction Guicciardini finds is with Machiavelli's counsel never to leave a thing by accord except when it can be held Machiavelli .02. His first case, forces not even with the enemy's, meets Machiavelli's arming with inferior forces Machiavelli .03, and answers it otherwise: where Machiavelli arms "even though" the forces are inferior, Guicciardini would come to an accord, since time can bring accidents. In the second case he takes Machiavelli's words: base Machiavelli .02 and insolent Machiavelli .01, though for Machiavelli the insolent hate before anyone yields, and for Guicciardini the enemy is made insolent by the yielding; "reputation" is his own word for what Machiavelli calls degree and esteem. Machiavelli's limit to one enemy Machiavelli .04 is not taken up.
