A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 31 of 39

Whether it is better, fearing to be assailed, to bring, or to await, war Discourses II.12, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli argues that a prince with armed peoples should await a powerful war at home, and one with disarmed subjects keep it away, because his virtù lies in money, which war at home impedes most. Guicciardini finds the case too doubtful to resolve easily: Machiavelli's distinction, armed or disarmed subjects, is not enough; the subjects' faith, the towns' strength, revenues and the enemy's condition also matter. He judges that whoever has war at home should, given the chance, begin it in the enemy's country too; against a stronger enemy, he says, one cannot think of attacking abroad, and with even means and the forces needed he would be inclined to counsel not awaiting war at home. He is not moved by the Roman counterfactual, which he calls an impossible case.

Whether it is better, fearing to be assailed, to bring, or to await, war.

Italian · 1824
English
2.12.012 notes1 variant
Io ho sentito da uomini assai pratichi nelle cose della guerra qualche volta disputare, se sono duoi Principi quasi di eguali forze, se quello più gagliardo abbia bandito la guerra contro a quell'altro, quale sia miglior partito per l'altro, o aspettare il nimico dentro ai confini suoi, o andarlo a trovare in casa, ed assaltare lui. I have heard men very practised in the things of war sometimes dispute, if there are two princes of almost equal forces, if that harder [one] has proclaimed war against that other, which is the better course for the other: either to await the enemy within his borders, or to go to find him at home and assail him. And I have heard reasons adduced for it on every side.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The chapter opens on a question Machiavelli has heard disputed by men practised in war, not on a thesis of his own. Two princes of almost equal forces; the harder has proclaimed war on the other; which course is better for the one attacked, to await the enemy within his borders or to go and assail him at home? He has heard reasons on every side, and the next three paragraphs report them before he says what he understands.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini begins from this report: the Discourse has many examples for both opinions, and there are also many reasons that make the case so doubtful that it is not easily resolved Guicciardini .01.

And whoever defends going to assail others cites for it the counsel that Croesus gave to Cyrus, when, [Cyrus] having arrived on the borders of the Massagetae to make war on them, their queen Tomyris sent to tell him that he choose which of the two courses he wished: either to enter her kingdom, where she would await him, or wished that she come to find him. And the thing having come into disputation, Croesus, against the opinion of the others, said that they go to find her; citing that if he vanquished her at a distance from her kingdom, he would not take the kingdom away from her, because she would have time to remake herself; but if he vanquished her within her borders, he could follow her in her flight, and, not giving her space to remake herself, take the state away from her. They cite also the counsel that Hannibal gave to Antiochus, when that king designed to make war on the Romans, where he showed how the Romans could not be vanquished except in Italy, because there another could avail himself of their arms and riches and friends; but whoever fought them outside Italy, and left them Italy free, left them that spring to which life never fails for supplying forces where it is required, and he concluded that from the Romans Rome could be taken away earlier than the empire, and Italy earlier than the other provinces. He cites also Agathocles, who, not being able to sustain the war at home, assailed the Carthaginians, who were making it on him, and brought them to ask for peace. He cites Scipio, who, to remove the war from Italy, assailed Africa.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The case for carrying the war abroad is made by authorities, each cited by those who hold it. Croesus counsels Cyrus to go and find Tomyris, because a victory at a distance from her kingdom would leave her time to remake herself, while a victory within her borders would let him follow her in flight and take her state from her. Hannibal tells Antiochus that the Romans cannot be vanquished except in Italy, because there another can avail himself of their arms, riches and friends; whoever fights them outside Italy and leaves them Italy free leaves them a spring that never fails to supply forces, and Rome could be taken from the Romans earlier than the empire, and Italy earlier than the other provinces. Agathocles, unable to sustain the war at home, assails the Carthaginians and brings them to ask for peace; Scipio assails Africa to remove the war from Italy. These are cited by those who defend assailing, not judged by Machiavelli.

History. The Croesus story is Herodotus' (1.206-214): Cyrus took the counsel, crossed into the Massagetae's country and was killed there. Machiavelli tells only the counsel. Hannibal's counsel to Antiochus III is in Livy (34.60). Agathocles of Syracuse invaded Africa in 310 BC while Syracuse was besieged; Scipio Africanus crossed to Africa in 204 BC, and Hannibal was recalled from Italy in 203 BC. The identifications are the editor's.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini sets Scipio and Agathocles under a different case, the man who already has the war at home and begins it in the enemy's country as well to constrain him to depart Guicciardini .01.

Argument. The contrary case: whoever wishes an enemy to come to a bad end, let him distance him from home. They cite the Athenians, who remained superior while they made war convenient to their home, and lost their liberty when they went with their armies into Sicily. They cite the poetic fables: Antaeus, king of Libya, was insuperable while he awaited Hercules within the borders of his kingdom, and lost his state and his life when he went to a distance from it through Hercules' craft; from this, the party says, came the fable of Antaeus, son of the Earth, lifted off the ground by Hercules. They cite modern judgments too. Ferdinand of Naples was held a most wise prince; when the report came, two years before his death, that Charles VIII of France wished to assail him, he made many preparations, fell ill, and among his last counsels told his son Alfonso to await the enemy within the kingdom, for nothing in the world to draw forces out of his state, and to await him within his borders all entire. Alfonso did not observe it, sent an army into Romagna, and without fighting lost the army and the state. The counsel is reported as Ferdinand's, and the loss is set beside its neglect; the whole is cited by those who argue for awaiting.

History. The Athenian expedition to Sicily ended in disaster in 413 BC. Ferdinand I of Naples (Ferrante) died in January 1494; Charles VIII invaded later that year, and Alfonso II sent forces into Romagna under his son Ferrandino before abdicating early in 1495. The identifications and dates are the editor's, and the sequence is Machiavelli's.

The reasons which, beyond the things said, are adduced on every side are: that whoever assails comes with greater spirit than whoever awaits, which makes the army more confident; he takes away, beyond this, many conveniences from the enemy for being able to avail himself of his things, he not being able to avail himself of those subjects who are sacked; and, through having the enemy at home, the lord is constrained to have more respect to drawing money from them and wearying them, so that he comes to dry up that spring, as Hannibal says, which makes it that that man can sustain the war. Beyond this, his soldiers, through finding themselves in others' countries, are more necessitated to fight, and that necessity makes virtù, as we have said more times. On the other hand it is said that in awaiting the enemy one awaits with much advantage, because without any hardship [to yourself] you can give him many hardships of victuals, and of every other thing that an army has need of; you can better impede his designs, through the notice of the country that you have more than he: you can meet him with more forces, through being able readily to unite them all, but not being able to distance them all from home; you can, being routed, remake yourself readily, both because much of your army will be saved, through having refuges near, and because the supplement does not have to come from a distance; so that you come to hazard all the forces, and not all the fortune, and, going to a distance, you hazard all the fortune, and not all the forces. And there have been some who, to weaken their enemy better, let him enter several days' [march] into their country, and take many towns, in order that, leaving garrisons in all of them, he may weaken his army, and they may afterwards fight him more readily.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The reasons, beyond the authorities, set side by side. For assailing: the assailant comes with greater spirit; the defender cannot avail himself of subjects who are sacked, and the lord, with the enemy at home, is constrained to have more respect in drawing money from them and wearying them, so that he comes to dry up the spring Hannibal spoke of; the assailant's soldiers, in others' countries, are more necessitated to fight, and necessity makes virtù. For awaiting: without any hardship to yourself you can give him many hardships of victuals and of everything an army needs, impede his designs through knowledge of the country, meet him with more forces because you can readily unite them all, and, if routed, remake yourself from near refuges and a supplement that does not have to come from a distance. What is said for awaiting closes on a chiasmus whose second member holds only under its condition: so that you come to hazard all the forces and not all the fortune; going to a distance, you hazard all the fortune and not all the forces. Some have let an enemy enter several days' march into their country and take many towns, so that, leaving garrisons in all of them, he weakens his army, and they may afterwards fight him more readily.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini gives reasons of his own, under a conditional, for carrying the war abroad when forces are even: greater reward in victory, less loss in defeat and more time to remake oneself, and the enemy's preparations disturbed. He does not cite this list Guicciardini .02.

Commentary · 3

Argument. The armed case: armed kingdoms are the more difficult to vanquish the nearer one comes, because such bodies can unite more forces to resist than to assail. Hannibal's authority does not move Machiavelli in this case, because Hannibal's passion and his advantage made him speak so to Antiochus. If the Romans had had in Gaul, in so short a space of time, the three routs they had from Hannibal in Italy, Machiavelli says, without doubt they were despatched: they could not have availed themselves of the residues of their armies, remade themselves as conveniently, or resisted with the forces they had. It is not found that Rome, to assail a province, ever sent out armies above fifty thousand, but to defend home it armed eighteen hundred thousand against the Gauls; it could not have routed them in Lombardy as it did in Tuscany. The Cimbri routed a Roman army in Germany, where the Romans had no remedy, and were despatched once they reached Italy and the Romans could put all their forces together; the Swiss are easy to vanquish outside their home, where they cannot send more than thirty or forty thousand men, and most difficult at home, where they can muster a hundred thousand. The counterfactual is stated as a certainty, and the figures are Machiavelli's.

History. The Cimbri defeated Roman armies beyond the Alps (at Noreia in Noricum, 113 BC; at Arausio in Gaul, 105 BC) and were destroyed by Marius at Vercellae in 101 BC. The Gallic war after the first Punic war is presumably the invasion of 225 BC, ended at Telamon in Etruria. The identifications are the editor's.

Cross-reference. This counterfactual does not move Guicciardini: it supposes an impossible case, that whoever is routed at another's home, above all in a distant place, could so suddenly after the first rout have sent two new armies there, one after the other Guicciardini .02.

Conchiudo adunque di nuovo, che quel Principe che ha i suoi Popoli armati e ordinati alla guerra, aspetti sempre in casa una guerra potente e pericolosa, e non la vada a rincontrare. Argument. The conclusion is repeated as counsel and divided in two. Se nel presente Discorso si truovano esempli assai nell'una e l'altra opinione, ci sono anche ragioni assai che fanno il caso sì dubio, che non è di facile resoluzione, e a volerlo bene deliberare ha bisogno di molte considerazioni che sono state pretermesse dallo Autore. Perché non basta sola quella distinzione: o io ho e' sudditi armati o e' sono disarmati, ma è necessario pensare più oltre: o e' populi miei sono fedeli o e' sono inclinati alle ribellioni, o le terre sono forti, o le sono deboli; o io posso, ancora che io abbia la guerra in casa che mi consumi le entrate, in quanto al danaio sostenerla lungamente, o io non potrei reggerla. S'ha ancora a considerare le condizioni dello inimico, cioè che milizia ha, che paesi, che entrate, che modo a sostenere la guerra in casa, che modo a farla fuora di casa; perché il governo e tutte le azioni della guerra s'hanno sempre a regolare secondo le qualità e i progressi dello inimico. È ancora differenza, quando io aspetto guerra da altri, il dire: io la porto a casa sua; il dire: io esco del mio paese, e rincontro lo inimico fuori del paese suo (e questo è lo esemplo del re Ferrando). È differenza il dire: io comincio la guerra in sul suo innanzi che lui l'abbia cominciata a me; a dire: io ho già la guerra in casa; ma per constrignere lo inimico a partirsene, io la comincio anche in sul suo, come fe Scipione quando Annibale era in Italia, come fece Agatocle assediato da Cartaginesi, come e' Fiorentini tante volte nelle guerre fatte loro da' Visconti. E quanto a questo ultimo caso io giudicherò sempre che chi ha la guerra in casa, se ha opportunità nel tempo medesimo di cominciarla in quello dello inimico, lo debba fare; perché essendo cosa inaspettata, disordina tutti e' disegni dello inimico; e ogni piccolo successo che vi abbia, lo constrigne a ritirarsi con tutto [o] con parte delle forze sue a difendere casa sua, e interviene come de' remedii che usano questi fisici a curare le infermità, tra' quali sempre la diversione è giudicata remedio potente e molto approvato.
If in the present Discourse many examples are found for the one and the other opinion, there are also many reasons which make the case so doubtful that it is not of easy resolution, and, [for one] wishing to deliberate it well, [it] has need of many considerations which have been passed over by the Author. For that distinction alone is not enough: either I have the subjects armed or they are disarmed; but it is necessary to think further: either my peoples are faithful or they are inclined to rebellions; either the towns are strong, or they are weak; either I can, even though I have the war at home that consumes my revenues, as to money sustain it long, or I could not uphold it. One has also to consider the conditions of the enemy, that is, what soldiery he has, what countries, what revenues, what way to sustain the war at home, what way to make it outside home; because the government and all the proceedings of the war have always to be regulated according to the qualities and the progress of the enemy. There is a difference also, when I await war from others, [between] saying: I carry it to his home; [and] saying: I go out of my country, and meet the enemy outside his country (and this is the example of King Ferdinand). There is a difference [between] saying: I begin the war on his [ground] before he has begun it on me; [and] saying: I have the war already at home, but to constrain the enemy to depart from it, I begin it also on his, as Scipio did when Hannibal was in Italy, as Agathocles did, besieged by Carthaginians, as the Florentines [did] so many times in the wars made on them by the Visconti. And as to this last case, I shall always judge that whoever has the war at home, if he has opportunity at the same time to begin it in that of the enemy, must do it; because, being a thing unexpected, it disorders all the enemy's designs; and every small success that he may have there constrains him to withdraw with all or with part of his forces to defend his home; and it befalls as with the remedies that these physicians use to cure sicknesses, among which diversion is always judged a powerful remedy and much approved.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The Discourse has many examples for both opinions, he says, and there are also many reasons that make the case so doubtful that it is not easily resolved; deliberating it well needs many considerations the Author passed over. Machiavelli's distinction, armed or disarmed subjects, is not enough by itself. One must also ask whether one's peoples are faithful or inclined to rebellions, whether the towns are strong or weak, and whether one can, as to money, sustain a war at home long while it consumes one's revenues; and of the enemy, what soldiery, countries, revenues and ways he has to sustain war at home and make it abroad, because the government and all the proceedings of a war must always be regulated by the enemy's qualities and progress. He then draws two differences. When one awaits war from others, saying "I carry it to his home" is not the same as saying "I go out of my country and meet the enemy outside his", and to this he adds, in a parenthesis, the example of King Ferdinand, without saying which part of it he means. And beginning a war in the enemy's country before he begins it is not the same as beginning it there, when the war is already at home, to constrain him to depart, as Scipio, Agathocles and the Florentines did. On that last case he gives a judgment, "I shall always judge": whoever has war at home, if he has opportunity at the same time to begin it in the enemy's country, must do it, because the surprise disorders the enemy's designs and any small success constrains him to withdraw with all or part of his forces. It befalls, he says, as with the remedies physicians use to cure sicknesses, among which diversion is always judged a powerful remedy and much approved.

Cross-reference. The distinction he finds not enough by itself is Machiavelli's own Machiavelli .05. Ferdinand is Machiavelli's modern example Machiavelli .03; Scipio and Agathocles are among the authorities cited for assailing Machiavelli .02, here set under a different case; and the Florentines who fought the Visconti answer Machiavelli's Florentines who assailed the duke of Milan Machiavelli .05.

Resta la resoluzione degli altri casi, ne' quali procedendo per distinzione, dico che quando lo inimico da chi tu [temi] la guerra ha più esercito e più potenza di te, che tu non puoi pensare di fargli la guerra in casa, perché bisognano molte forze e molte opportunità a portare la guerra a casa di altri, le quali non sono così necessarie a chi fa guerra in casa sua, perché si serve del favore del paese, de' sudditi e delle difficultà degli inimici, co' quali remedii può andarsi temporeggiando. E in questo grado era il re Ferrando, il quale non poteva mettere in campagna esercito pari a quello degli inimici. Ma quando tu ti senti e di gente e di danari e dell'altre opportunità della guerra pari allo inimico, e ordinato di quelle forze che sono necessarie a fare guerra in casa sua, io sarei inclinato a consigliare di non aspettare la guerra a casa propria, perché vincendo, il premio è maggiore, potendoti portare quella vittoria facilmente lo acquisto del regno di altri; dove la vittoria in casa tua non ti porta altro che la liberazione del tuo Stato, perdendo, il danno è minore, perché non perdi altro che quello esercito, e hai più tempo a rifarti; dove perdendo in casa, se lo inimico accelera la vittoria, come potette fare Annibale a Canne, come a' tempi moderni Paolo Orsino a Ladislao, il duca Giovanni al re Ferrando, una giornata è bastante a farti perdere lo Stato. There remains the resolution of the other cases, in which, proceeding by distinction, I say that when the enemy from whom you fear the war has more army and more power than you, that you cannot think of making the war on him at home, because many forces and many opportunities are required to carry the war to the home of others, which are not so necessary to whoever makes war at his own home, because he makes use of the favour of the country, of the subjects, and of the difficulties of the enemies, with which remedies he can go on temporizing. And in this degree was King Ferdinand, who could not put into the open country an army even with that of the enemies. But when you feel yourself, both in troops and in money and in the other opportunities of war, even with the enemy, and ordered with those forces that are necessary to make war at his home, I should be inclined to counsel not awaiting the war at one's own home, because, winning, the reward is greater, that victory being able readily to bring you the acquisition of the kingdom of others, whereas victory at your home brings you nothing other than the liberation of your state; losing, the damage is less, because you lose nothing other than that army, and you have more time to remake yourself; whereas, losing at home, if the enemy accelerates the victory, as Hannibal could do at Cannae, as in modern times Paolo Orsino to Ladislaus, Duke John to King Ferdinand, one battle is enough to make you lose the state. Carrying the war to the enemy's home, you have already disturbed his design of making the war at your home, you have impeded the preparations necessary to this effect, in such a way that, even vanquishing you, he has need of time and of new orders to come to wage war on you at home; which gives you space to reorder and remake yourself. And so much the more readily I should adhere to this conclusion, the more I saw the enemy not to have a strong country, or not to have faithful subjects, or [his] state so conditioned that his revenues could readily be disordered, or it to be difficult for him, if he had a rout, to remake himself in a short space of time. I see that the Romans always, when they could, forestalled the wars at others' home, against Philip king of Macedonia, against Antiochus, against the Carthaginians; and when they did not do it, they were discontented not to have done it. Nor does what the Writer says move me, that if the Romans had had, in so much space of time, those three routs in France that they had in Italy from Hannibal, they would without doubt have been despatched; because an impossible case is set, that whoever has a rout at the home of others, above all in a distant place, could so suddenly, after the first rout, have sent there again, the one after the other, two new armies. And whoever resolves well on the course of fleeing war at home by carrying it to the home of others, goes there on such a foundation that he can as well hope to rout the enemy as fear to be routed; otherwise he awaits it at home, as the Romans did from Hannibal; who, being already for many years, as Livy says, inexpert in war, and having the war with a captain and with soldiers most expert, if they were routed at home, would perhaps more readily have been, at the beginning of the war, routed by him in Spain or in Africa
Commentary · 3

Argument. The other cases are resolved by distinction, in his own voice ("I say"). When the enemy whose war you fear has more army and power than you, you cannot think of making war on him at home, because carrying war abroad needs many forces and opportunities that are not so necessary to whoever makes war at his own home: he makes use of the favour of the country, of the subjects, and of the enemy's difficulties, and can temporize. Ferdinand, who could not put an even army into the open country, is the example of that degree. Where forces are even and you are ordered with the forces needed for war in the enemy's country, he "should be inclined to counsel" not awaiting war at home, and gives reasons: victory abroad can readily bring a kingdom, victory at home only the liberation of your state; defeat abroad loses nothing but that army and leaves more time to remake yourself, while at home, if the enemy accelerates the victory, one battle is enough to make you lose the state: as Hannibal could do at Cannae, and, in modern times, Paolo Orsino against Ladislaus and Duke John against King Ferdinand. Carrying the war out also disturbs the enemy's design and impedes his preparations. He should adhere to this conclusion the more readily, the more he saw the enemy without a strong country, or without faithful subjects, or with his state so conditioned that his revenues could readily be disordered, or unable, if routed, to remake himself in a short space of time. He sees that the Romans always, when they could, forestalled wars at others' home, against Philip of Macedonia, Antiochus and the Carthaginians, and were discontented when they did not. What the Writer says of three Roman routs in Gaul does not move him, because it sets an impossible case: that whoever has a rout at another's home, above all in a distant place, could so suddenly after the first rout have sent there again two new armies, one after the other. Whoever resolves well to flee war at home by carrying it to another's goes on such a foundation that he can as well hope to rout the enemy as fear to be routed; otherwise he awaits it at home, as the Romans did with Hannibal. The Romans had been, as Livy says, inexpert in war for many years and faced a most expert captain and soldiers; if they were routed at home, they would perhaps more readily have been routed by him at the beginning of the war in Spain or in Africa. The reply ends there, under that "perhaps", without a stop.

History. Paolo Orsino's action against King Ladislaus of Naples and Duke John of Anjou's against King Ferdinand are named by Guicciardini without detail; on the editor's reading the second is John of Anjou's victory over Ferdinand I at Sarno in 1460. The first is not identified here. Philip is presumably Philip V of Macedon and Antiochus is Antiochus III (the editor's identifications).

Cross-reference. "Nor does what the Writer says move me" turns Machiavelli's own words against Hannibal on Machiavelli Machiavelli .06. His reasons for carrying the war abroad can be read beside the reasons Machiavelli reports, which he does not cite Machiavelli .04, and the Roman counterfactual that does not move him is the one in Machiavelli .06. He does not take up Croesus, Hannibal's counsel, Athens, Antaeus, Castruccio, the Cimbri or the Swiss, and he does not deny Machiavelli's conclusion for armed and disarmed peoples Machiavelli .07; he adds the conditions under which he would decide.
