A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 30 of 39

Money is not the sinew of war, as is the common opinion Discourses II.10, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli argues that good soldiers, not money as the common opinion holds, are the sinew of war, since good soldiers can find gold and gold cannot find good soldiers; money is necessary in the second place. Guicciardini answers that the sentence's author did not mean money alone is enough, or more necessary than soldiers, but that without it war cannot be sustained; armies that sometimes find it by virtù and victory are very rare, above all in our times. He grants that soldiers of one's own make war with less money, but money is still required, not everyone has such soldiers, and it is much easier to find soldiers with money than money with soldiers; read as meant, and as commonly understood, the sentence is not to be condemned.

2.10.017 notes2 variants
Perché ciascuno può cominciare una guerra a sua posta, ma non finirla, debbe un Principe avanti che prenda una impresa misurare le forze sue, e secondo quelle governarsi. Ma debbe avere tanta prudenza, che delle sue forze ei non s'inganni; ed ogni volta s'ingannerà quando le misuri o dai danari, o dal sito, o dalla benivolenza degli uomini, mancando dall'altra parte d'armi proprie. Perché le cose predette ti accrescono bene le forze, ma le non te le danno, e per sé medesime sono nulla, e non giovano alcuna cosa senza l'armi fedeli. Perché i danari assai non ti bastano senza quelle, non ti giova la fortezza del paese, e la fede e benivolenza degli uomini non dura, perché questi non ti possono esser fedeli, non li potendo difendere. Ogni monte, ogni lago, ogni luogo inaccessibile diventa piano, dove i forti difensori mancano. I danari ancora non solo non ti difendono, ma ti fanno predare più presto.
Because each one can begin a war at his pleasure, but not finish it, a prince must, before he takes up an enterprise, measure his forces, and govern himself according to them. But he must have such prudence that he does not deceive himself about his forces; and he will deceive himself every time that he measures them either by money, or by the site, or by the benevolence of men, lacking, on the other hand, arms of his own. For the things aforesaid do indeed increase your forces, but they do not give them to you, and by themselves they are nothing, and do not help in any thing without faithful arms. For much money is not enough for you without them, the strength of the country does not help you, and the faith and benevolence of men does not last, because these cannot be faithful to you, you not being able to defend them. Every mountain, every lake, every inaccessible place becomes [a] plain where strong defenders fail. Money, too, not only does not defend you, but makes you be plundered the sooner.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The chapter opens on a rule of prudence, not on money. A war can be begun at will but not ended at will, so a prince must measure his forces before an enterprise and govern himself by them; and he deceives himself whenever he measures them by money, by the site, or by the benevolence of men, while lacking arms of his own. Those things do increase forces, but they do not give them, and without faithful arms they are nothing: money is not enough, the strength of the country does not help, faith does not last where the prince cannot defend those who keep it. The last sentence goes further: money not only fails to defend, it makes a prince be plundered sooner. Of the three measures only money is carried into the title and the rest of the chapter.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's one concession is made on this paragraph's term, put as soldiers of one's own Guicciardini .01: that they make war with less money than mercenaries. He does not grant that arms of one's own are the measure of a prince's forces.

2.10.025 notes1 variant
Né può essere più falsa quella comune opinione che dice, che i danari sono il nervo della guerra. La quale sentenza è detta da Quinto Curzio nella guerra che fu tra Antipatro macedone e il Re spartano; dove narra, che per difetto di danari il Re di Sparta fu necessitato azzuffarsi, e fu rotto; che se ei differiva la zuffa pochi giorni, veniva la nuova in Grecia della morte d'Alessandro, donde e' sarebbe rimaso vincitore senza combattere. Ma mancandogli i danari, e dubitando che lo esercito suo per difetto di quelli non lo abbandonasse, fu costretto tentare la fortuna della zuffa. Talché Quinto Curzio per questa cagione afferma, i danari essere il nervo della guerra. La qual sentenza è allegata ogni giorno, e da' Principi non tanto prudenti che basti, seguitata. Perché fondatisi sopra quella, credono che basti loro a difendersi avere tesoro assai, e non pensano che se il tesoro bastasse a vincere, che Dario arebbe vinto Alessandro, i Greci arebbono vinti i Romani, ne' nostri tempi il Duca Carlo arebbe vinti i Svizzeri, e pochi giorni sono il Papa e i Fiorentini insieme non arebbono avuta difficultà in vincere Francesco Maria, nipote di Papa Giulio II nella guerra di Urbino. Ma tutti i soprannominati furono vinti da coloro, che non il danaro, ma i buoni soldati stimano essere il nervo della guerra.
Nor can that common opinion be more false which says that money is the sinew of war. Which sentence is said by Quintus Curtius in the war that was between Antipater the Macedonian and the Spartan king; where he narrates that for defect of money the king of Sparta was necessitated to engage, and was routed; for, had he deferred the engagement a few days, the news of the death of Alexander would have come into Greece, whence he would have remained victor without fighting. But, money failing him, and doubting lest his army, for defect of it, should abandon him, he was constrained to try the fortune of the engagement. So that Quintus Curtius, for this cause, affirms money to be the sinew of war. Which sentence is cited every day and, by princes not so prudent as would be enough, followed. For, having founded themselves upon it, they believe that to have much treasure is enough for them to defend themselves, and they do not think that, if treasure were enough to win, Darius would have vanquished Alexander, the Greeks would have vanquished the Romans, in our times Duke Charles would have vanquished the Swiss, and, a few days ago, the pope and the Florentines together would not have had difficulty in vanquishing Francesco Maria, nephew of Pope Julius II, in the war of Urbino. But all the above-named were vanquished by those who esteem not money but good soldiers to be the sinew of war.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The common opinion is named and called as false as an opinion can be: that money is the sinew of war. Machiavelli gives its source and its occasion, Quintus Curtius on the Spartan king who, short of money and doubting lest his army leave him, fought and was routed a few days before the news of Alexander's death would, he says, have given him the victory without a battle. The sentence is now cited daily and followed by princes not prudent enough, who believe treasure enough to defend them. Four counterfactuals answer them, two ancient and two of his own time, and a single rule closes the paragraph: those who won esteemed good soldiers, not money, the sinew of war.

History. On the editor's identification, the king is Agis III of Sparta, defeated by Antipater at Megalopolis in 331 BC, whose war Curtius tells; Alexander died in 323, and the chronology of the news is Machiavelli's. The phrase occurs in Cicero's Fifth Philippic; the attribution to Curtius is Machiavelli's, and the editor has not checked whether Curtius uses it. Charles the Bold of Burgundy was beaten by the Swiss at Grandson and Morat in 1476 and killed at Nancy in 1477. Leo X and Florence drove Francesco Maria della Rovere from Urbino in 1516 and fought his return in 1517; to the editor, pochi giorni sono suggests the chapter was written close to that war.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini takes up the sentence itself, not the examples, and states what its author meant by it Guicciardini .01.

2.10.034 notes
Tra l'altre cose che Creso Re di Lidia mostrò a Solone ateniese, fu un tesoro innumerabile; e domandando quel che gli pareva della potenza sua, gli rispose Solone, che per quello non lo giudicava più potente, perché la guerra si faceva col ferro e non con l'oro, e che poteva venire uno che avesse più ferro di lui, e torgliene. Oltra di questo, quando dopo la morte d'Alessandro Magno una moltitudine di Francesi passò in Grecia, e poi in Asia, e mandando i Francesi oratori al Re di Macedonia per trattare certo accordo, quel Re per mostrare la potenza sua e per isbigottirli, mostrò loro oro ed argento assai; donde quelli Francesi che di già avevano come ferma la pace, la ruppero; tanto desiderio in loro crebbe di torgli quell'oro. E così fu quel Re spogliato per quella cosa, che egli aveva per sua difesa accumulata. I Veneziani pochi anni sono, avendo ancora lo erario loro pieno di tesoro, perderono tutto lo Stato, senza potere essere difesi da quello.
Among the other things that Croesus, king of Lydia, showed to Solon the Athenian was an innumerable treasure; and, [Croesus] asking what seemed to him of his power, Solon answered him that for that he did not judge him more powerful, because war was made with steel and not with gold, and that one might come who had more steel than he, and take it away from him. Beyond this, when, after the death of Alexander the Great, a multitude of the French passed into Greece, and then into Asia, and, the French sending orators to the king of Macedonia to treat of a certain accord, that king, to show his power and to frighten them, showed them much gold and silver; whence those French, who already held the peace as firm, broke it; so much desire grew in them to take that gold away from him. And so that king was despoiled through that thing which he had accumulated for his defence. The Venetians, a few years ago, having their treasury still full of treasure, lost all the state, without being able to be defended by it.
Commentary · 2

Argument. Three cases in which treasure did not make its holder powerful. Solon told Croesus that his treasure did not make him more powerful, since war was made with steel, and a man with more steel could take it from him. A king of Macedonia showed gold to the envoys of the invading French to frighten them, and they broke the peace to take it: what he had gathered for his defence despoiled him. The Venetians lost all their state with their treasury full, without its defending them. The cases move from a saying, to a story, to Machiavelli's own time; none is argued, each is set down as showing the same thing.

History. Solon's answer to Croesus is told in later Greek writers; the editor has not identified Machiavelli's source. Machiavelli's French are, on the editor's identification, the Gauls who invaded Macedonia and Greece in 280-279 BC and crossed into Asia Minor; the king who showed them his gold is not named. The Venetian loss is, on the editor's identification, the defeat at Agnadello in 1509; tutto lo Stato is Machiavelli's.

2.10.042 notes1 variant
Dico pertanto non l'oro, come grida la comune opinione, essere il nervo della guerra, ma i buoni soldati; perché l'oro non è sufficiente a trovare i buoni soldati, ma i buoni soldati sono ben sufficienti a trovar l'oro. Ai Romani, s'egli avessero voluto fare la guerra più coi danari che con il ferro, non sarebbe bastato avere tutto il tesoro del Mondo, considerato le grandi imprese che fecero, e le difficultà che vi ebbero dentro. Ma facendo le loro guerre con il ferro, non patirono mai carestia dell'oro, perché da quelli che li temevano era portato l'oro infino ne' campi.
I say, on that account, that not gold, as the common opinion cries, is the sinew of war, but good soldiers; because gold is not sufficient to find good soldiers, but good soldiers are indeed sufficient to find gold. For the Romans, if they had wished to make war more with money than with steel, it would not have been enough to have all the treasure of the world, considering the great enterprises that they made and the difficulties that they had in them. But, making their wars with steel, they never suffered dearth of gold, because by those who feared them gold was carried even into the camps.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The thesis, stated in his own voice (Dico pertanto) against the opinion that cries the contrary: not gold but good soldiers are the sinew of war, because gold is not sufficient to find good soldiers, and good soldiers are sufficient to find gold. The proof is Rome, put as a counterfactual. Had the Romans fought more with money than with steel, all the treasure of the world would not have been enough for the enterprises they made; fighting with steel, they never suffered dearth of gold, which those who feared them carried into the camps. The claim is about which of the two can find the other, not that money is useless.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini turns the chiasmus of this paragraph round, più facile co' danari trovare soldati, che co' soldati trovare danari Guicciardini .01, in Machiavelli's verb, find, with easy for sufficient and soldiers for good soldiers. His non corrono e' danari dietro agli eserciti, se non da poi che hanno vinto answers the gold carried into the Roman camps.

2.10.056 notes
E se quel Re spartano per carestia di danari ebbe a tentare la fortuna della zuffa, intervenne a lui quello per conto de' danari, che molte volte è intervenuto per altre cagioni; perché si è veduto che mancando ad uno esercito le vettovaglie, ed essendo necessitati o a morire di fame o azzuffarsi si piglia il partito sempre d'azzuffarsi, per essere più onorevole, e dove la fortuna ti può in qualche modo favorire. Ancora è intervenuto molte volte, che veggendo un Capitano al suo esercito nimico venire soccorso, gli conviene o azzuffarsi con quello e tentare la fortuna della zuffa, o aspettando ch'egli ingrossi avere a combattere in ogni modo con mille suoi disavvantaggi. Ancora si è visto, come intervenne ad Asdrubale quando nella Marca fu assaltato da Claudio Nerone, insieme con l'altro Consolo romano, che un Capitano che è necessitato o a fuggirsi o a combattere, come sempre elegge il combattere; parendogli in questo partito, ancoraché dubbiosissimo, poter vincere, e in quell'altro avere a perdere in ogni modo. Sono adunque molte necessitadi che fanno a un Capitano fuori della sua intenzione pigliare partito d'azzuffarsi, e tra le quali qualche volta può essere la carestia dei danari; né per questo si debbono i danari giudicare essere il nervo della guerra, più che l'altre cose, che inducono gli uomini a simili necessità.
And if that Spartan king, through dearth of money, had to try the fortune of the engagement, there befell him, on account of money, that which has many times befallen through other causes; for it has been seen that, the victuals failing an army, and [the men] being necessitated either to die of hunger or to engage, the course of engaging is always taken, as being more honourable, and [the course] where fortune can in some way favour you. Again, it has befallen many times that a captain, seeing succour come to the army [that is] his enemy, it is fitting for him either to engage with it and try the fortune of the engagement, or, waiting until it grows in force, to have to fight in any case, with a thousand disadvantages of his own. Again, it has been seen, as befell Hasdrubal when in the Marches he was assaulted by Claudius Nero together with the other Roman consul, that a captain who is necessitated either to flee or to fight always chooses fighting, it seeming to him that in this course, even though most doubtful, he can win, and in that other he has to lose in any case. There are, then, many necessities that make a captain, outside his intention, take the course of engaging, and among which the dearth of money can sometimes be; nor for this must money be judged to be the sinew of war, more than the other things that induce men to like necessities.
Commentary · 2

Argument. Back to the Spartan king, to take the case away from the opinion. What befell him through want of money has befallen armies through other causes: an army short of victuals takes the course of engaging as more honourable and as the course where fortune may favour it; a captain who sees succour coming to his enemy engages now, or later with a thousand disadvantages; a captain forced to flee or fight always fights, as Hasdrubal did against the two consuls. Want of money is only one of the necessities that make a captain engage against his intention, sometimes, and it no more proves money the sinew of war than any other. The argument removes the example; it does not deny that the want of money can force a battle.

History. On the editor's identification, Hasdrubal, Hannibal's brother, was caught by the consuls Gaius Claudius Nero and Marcus Livius Salinator at the Metaurus in 207 BC; Livy tells the battle in Book 27.

2.10.067 notes2 variants
Non è adunque, replicandolo di nuovo, l'oro il nervo della guerra, ma i buoni soldati. Sono ben necessari i danari in secondo luogo, ma è una necessità, che i soldati buoni per sé medesimi la vincono; perché è impossibile che a' buoni soldati manchino i danari, come che i danari per loro medesimi trovino i buoni soldati. Mostra, questo che noi diciamo essere vero, ogni istoria in mille luoghi. Non ostante che Pericle consigliasse gli Ateniesi a far guerra con tutto il Peloponneso, mostrando che e' potevano vincere quella guerra con la industria e con la forza del danaro, e benché in tale guerra gli Ateniesi prosperassero qualche volta, in ultimo la perderono, e valsono più il consiglio e i buoni soldati di Sparta, che la industria ed il danaro di Atene. Ma Tito Livio è di questa opinione più vero testimone che alcun altro, dove discorrendo se Alessandro Magno fusse venuto in Italia, s'egli avesse vinto i Romani, mostra essere tre cose necessarie nella guerra, assai soldati e buoni, Capitani prudenti, e buona fortuna: dove esaminando quali o i Romani o Alessandro prevalessero in queste cose, fa dipoi la sua conclusione senza ricordare mai i danari. Doverono i Campani, quando furono richiesti dai Sidicini che prendessero l'arme per loro contro ai Sanniti, misurare la potenza loro dai danari, e non dai soldati; perché preso ch'egli ebbero partito di ajutarli, dopo due rotte furono costretti farsi tributarj de' Romani, se si vollono salvare.
Gold, then, to repeat it anew, is not the sinew of war, but good soldiers. Money is indeed necessary in the second place, but it is a necessity which good soldiers by themselves vanquish; because it is impossible that money should fail good soldiers, as [it is impossible] that money by itself should find good soldiers. Every history shows in a thousand places that this which we say is true. Notwithstanding that Pericles counselled the Athenians to make war with all the Peloponnese, showing that they could win that war with industry and with the force of money, and albeit in such a war the Athenians prospered sometimes, in the end they lost it, and the counsel and the good soldiers of Sparta were of more worth than the industry and the money of Athens. But Livy is a truer witness of this opinion than any other, where, discoursing, if Alexander the Great had come into Italy, whether he would have vanquished the Romans, he shows three things to be necessary in war, many soldiers and good, prudent captains, and good fortune: where, examining which, either the Romans or Alexander, prevailed in these things, he afterwards makes his conclusion without ever recalling money. The Campanians, when they were asked by the Sidicini to take up arms for them against the Samnites, must have measured their power by money, and not by soldiers; because, having taken the course of aiding them, after two routs they were constrained to make themselves tributaries of the Romans, if they wished to save themselves.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The conclusion, with its concession. Gold is not the sinew of war but good soldiers; money is necessary in the second place, and that necessity good soldiers vanquish by themselves, since it is impossible that money should fail good soldiers, as it is impossible that money by itself should find good soldiers. History shows it. Athens, advised by Pericles that it could win with industry and the force of money, prospered sometimes and lost to Sparta's counsel and good soldiers. Livy, weighing Alexander against Rome, names soldiers, captains and fortune and, Machiavelli says, never recalls money: an argument from silence. The Campanians, who on Machiavelli's inference must have measured their power by money, were routed twice and became tributaries of Rome. The concession, money necessary in the second place, is what Guicciardini's reply most nearly touches.

History. On the editor's identifications, Pericles' estimate of Athenian resources is in Thucydides 1.140-144 and 2.13; Livy's digression on Alexander is 9.17-19; the Campanians' defence of the Sidicini against the Samnites and their surrender to Rome are in Livy 7.29-31.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not deny that soldiers are needed; he says that without money war cannot be sustained, in a quantity hard to imagine, and that it is easier to find soldiers with money than money with soldiers Guicciardini .01. He does not refer to the second place, and he does not take up Pericles, Livy or the Campanians.

Notes

danari, danaro; il nervo della guerra. "Money"; "the sinew of war". Machiavelli's plural danari and his singular danaro (.02, .06) are both "money", a recorded collapse; oro, gold, and tesoro, treasure, are kept apart from it, since the chapter moves among all three. nervo is singular, "sinew", not the proverbial English plural.

¶ 01 a sua posta. "At his pleasure": when he chooses.

¶ 01 ed ogni volta s'ingannerà quando le misuri o dai danari, o dal sito, o dalla benivolenza degli uomini. "And he will deceive himself every time that he measures them either by money, or by the site, or by the benevolence of men": ingannarsi, deceive oneself (glossary); sito, site (glossary); benivolenza, benevolence. The three false measures are the three things taken in turn in the next two periods: money, the strength of the country, the faith and benevolence of men. The 1554 runs the clauses together (non s'ingannera, quando) and has no money in the list, the measure the chapter is about (NM-DISC-II10-D001, D428).

¶ 01 mancando dall'altra parte d'armi proprie. "Lacking, on the other hand, arms of his own": mancare di, lack (D312); dall'altra parte, on the other hand (D24). armi proprie is the term the chapter turns on, held as "arms of one's own"; Guicciardini's soldati proprii is "soldiers of his own" (Guicciardini .01).

¶ 01 le cose predette ti accrescono bene le forze, ma le non te le danno. "The things aforesaid do indeed increase your forces, but they do not give them to you": accrescere, increase (D69); forze, forces (glossary). The second person enters here and runs to ti fanno predare; the English keeps it. The doubled le is the Florentine subject pronoun, not rendered twice.

¶ 01 non ti giova la fortezza del paese. "The strength of the country does not help you": fortezza here is neither the virtue, fortitude (FG I.10), nor a stronghold, fortress (D312), but what mountains, lakes and passes give a country, as the next period shows. "Strength", a third sense of the word decided by sense, as fazione (D432).

¶ 01 diventa piano, dove i forti difensori mancano. "Becomes [a] plain where strong defenders fail": piano, flat, open ground, the noun supplied so that "plain" is not read as "evident"; mancare, fail (D206).

¶ 01 ma ti fanno predare più presto. "But makes you be plundered the sooner": predare, to take as prey; più presto without a following che is "sooner", not D135's "rather than".

¶ 02 La quale sentenza è detta da Quinto Curzio nella guerra. "Which sentence is said by Quintus Curtius in the war": sentenza, sentence, an opinion pronounced (glossary); the 1554 reads e dato (NM-DISC-II10-D005, D428). On the editor's identification, the Spartan king is Agis III, defeated by Antipater in 331 BC, whose war Quintus Curtius Rufus tells; Alexander died in 323, and the chronology of the news is Machiavelli's, not corrected. The editor notes that the phrase occurs in Cicero's Fifth Philippic, nervos belli, pecuniam; the attribution to Curtius is Machiavelli's.

¶ 02 che se ei differiva la zuffa pochi giorni, veniva la nuova. "For, had he deferred the engagement a few days, there would have come ... the news ... would have come": the imperfects carry the unreal condition, as Italian allows and English does not; the clause is exposition and goes into plain order (D263); differire, defer (D334); zuffa, engagement (D49).

¶ 02 dubitando che lo esercito suo per difetto di quelli non lo abbandonasse. "Doubting lest his army, for defect of it, should abandon him": dubitare che ... non, apprehension (D205); with non and a feared outcome, "doubt lest", as in the proem to this book (dubitando non incorrere; D431); difetto, defect (glossary).

¶ 02 da' Principi non tanto prudenti che basti, seguitata. "By princes not so prudent as would be enough, followed": the participle held to the end, as printed; bastare, be enough (D205); seguitare, follow, the verb Guicciardini's reply opens with (chi l'ha poi seguitata). allegare, cite, in its old sense of adducing an authority, not "allege" (D431).

¶ 02 Dario ... il Duca Carlo ... il Papa e i Fiorentini ... Francesco Maria. On the editor's identifications: Darius III, beaten by Alexander; Charles the Bold, duke of Burgundy, beaten by the Swiss in 1476-77; Pope Leo X and Florence against Francesco Maria della Rovere, duke of Urbino, nephew of Julius II, in the war of 1517. pochi giorni sono, "a few days ago", suggests to the editor that the chapter was written close to that war.

vincere. "Vanquish" of an enemy, and of the necessity in .06; "win" of a war (.06 vincere quella guerra) and without an object (.02, .05), as D313 (D431).

¶ 03 fu un tesoro innumerabile; e domandando quel che gli pareva della potenza sua. "Was an innumerable treasure; and, [Croesus] asking what seemed to him of his power": the gerund has no stated subject; the asker is Croesus, and the bracket names him, since an English participle would attach to Solon.

¶ 03 perché la guerra si faceva col ferro e non con l'oro. "Because war was made with steel and not with gold": ferro is "the steel" in the glossary (D402, NM I.58, the sword as remedy); here and at .04 it is the metal of arms counted against the metal of money, and the article is dropped (D433).

¶ 03 una moltitudine di Francesi. "A multitude of the French": Machiavelli's name for the Gauls, kept and noted (names.md, as NM I.7); on the editor's identification, the invasion of Macedonia and Greece in 280-279 BC, and the crossing into Asia. The king of Macedonia is not named.

¶ 03 I Veneziani pochi anni sono. "The Venetians, a few years ago": on the editor's identification, the defeat at Agnadello in 1509; tutto lo Stato, "all the state", is Machiavelli's and is not narrowed. erario, treasury, the public chest; tesoro, treasure.

¶ 04 Dico pertanto non l'oro, come grida la comune opinione, essere il nervo della guerra, ma i buoni soldati. "I say, on that account, that not gold, as the common opinion cries, is the sinew of war, but good soldiers": pertanto, on that account (D98); gridare, cry, as the opinion is said aloud. sufficiente, sufficient, kept apart from bastare, be enough (D205): the chiasmus with gold and good soldiers is held, and it is the sentence Guicciardini turns round (Guicciardini .01).

¶ 04 Ai Romani, s'egli avessero voluto fare la guerra più coi danari che con il ferro. "For the Romans, if they had wished to make war more with money than with steel": egli, plural, as printed. The 1554 reads ſe non haueſſero uoluto, which reverses the condition; the next period answers the 1824's (NM-DISC-II10-D002, D428). considerato le grandi imprese, "considering the great enterprises", the participle unagreed before its object; the 1554 conſiderate (NM-DISC-II10-D005, D428).

¶ 05 E se quel Re spartano per carestia di danari ebbe a tentare la fortuna della zuffa. "And if that Spartan king, through dearth of money, had to try the fortune of the engagement": carestia, dearth, the same word as carestia dell'oro in .04; avere a, necessity, "have to" (D261); intervenire, befall (D298), three times in .05.

¶ 05 si piglia il partito sempre d'azzuffarsi, per essere più onorevole, e dove la fortuna ti può in qualche modo favorire. "The course of engaging is always taken, as being more honourable, and [the course] where fortune can in some way favour you": partito, course (D236), three times in the paragraph; pigliare, take (glossary). The second reason is a place, dove, and the bracket supplies its noun.

¶ 05 che veggendo un Capitano al suo esercito nimico venire soccorso, gli conviene o azzuffarsi con quello. "That a captain, seeing succour come to the army [that is] his enemy, it is fitting for him either to engage with it": the sentence changes construction after its subject, and the English keeps the break; in ogni modo, "in any case", here and below (D431); convenire, impersonal, to be fitting (D85); ingrossare, grow in force, with grosso of an army (D238).

¶ 05 come intervenne ad Asdrubale quando nella Marca fu assaltato da Claudio Nerone, insieme con l'altro Consolo romano. On the editor's identification, Hasdrubal, Hannibal's brother, at the Metaurus in 207 BC, against the consuls Gaius Claudius Nero and Marcus Livius Salinator; the second is not named. la Marca, the Marches.

¶ 05 che un Capitano che è necessitato o a fuggirsi o a combattere, come sempre elegge il combattere. "That a captain who is necessitated either to flee or to fight always chooses fighting": come after che repeats the conjunction and is not rendered; Capitano, captain (glossary); eleggere, choose (glossary); dubbiosissimo, most doubtful.

¶ 05 fuori della sua intenzione. "Outside his intention": against his purpose. necessitadi, necessities, one root held with necessitare, necessitate, necessità, necessity, and necessario, necessary, through .05 and .06.

¶ 06 Non è adunque, replicandolo di nuovo, l'oro il nervo della guerra, ma i buoni soldati. "Gold, then, to repeat it anew, is not the sinew of war, but good soldiers": the restatement of .04, in plain order (D263), the gerund given as an infinitive so that it does not attach to "gold"; adunque, then (D74); replicare, repeat (D423); di nuovo, anew (glossary). The 1554 adds e before l'oro (NM-DISC-II10-D006, D428).

¶ 06 ma è una necessità, che i soldati buoni per sé medesimi la vincono. "But it is a necessity that good soldiers by themselves vanquish": la is the necessity, and the relative keeps the claim from reading "it is necessary that good soldiers win"; vincere of a difficulty (D313).

¶ 06 come che i danari per loro medesimi trovino i buoni soldati. "As [it is] that money by itself should find good soldiers": come che compares the second impossibility with the first, and the bracket supplies what the Italian carries over.

¶ 06 Mostra, questo che noi diciamo essere vero, ogni istoria in mille luoghi. "Every history shows in a thousand places that this which we say is true": exposition, in plain order (D263); istoria, history (glossary).

¶ 06 mostrando che e' potevano vincere quella guerra con la industria e con la forza del danaro. "Showing that they could win that war with industry and with the force of money": Pericles' counsel, on the editor's identification Thucydides 1.140-144. The 1554 reads & non con la forZa del danaio, which makes Pericles advise against money; the close of the period, che la industria ed il danaro di Atene, answers the 1824's reading (NM-DISC-II10-D003, D428). industria, industry, skill and diligence, not manufacture. benché, albeit (D31); tale guerra, "such a war".

¶ 06 Ma Tito Livio è di questa opinione più vero testimone che alcun altro. "But Livy is a truer witness of this opinion than any other": on the editor's identification, the digression on Alexander in Livy 9.17-19; discorrere, discourse (D01); testimone, witness, as NM I.60 and the proem's testimonj; ricordare, recall, mention. The three things are Livy's (9.17); the editor has not checked whether Livy names money there.

¶ 06 Doverono i Campani ... misurare la potenza loro dai danari, e non dai soldati. "The Campanians ... must have measured their power by money, and not by soldiers": dovere, must (D274), here "must have", of what Machiavelli infers they did (D431). On the editor's identification, Livy 7.29-31: the Campanians took up the Sidicini's cause against the Samnites, were beaten twice, and gave themselves into Roman power. The 1554 names them Capouani, by their city, Capua (NM-DISC-II10-D004, D428). tributarj, tributaries; salvarsi, save oneself (glossary). The unit ends with a stop.

Considerazione sul capitolo X del libro II

Considerazioni II.10

I danari non sono il nervo della guerra, secondo che è la comune oppinione

Money is not the sinew of war, as is the common opinion

Italian · 1857
English
2.10.0117 notes3 variants
Chi fu autore di quella sentenza, che e' danari furno il nervo della guerra, e chi l'ha poi seguitata, non intese che e' danari soli bastassino a fare la guerra, né che e' fussino più necessarii che e' soldati, perché sarebbe stata opinione non solo falsa, ma ancora molto ridicula, ma intese che chi faceva guerra aveva bisogno grandissimo di danari, e che sanza quelli era impossibil[e] a sostenerla, perché non solo sono necessarii per pagare e' soldati, ma per provedere le armi, le vettovaglie, le spie, le munizioni e tanti instrumenti che si adoperano nella guerra, e' quali ne ricercano tanto profluvio, che a chi non l'ha provato è impossibile a immaginarlo. E se bene qualche volta uno esercito carestioso di danari con la virtù sua e col favore delle vittorie gli provede, nondimeno a' tempi nostri massime sono esempli rarissimi; e in ogni caso e in ogni tempo non corrono e' danari dietro agli eserciti, se non da poi che hanno vinto. Confesso che chi ha soldati proprii fa la guerra con manco danari che non fa chi ha soldati mercenarii, nondimeno e anche danari bisognano a chi fa guerra co' soldati proprii, e ognuno non ha soldati proprii, ed è molto più facile co' danari trovare soldati, che co' soldati trovare danari. Chi adunque interpreterà quella sentenza secondo il senso di chi la disse, e secondo che comunemente è intesa, non se ne maraviglierà, né la dannerà in modo alcuno
He who was the author of that sentence, that money was the sinew of war, and whoever has since followed it, did not understand [by it] that money alone was enough to make war, nor that it was more necessary than soldiers, because that would have been an opinion not only false but also very ridiculous; but understood [by it] that whoever made war had very great need of money, and that without it it was impossible to sustain it, because it is necessary not only for paying the soldiers but for providing the arms, the victuals, the spies, the munitions and so many instruments that are employed in war, which call for so great a flood of it that it is impossible for whoever has not tried it to imagine it. And although sometimes an army in dearth of money provides for it by its virtù and by the favour of victories, nonetheless in our times above all these are most rare examples; and in every case and at every time money does not run after armies, except after they have won. I confess that whoever has soldiers of his own makes war with less money than does whoever has mercenary soldiers; nonetheless money too is required by whoever makes war with soldiers of his own, and not everyone has soldiers of his own, and it is much easier with money to find soldiers than with soldiers to find money. Whoever, then, will interpret that sentence according to the sense of him who said it, and as it is commonly understood, will not marvel at it, nor condemn it in any way
Commentary · 2

Argument. Guicciardini passes over the chapter's examples and takes up its reading of the sentence. Whoever first said that money is the sinew of war, and whoever has followed it since, did not mean that money alone makes war, nor that it is more necessary than soldiers: that would have been an opinion false and ridiculous. They meant that whoever made war had very great need of money, and that without it war could not be sustained. The reasons that follow are his own, in the present tense: money is needed to pay soldiers and to provide arms, victuals, spies and munitions, in a quantity no one who has not tried it can imagine. An army in dearth of money has sometimes provided for it by its virtù and its victories, but these are most rare examples, above all in his own time, and in every case and at every time money follows armies only after they have won. He confesses that soldiers of one's own make war with less money than mercenaries; but even they need money, not everyone has them, and it is much easier to find soldiers with money than money with soldiers. The close is a prediction under a condition: whoever interprets the sentence in its author's sense, and as it is commonly understood, will neither marvel at it nor condemn it. He does not call the sentence true in so many words, and he says nothing to deny that soldiers are needed.

Cross-reference. The sentence and its followers are Machiavelli's report of Curtius and of the princes who follow him (La quale sentenza, seguitata) Machiavelli .02; Guicciardini's falsa answers Machiavelli's Né può essere più falsa there. The concession is made on the arms of one's own of Machiavelli .01; the chiasmus reverses Machiavelli .04; and non corrono e' danari dietro agli eserciti answers the gold carried into the Roman camps there. He passes over the Spartan king, the necessities of Machiavelli .05, and every example, and he does not refer to Machiavelli's money necessary in the second place Machiavelli .06.

Notes

The heading and the rubric. Canestrini's heading, CONSIDERAZIONE SUL CAPITOLO X DEL LIBRO II., with del libro II as he prints it for every Book II reply (D421). His rubric, I danari non sono il nervo della guerra, secondo che è la comune oppinione, is Machiavelli's word for word but for the spelling oppinione, and is rendered as Machiavelli's (Machiavelli, rubric). Both readers doubted the accent of è and the stop after oppinione; the verb is read, as the 1824 rubric has it, and no stop is set (D430). Canestrini's note on provede, gathering Machiavelli's sentences on steel and gold with a French proverb, is his commentary and is not carried.

¶ 01 Chi fu autore di quella sentenza, che e' danari furno il nervo della guerra. "He who was the author of that sentence, that money was the sinew of war": sentenza, sentence (glossary), Machiavelli's word for the maxim (Machiavelli .02); furno, were, puts the maxim in the past, as once pronounced. The 1933 edition reads siano, "is", a standing claim; both readers read furno without doubt, and Canestrini is kept (FG-CONS-II10-D002, D429). Guicciardini does not name Curtius.

¶ 01 e chi l'ha poi seguitata, non intese. "And whoever has since followed it, did not understand [by it]": seguitare, follow, Machiavelli's verb for the princes who follow it (Machiavelli .02); intendere, understand (D374), here and at ma intese and comunemente è intesa: the reply is about what the sentence was taken to mean, and the bracket keeps "did not understand that" from reading as a failure to grasp a truth. Chi, the author, is "he who" here and at chi la disse; chi of anyone, "whoever".

¶ 01 né che e' fussino più necessarii che e' soldati. "Nor that it was more necessary than soldiers": danari, money (glossary), takes the English singular throughout; necessario, necessary, Machiavelli's Sono ben necessari i danari in secondo luogo (Machiavelli .06), which Guicciardini's first clauses do not contest.

¶ 01 perché sarebbe stata opinione non solo falsa, ma ancora molto ridicula. "Because that would have been an opinion not only false but also very ridiculous": the counterfactual answers Machiavelli's Né può essere più falsa quella comune opinione (Machiavelli .02) in his own adjective: Guicciardini agrees that such an opinion would be false, and says no one held it. ridicula, ridiculous; grandissimo, below, "very great", as elsewhere in the edition.

¶ 01 aveva bisogno grandissimo di danari, e che sanza quelli era impossibil[e] a sostenerla. "Had very great need of money, and that without it it was impossible to sustain it": bisogno, need (glossary); sanza, without; sostenere, sustain, of a war; "detain" stays for custody (FG I.10). Canestrini's impression prints impossibila; one reader read the a, the other preferred e; emended from the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-II10-D001, D429). The first impossibile answers Machiavelli's è impossibile che a' buoni soldati manchino i danari (Machiavelli .06): what is impossible without money is the war.

¶ 01 le armi, le vettovaglie, le spie, le munizioni e tanti instrumenti. "The arms, the victuals, the spies, the munitions and so many instruments": the catalogue as printed, the articles kept. vettovaglie, victuals, Machiavelli's word for the army's food (Machiavelli .05).

¶ 01 e' quali ne ricercano tanto profluvio. "Which call for so great a flood of it": profluvio, a flowing forth, rendered by the image and not by the medical "profluvium".

¶ 01 E se bene qualche volta uno esercito carestioso di danari con la virtù sua e col favore delle vittorie gli provede. "And although sometimes an army in dearth of money provides for it by its virtù and by the favour of victories": se bene, although (D31); qualche volta, sometimes, as Machiavelli's qualche volta ... la carestia dei danari (Machiavelli .05); carestioso, from carestia, dearth, Machiavelli's word (Machiavelli .04, .05); virtù (D16); gli provede, provides for it, the want of money. Granted as possible, sometimes.

¶ 01 nondimeno a' tempi nostri massime sono esempli rarissimi. "Nonetheless in our times above all these are most rare examples": nondimeno, nonetheless (D45); massime, above all (glossary). The limit is to frequency, not to possibility.

¶ 01 non corrono e' danari dietro agli eserciti, se non da poi che hanno vinto. "Money does not run after armies, except after they have won": correre, run, the root of the currere family (D01), kept literal; the subject of hanno vinto is the armies; vincere without an object, "win" (D313). This is the reply to Machiavelli's Romans, to whom gold was carried into their camps (Machiavelli .04), and it holds in ogni caso e in ogni tempo, without exception.

¶ 01 Confesso. "I confess": the reply's one concession, and a narrow one: soldiers of one's own make war with less money than mercenaries. It does not grant that arms of one's own are the measure of a prince's forces (Machiavelli .01). An act beside credo, non so, reputo, non concorro facilmente and non accetto (D374); in an editor's summary, "grants" (D431).

¶ 01 chi ha soldati proprii fa la guerra con manco danari che non fa chi ha soldati mercenarii. "Whoever has soldiers of his own makes war with less money than does whoever has mercenary soldiers": soldati proprii, Machiavelli's armi proprie (Machiavelli .01) put in his own term; manco, less; non after the comparative is not rendered.

¶ 01 nondimeno e anche danari bisognano a chi fa guerra co' soldati proprii. "Nonetheless money too is required by whoever makes war with soldiers of his own": bisognare, be required (D260), here personal, with danari its subject. The full note on bisognare is at Machiavelli I.2.

¶ 01 e ognuno non ha soldati proprii. "And not everyone has soldiers of his own": the Italian negates the verb, not ognuno, and means that some lack them, not that no one has them.

¶ 01 ed è molto più facile co' danari trovare soldati, che co' soldati trovare danari. "And it is much easier with money to find soldiers than with soldiers to find money": Machiavelli's chiasmus reversed (l'oro non è sufficiente a trovare i buoni soldati, ma i buoni soldati sono ben sufficienti a trovar l'oro, Machiavelli .04), in his verb, trovare, find, with facile, easy (D27), in place of his sufficiente; and soldati, not his buoni soldati.

¶ 01 Chi adunque interpreterà quella sentenza secondo il senso di chi la disse, e secondo che comunemente è intesa. "Whoever, then, will interpret that sentence according to the sense of him who said it, and as it is commonly understood": adunque, then (D74); secondo che, as, as in the rubric; comunemente, commonly, answers Machiavelli's comune opinione (Machiavelli .02, .04).

¶ 01 non se ne maraviglierà, né la dannerà in modo alcuno. "Will not marvel at it, nor condemn it in any way": maravigliarsi, marvel (glossary); dannare, condemn (D99). He does not call the sentence true in so many words; he defends what it was meant to say with reasons of his own, and the close is a prediction under a condition (Chi ... interpreterà). The reply ends without a stop (D40, D429).
