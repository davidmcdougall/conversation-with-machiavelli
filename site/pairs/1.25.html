A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 17 of 39

Whoever wishes to reform an ancient state in a free city, let him retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways Discourses I.25, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that whoever reforms the state of a free city must, to have it accepted, keep at least the shadow of the ancient ways, because men feed as much on what seems as on what is; so Rome kept the kings' number of lictors and a king for the sacrifices, whereas whoever makes a tyranny must renew everything. Guicciardini says the conclusion is more necessary where a government is reformed than where its kind is changed: when a kingdom is made free, as at Rome, men already think the old way of living not good, and the ancient orders need not be kept so exactly. The Roman examples, he says, matter little: one allowed for superstition, and in the other greater insignia would not have been tolerable.

NM-DISC.1.25

Capitolo XXV

Chi vuole riformare uno Stato antico in una Città libera, ritenga almeno l'ombra de' modi antichi.

Whoever wishes to reform an ancient state in a free city, let him retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways.

Italian · 1824
English
1.25.01
Colui che desidera o che vuole riformare uno stato d'una Città, a volere che sia accetto, e poterlo con satisfazione di ciascuno mantenere, è necessitato a ritenere l'ombra almanco de' modi antichi, acciò che ai Popoli non paja avere mutato ordine, ancora che in fatto gli ordini nuovi fussero al tutto alieni dai passati: perché l'universale degli uomini si pasce così di quel che pare, come di quello che è; anzi molte volte si muovono più per le cose che pajono, che per quelle che sono.
He who desires or who wishes to reform a state of a city, if he wishes it to be accepted, and to be able to maintain it with the satisfaction of each, is necessitated to retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways, so that it may not seem to the peoples to have changed order, even though in fact the new orders were altogether alien from the past ones: for the generality of men feeds itself as much on what seems as on what is; indeed many times they are moved more by the things that seem than by those that are.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The precept, its condition and its reason, in one period. Whoever would reform the state of a city, if he wants the reform accepted and maintained with the satisfaction of each, is necessitated to retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways, so that it may not seem to the peoples that the order has changed, even though the new orders are in fact altogether alien from the old. The reason is a claim about the generality of men: they feed on what seems as much as on what is, and many times are moved more by what seems. The precept is conditional: it binds whoever wants the reform accepted and maintained.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini answers this period by ranking the cases it fits Guicciardini .01: reform within a species of government, where it is more necessary, against a change of species, where it is less.

1.25.02
Per questa cagione i Romani conoscendo nel principio del loro vivere libero questa necessità, avendo in cambio d'un Re creati i duoi Consoli, non vollono ch'egli avessero più che dodici Littori, per non passare il numero di quelli che ministravano ai Re. Oltra di questo facendosi in Roma uno sacrifizio anniversario, il quale non poteva essere fatto se non dalla persona del Re, e volendo i Romani che quel Popolo non avesse a desiderare per la assenza degli Re alcuna cosa dell'antiche, crearono un Capo di detto sacrifizio, il quale essi chiamarono Re sacrificolo, e lo sottomessono al sommo Sacerdote. Talmenteché quel Popolo per questa via venne a satisfarsi di quel sacrifizio, e non avere mai cagione per mancamento di esso di desiderare la tornata de' Re.
For this cause the Romans, knowing this necessity in the beginning of their free living, having created in place of one king the two consuls, did not wish them to have more than twelve lictors, so as not to pass the number of those who ministered to the kings. Besides this, there being made in Rome an anniversary sacrifice, which could not be made except by the person of the king, and the Romans wishing that that people should not have to desire, through the absence of the kings, any thing of the ancient ones, [they] created a head of the said sacrifice, whom they called king sacrificulus, and subjected him to the supreme priest. So that that people by this path came to satisfy itself of that sacrifice, and never to have cause, through the failing of it, to desire the return of the kings.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The Roman proof, in two cases, and each keeps something different. When the kings were expelled and two consuls created in place of one king, the consuls were given no more than the twelve lictors the king had had, so as not to pass the old number: the number kept. And since a yearly sacrifice could be made only by the king, the Romans, so that the people should not come to desire, through the absence of the kings, anything of the ancient things, created a head of that sacrifice, called him king, and subjected him to the supreme priest: the name kept, and the man set under the supreme priest. The people were satisfied, and never had the want of it as a cause to desire the kings' return.

History. Livy tells of both. Livy's first consuls keep all the kings' rights and insignia, and the only caution is that both should not have the fasces at once, so that the terror should not seem doubled (2.1.8); the kings' twelve lictors go back to Romulus (1.8). Of the rex sacrificulus, that because some public rites had been performed by the kings in person, a king of sacrifices was created so that the kings should nowhere be missed, and set under the pontifex lest the name, joined to an honour, should stand in the way of liberty (2.2.1-2). Machiavelli's reason for the second case, the people's desire, is Livy's; the first he turns into the keeping of a number.

1.25.03
E questo si debbe osservare da tutti coloro che vogliono scancellare uno antico vivere in una Città, e ridurla ad uno vivere nuovo e libero. Perché alterando le cose nuove le menti degli uomini, ti debbi ingegnare che quelle alterazioni ritengano più dell'antico sia possibile; e se i Magistrati variano e di numero e di autorità e di tempo dagli antichi, che almeno ritengano il nome. E questo, come ho detto, debbe osservare colui che vuole ordinare una potenza assoluta, o per via di Repubblica, o di Regno; ma quello che vuol fare una potestà assoluta, la quale dagli autori è chiamata Tirannide, debbe rinnovare ogni cosa, come nel seguente capitolo si dirà.
And this must be observed by all those who wish to cancel an ancient living in a city, and reduce it to a new and free living. For, new things altering the minds of men, you must contrive that those alterations retain as much of the ancient as is possible; and if the magistracies vary from the ancient ones both in number and in authority and in time, that at least they retain the name. And this, as I have said, he must observe who wishes to order an absolute power, either by the path of a republic or of a kingdom; but he who wishes to make an absolute power, which is called tyranny by the authors, must renew every thing, as will be said in the following chapter.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The general precept, its terms, and its limit. Whoever would cancel an ancient living in a city and bring it to a new and free one must observe this: since new things alter men's minds, the alterations should retain as much of the old as possible, and if the magistracies vary in number, authority and time, they should at least keep the name. The Roman cases return in this list: the lictors were a matter of number, the king of sacrifices a matter of name. The last period, "as I have said", fixes the precept's scope: it holds for whoever would order an absolute power by the path of a republic or a kingdom; whoever would make the absolute power the authors call tyranny must renew every thing, as the following chapter will say.

Cross-reference. I.26, whose rubric is that a new prince must make every thing new in a city or province taken by him, is the next chapter in this edition, and Guicciardini answers it too.

Text. The 1554 edition drops the words between the first assoluta and the second, so that the sentence runs on without the contrast between republic or kingdom and tyranny.

a volere che sia accetto. "If he wishes it to be accepted": the infinitive a volere is conditional. accetto, accepted, of the new state.

è necessitato a ritenere. "Is necessitated to retain": necessitato, the participle of necessity, kept apart from è necessario, "it is necessary", and from bisogna, "it is required" (D260).

l'ombra almanco de' modi antichi. "At least the shadow of the ancient ways": almanco sits after l'ombra in the Italian and applies to it; the English puts "at least" before "the shadow", where its scope is the same.

"Having created in place of one king the two consuls": the numerals are Machiavelli's point, one king, two consuls, and the twelve lictors that one king had; the Italian order is kept for that contrast (D263). creare, create, of magistrates, as FG I.3 (D89).

Livy (2.1.8) has only one of the first consuls hold the fasces, Brutus first by his colleague's leave, so that the terror should not seem doubled; Machiavelli gives the twelve lictors and the reason in his own terms.

Considerazione sul capitolo XXV

Chi vuole riformare uno stato antico in una città libera, ritenga almeno l'ombra de' modi antichi.

Whoever wishes to reform an ancient state in a free city, let him retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways. Canestrini's setting of the chapter title, in lower case, with stato and città uncapitalised; the stop is faint. It is heading apparatus, not Guicciardini's (D32).

Italian · 1857
English
1.25.01
La c[o]nclusione del Discorso è più necessaria a chi non muta spezie di governo, ma lo riforma, verbi gratia a chi vuole introdurre nuovi ordini in una città libera, che a chi muta spezie di governo, perché se di uno regno io introduco una libertà, come feciono e' Romani, essendo già nella opinione degli uomini che quello vivere non sia buono, non accade conservare sì esattamente gli ordini antichi. E lo esemplo de' littori e del re Sacrificulo non sono di molto momento, perché nell'uno s'ebbe rispetto alla superstizione che potevano avere gli uomini nella religione; nell'altro non sarebbe stato tollerabile che mutando la potestà regia come troppa, si armassino e' consuli con insegne di maggiore potestà.
The conclusion of the Discourse is more necessary to whoever does not change the species of government but reforms it, verbi gratia to whoever wishes to introduce new orders in a free city, than to whoever changes the species of government, because if out of a kingdom I introduce a liberty, as the Romans did, it being already in the opinion of men that that living is not good, there is no call to conserve the ancient orders so exactly. And the example of the lictors and of the king sacrificulus are not of much moment, because in the one respect was had to the superstition which men could have in religion; in the other it would not have been tolerable that, changing the royal power as too much, the consuls should be armed with insignia of greater power.
Commentary · 5

Argument. A comparative, then a reclassification, then substitute reasons. Guicciardini does not deny the conclusion of the Discourse: he says it is more necessary for whoever reforms a government without changing its species, for example by introducing new orders in a free city, than for whoever changes the species. The reason is men's opinion. If out of a kingdom one introduces a liberty, as the Romans did, men already hold that the old living is not good, and there is no call to conserve the old orders so exactly.

Note. Then Machiavelli's two Roman examples, which he finds not of much moment. He does not call them false; he gives each another reason. In the king of sacrifices, respect was had to the superstition men could have in religion. The lictors were limited because, the royal power having been changed as too great, it would not have been tolerable to arm the consuls with insignia of greater power. Neither is Machiavelli's reason, the keeping of the shadow of the old ways.

Cross-reference. The reply moves Machiavelli's own case into the class where the rule matters less. Machiavelli applies the precept to whoever would bring a city from an ancient living to "a new and free living" Machiavelli .03, and proves it by Rome after the kings Machiavelli .02: exactly the change of species from kingdom to liberty. Guicciardini's example, in una città libera, is the rubric's own case: a city already free, reformed within its form.

Cross-reference. On the lictors the two authors address the same limit from different sides. Machiavelli says the consuls were held to twelve "so as not to pass the number" of the kings' attendants Machiavelli .02, and reads it as keeping the shadow of the old; Guicciardini says nothing of the number: after a royal power rejected as too great, greater insignia would not have been borne. On the king of sacrifices, Machiavelli's reason is the people's desire for the kings; Guicciardini's is superstition; Machiavelli gives no religious reason.

History. Livy's account (2.1.8; 2.2.1-2) has both readings in view: the fasces were not held by both consuls at once, so that the terror should not seem doubled, near Guicciardini's reason for the lictors; the king of sacrifices was created for rites the kings had performed, so that the kings should nowhere be missed, where the occasion is religious and the motive is Machiavelli's Machiavelli .02. Neither author cites Livy here.

Notes

La c[o]nclusione del Discorso. "The conclusion of the Discourse": il Discorso, Machiavelli's chapter, as at FG I.23 and I.24. Canestrini prints canclusione, which both cold readers read and which is not a word; emended from the 1933 edition (D267; FG-CONS-I25-D001).

è più necessaria a chi non muta spezie di governo, ma lo riforma ... che a chi muta spezie di governo. "Is more necessary to whoever does not change the species of government but reforms it ... than to whoever changes the species of government": a comparative. Guicciardini does not deny Machiavelli's conclusion; he ranks the cases it fits. Machiavelli applies the rule to exactly the case Guicciardini ranks lower, tutti coloro che vogliono scancellare uno antico vivere in una Città, e ridurla ad uno vivere nuovo e libero (Machiavelli .03), and keeps the renewal of every thing for tyranny alone; Guicciardini's in una città libera takes the rubric's own case as the one the conclusion fits. spezie, species (glossary); riformare, reform, the rubric's verb, now set against mutare, change. The readers split on the letter before chi muta (A a, B n); ruled a (D268; FG-CONS-I25-D002).

verbi gratia. Latin, "for example", kept in italic, as FG I.23.

introdurre nuovi ordini. "To introduce new orders": ordine, order (glossary), as Machiavelli's ordini nuovi (Machiavelli .01).

se di uno regno io introduco una libertà. "If out of a kingdom I introduce a liberty": the first person is Guicciardini's hypothetical founder, not a claim about himself. libertà, liberty (glossary). The readers split on uno (B ano); ruled uno (D268; FG-CONS-I25-D003).

come feciono e' Romani. "As the Romans did": the expulsion of the kings, Machiavelli's own example, which Guicciardini reclassifies as a change of species, not a reform.

essendo già nella opinione degli uomini che quello vivere non sia buono. "It being already in the opinion of men that that living is not good": opinione, opinion (D02); vivere, living, as Machiavelli's vivere libero (.02) and uno antico vivere (.03). The subjunctive sia is that of the reported opinion.

non accade conservare sì esattamente gli ordini antichi. "There is no call to conserve the ancient orders so exactly": non accade, impersonal; accadere is "happen" elsewhere (FG I.23), but "it does not happen to conserve" is not English, and "occasion" is occasione's. conservare, conserve (D220). The claim is of degree, sì esattamente, not that the ancient orders may be thrown away.

E lo esemplo de' littori e del re Sacrificulo non sono di molto momento. "And the example of the lictors and of the king sacrificulus are not of much moment": the singular esemplo takes the plural sono, two examples counted as one; the English keeps the disagreement rather than correct it: kept, not a misprint. di molto momento, of much moment (glossary, I.8). re Sacrificulo, as Machiavelli's Re sacrificolo (Machiavelli .02), in Guicciardini's Latin form.

nell'uno s'ebbe rispetto alla superstizione. "In the one respect was had to the superstition": l'uno is the second-named, the king of sacrifices; l'altro the lictors. The order is crossed against the order of naming. avere rispetto a, have respect to (glossary). Guicciardini gives the king of sacrifices a religious reason, where Machiavelli gave a political one (the people should not miss the kings); he calls it superstition, Machiavelli did not.

che potevano avere gli uomini nella religione. "Which men could have in religion": religione, religion (glossary).

nell'altro non sarebbe stato tollerabile. "In the other it would not have been tolerable": the lictors. Machiavelli too says the number was held per non passare that of the kings (Machiavelli .02); Guicciardini reads the same limit differently: not as keeping the shadow of the old, but because, the kingship having been changed as too great, insignia of greater power would not have been borne.

mutando la potestà regia come troppa. "Changing the royal power as too much": the gerund has no expressed subject, and the English leaves it so; the Romans changed the kingship because its power was too great. mutare, change, the reply's verb (muta twice in the first period). potestà, power (D78). Machiavelli's one potestà is the tyrant's, set against potenza, ordered power (Machiavelli .03); Guicciardini uses potestà twice, of royal and of consular power. The collapse in English hides that; the collapse is kept by ruling (D278).

si armassino e' consuli con insegne di maggiore potestà. "The consuls should be armed with insignia of greater power": si passive. What this is

A free, openly licensed, bilingual edition of Guicciardini's 39 Considerazioni and the Discorsi chapters they answer — 78 text units, Italian and English facing, every reading traceable to a named public-domain witness.

It is a reading edition with a transparent apparatus, not a text of record. It does not adjudicate the critical text against Bausi 2001 or the manuscript tradition, and does not claim to.

On the title

Machiavelli had been dead two years when Guicciardini wrote; the Discorsi were unpublished, and the Considerazioni stayed unprinted until 1857. Machiavelli never answers.

“Conversation” is a quotation, not a description. It invokes Machiavelli's letter to Francesco Vettori of 10 December 1513: at evening he puts on royal and curial garments (panni reali e curiali), enters the ancient courts of ancient men (le antiche corti degli antichi uomini), asks them the reason for their actions, and quelli per loro umanità mi rispondono, “they, for their humanity, answer me”. Guicciardini does to Machiavelli what Machiavelli did to Livy — and is denied the reply Machiavelli claimed. The letter is quoted from the 1813 Opere, vol. 8, p. 96, read twice from the page image.

Method

No image-derived reading enters the text on a single reading. Each unit is transcribed twice, independently, by readers who have not seen each other's work; disagreements are adjudicated against the page images and recorded.

Existing English translations of the Considerazioni — Grayson (1965), Atkinson & Sices (2002) — are never used as translation inputs.
