A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 34 of 39

Weak states will always be ambiguous in resolving themselves, and always slow deliberations are harmful Discourses II.15, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that weak states will always be ambiguous in resolving and that slow deliberations are always harmful, since slowness helps no one and harms oneself; it comes from weakness of spirit and forces or from the malice of those who deliberate. Guicciardini finds two causes of ambiguity: weakness of prudence and mind, which he believes is what the chapter meant by weak states, though weak forces can in part add to irresolution; and, in republics alone, varied opinions, from malice or honest difference. He grants that suspension is commonly harmful, its occasional use more by chance than otherwise. But neutrality and delay can come from resolution: neutrality is then useful or harmful by the case, and deliberate delay can be laudable; delay from irresolution is always to be condemned.

Capitolo XV

Discorsi II.15

Gli Stati deboli sempre fieno ambigui nel risolversi, e sempre le deliberazioni lente sono nocive.

Weak states will always be ambiguous in resolving themselves, and always slow deliberations are harmful.

English

I Latini inteso questo, ed avendo coscienza di molte cose fatte contro alla voglia de' Romani, fecero consiglio per ordinare chi dovesse ire a Roma, e dargli commissione di quello ch'egli avesse a dire. Facile erit, explicatis consiliis, accomodare rebus verba.
In this same matter, and in these same beginnings of war between the Latins and the Romans, one can note that in every consultation it is good to come to the individual [point] of that which is to be deliberated, and not to stand always in the ambiguous, nor upon the uncertain of the thing. Which is seen manifest in the consultation that the Latins made when they were thinking to alienate themselves from the Romans. Because, the Romans having had a presentiment of this bad humour that had entered into the Latin peoples, to certify themselves of the thing, and to see whether they could, without putting hand to arms, regain those peoples, made them understand that they send to Rome eight citizens, because they were to consult with them. The Latins, having understood this, and having conscience of many things done against the will of the Romans, made council to order who must go to Rome, and to give him commission of that which he was to say. And, being in the council on this dispute, Annius, their praetor, said these words. Ad summam rerum nostrarum pertinere arbitror, ut cogitetis magis, quid agendum nobis, quam quid loquendum sit. Facile erit, explicatis consiliis, accomodare rebus verba.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The chapter stays with the beginnings of the Latin war that II.13 and II.14 treated, and draws from them a rule for counsel: in every consultation it is good to come to the individual point of what is to be deliberated, and not to stand always in the ambiguous or on the uncertain of the thing. The Latins' consultation shows it. The Romans, sensing the Latins' bad humour and wishing to regain them without arms if they could, asked for eight citizens to consult with; the Latins, conscious of much done against Rome's will, met in council to choose who must go and settle what they were to say, and Annius, their praetor, told them to think more of what was to be done than of what was to be said: once counsels are unfolded, words are easily fitted to things.

History. Livy tells of the Roman summons of the leading Latins, and of Annius' speech in the Latin council, at 8.3-8.4 (the editor's references).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not mention the Latin council. His reply opens on the word this paragraph sets against coming to the point, the ambiguity of deliberations Guicciardini .01.

These words are without doubt most true, and must be relished by every prince and by every republic; because in the ambiguity and in the incertitude of that which one wishes to do, one does not know how to accommodate the words; but the spirit once fixed, and deliberated what is to be executed, it is an easy thing to find the words for it. I have noted this part the more willingly, inasmuch as I have many times known such ambiguity to have harmed public business, with damage and with shame to our republic. Commentary · 2

Argument. Machiavelli calls Annius' words most true, words that must be relished by every prince and every republic: in ambiguity and incertitude about what one wishes to do, one does not know how to accommodate the words; once the spirit is fixed and the thing to be executed deliberated, it is easy to find them. He has noted this part the more willingly because he has many times known such ambiguity harm public business, with damage and shame to "our republic", Florence. And it will always happen, in doubtful courses and where spirit is required to deliberate them, that this ambiguity arises when they are to be counselled and deliberated by weak men. "Weak", the rubric's word, first appears in the body of the chapter of men; the next paragraph gives weakness of spirit and of forces Machiavelli .03.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini takes the weakness to be of prudence and mind, not of forces, and believes that is what the Discourse meant, though weakness of forces, he allows, can in part increase irresolution Guicciardini .01. Machiavelli's "spirit" is not Guicciardini's "mind", and Machiavelli keeps forces beside it.

2.15.037 notes1 variant
Non sono meno nocive ancora le deliberazioni lente e tarde, che ambigue, massime quelle che si hanno a deliberare in favore di alcuno amico; perché con la lentezza loro non si ajuta persona, e nuocesi a sé medesimo. Queste deliberazioni così fatte procedono o da debolezza di animo e di forze, o da malignità di coloro che hanno a deliberare; i quali mossi dalla passione propria di volere rovinare lo Stato, o adempire qualche suo desiderio, non lasciano seguire la deliberazione, ma la impediscono e l'attraversano. Perché i buoni Cittadini, ancora che veggano una foga popolare voltarsi alla parte perniciosa, mai impediranno il deliberare, massime di quelle cose che non aspettano tempo.
No less harmful also are slow and tardy deliberations than ambiguous [ones], above all those that are to be deliberated in favour of some friend; because with their slowness one aids no person, and one harms oneself. These deliberations so made proceed either from weakness of spirit and of forces, or from the malignity of those who are to deliberate; who, moved by their own passion of wishing to ruin the state, or to fulfil some desire of theirs, do not let the deliberation follow, but impede it and cross it. Because good citizens, even though they see a popular rush turning toward the pernicious side, will never impede deliberating, above all on those things that do not wait for time.
Commentary · 2

Argument. Slow and tardy deliberations are no less harmful than ambiguous ones, above all those to be taken in favour of a friend: slowness aids no one and harms oneself. Machiavelli gives them two sources, weakness of spirit and of forces, or the malignity of those who are to deliberate, who, from a passion to ruin the state or to fulfil a desire of their own, impede and cross the deliberation. Good citizens, even when they see a popular rush turning to the pernicious side, will never impede deliberating, above all on things that do not wait for time. The last sentence is the ground of the charge of malignity: obstruction is what good citizens do not do.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini keeps malignity as one ground of varied opinion in republics, beside a difference of judgments without malignity, as happens often even among the prudent Guicciardini .01; he does not take up the friend in need, and he answers slowness in the Lavinians' case and directly at the end, by distinguishing tardiness from irresolution from tardiness done deliberately.

2.15.048 notes1 variant
Morto che fu Girolamo Tiranno in Siracusa, essendo la guerra grande tra i Cartaginesi e i Romani, vennero i Siracusani in disputa se dovevano seguire l'amicizia romana o la cartaginese. E tanto era l'ardor delle parti, che la cosa stava ambigua, né se ne prendeva alcun partito, infino a tanto che Apollonide, uno dei primi in Siracusa, con una sua Orazione piena di prudenza mostrò, come non era da biasimare chi teneva l'opinione di aderirsi ai Romani, né quelli che volevano seguire la parte cartaginese; ma che era ben da detestare quella ambiguità e tardità di pigliare il partito, perché vedeva al tutto in tale ambiguità la rovina della Repubblica; ma preso che si fusse il partito, qualunque e' si fusse, si poteva sperare qualche bene. Hieronymus the tyrant being dead in Syracuse, and the war being great between the Carthaginians and the Romans, the Syracusans came into dispute whether they must follow the Roman friendship or the Carthaginian. And so great was the ardour of the parties that the thing stood ambiguous, nor was any course taken on it, until Apollonides, one of the first [men] in Syracuse, with an oration of his full of prudence, showed that he who held the opinion of adhering to the Romans was not to be blamed, nor those who wished to follow the Carthaginian party; but that that ambiguity and tardiness in taking the course was indeed to be detested, because he saw in such ambiguity altogether the ruin of the republic; but, the course once taken, whatever it might be, one could hope for some good. Commentary · 3

Argument. The first example. After the death of Hieronymus, with the war great between the Carthaginians and the Romans, the Syracusans were divided whether to follow Rome or Carthage, and the ardour of the parties left the thing ambiguous and no course taken, until Apollonides showed that neither party was to be blamed, but the ambiguity and tardiness in taking a course was to be detested, since he saw in such ambiguity altogether the ruin of the republic, while any course taken offered some hope of good. Machiavelli judges that Livy could not show more than he does here the damage that "standing suspended" draws behind it.

History. Hieronymus was killed in 214 BC; the debate in the Syracusan assembly and Apollonides' speech are in Livy 24.28 (the editor's references). Machiavelli gives the gist of the speech, not its words.

Cross-reference. "Standing suspended" is the phrase Guicciardini turns into "stand suspended" and the noun "suspension": "these suspensions are commonly pernicious, because while you stand suspended you cannot provide for either the one case or the other" Guicciardini .01.

Argument. The second example, from the same Latin war. The Lavinians, asked by the Latins for aid against Rome, deferred so long that their troops were just out of the gate when news came of the Latin rout; their praetor Milionius said that this little of the path would cost them much with the Roman people. Cross-reference. Guicciardini grants that suspensions are commonly pernicious and takes up the Lavinians inside that concession: if suspensions are sometimes useful, as they would have been to the Lavinians had they delayed three or four days more, that utility comes rather by chance than otherwise Guicciardini .01.

And if the Florentines had noted this text, they would not have had with the French either so many damages or so many vexations as they had in the passage that King Louis XII of France made into Italy against Lodovico, Duke of Milan. Because, the King treating of such a passage, [he] sought the Florentines for accord, and the orators who were near the King came to [an] accord with him that they stand neutral, and that the King, coming into Italy, was to maintain them in the state and receive them into protection, and [he] gave the city a month's time to ratify it. Commentary · 3

Argument. Machiavelli turns to Florence. Had the Florentines noted this text, they would have been spared many damages and vexations at the passage of Louis XII against Lodovico of Milan. Louis sought an accord; the Florentine orators agreed with him that Florence stand neutral and that he was to maintain the city in its state and take it into protection, with a month to ratify. Whoever, through little prudence, favoured Lodovico deferred the ratification until Louis was on the point of victory, and then he refused it, as one who knew the Florentines had come to his friendship forced, not of their will. It cost Florence much money, and it was about to lose its state, as later, another time, befell it for a similar cause. The course was the more damnable because it did not even serve Lodovico (ancora, "even"; the editor's reading, "yet" being possible), who, had he won, would have shown many more signs of enmity to the Florentines than the King did. In the editor's reading, the case shows the rubric's second half, harmful slowness, which .07 assigns to "this weakness"; the delay is laid on whoever favoured Lodovico, not on the city as a whole.

History. Louis XII's descent on Milan was in 1499; Florence's agreement with him and its delayed ratification belong to the same year, when Machiavelli was secretary of the Second Chancery (the editor's account). The "other time" is very probably 1512 (the editor's identification): the republic, which had not joined the Holy League, fell to the League's Spanish army, and the Medici returned. Machiavelli does not name it.

Cross-reference. In the editor's reading, this neutrality is a case for Guicciardini's distinction: standing neutral can also proceed from resolution, not from suspension; from suspension it is reprehensible, from resolution it can be both useful and pernicious, according to the quality of the cases, which he says is not now his matter Guicciardini .01. He does not name the Florentine case.

Argument. Machiavelli acknowledges that it has been discoursed in another chapter of the ill that arises to republics from this weakness, and repeats it because a new accident gave him occasion, and because it seems to him above all a matter to be noted by republics similar to ours. "This weakness" brings the last paragraph back to the rubric's weak states.

Notes

¶ 01 In questa medesima materia, e in questi medesimi principj di guerra. "In this same matter, and in these same beginnings of war": materia, matter, again at the close (.07); principio, beginning (glossary), the outbreak of the Latin war, which II.13 and II.14 have been treating. The chapter continues II.14 from Livy's eighth book.

¶ 01 si può notare, come in ogni consulta è bene venire allo individuo di quello che si ha a deliberare. "One can note that in every consultation it is good to come to the individual [point] of that which is to be deliberated": come after a verb of noting, "that" (D334), as at mostrò, come (.04); individuo, the indivisible, the particular in which a question can be settled, kept as the cognate with the supplied noun, since "the individual" alone would read as a person. The phrase is the chapter's positive term; everything after it is its opposite. avere a destinative, "is to be" (D261).

¶ 01 non stare sempre in ambiguo, né in su lo incerto della cosa. "Not to stand always in the ambiguous, nor upon the uncertain of the thing": two adjectives used as nouns, ambiguo and incerto, kept as English substantives. stare, stand, the verb of la cosa stava ambigua (.04), lo stare sospeso "standing suspended" (.04) and stessero neutrali (.06), and of Guicciardini's stai sospeso and lo stare neutrale (Guicciardini .01): one English verb for the posture throughout.

¶ 01 quando e' pensavano alienarsi da' Romani. "When they were thinking to alienate themselves from the Romans": e', they, the old subject pronoun.

¶ 01 avendo presentito questo cattivo umore. "Having had a presentiment of this bad humour": presentire, to sense beforehand; cattivo, bad (D03); umore, humour (glossary). The participle has the Romans as its subject; the English names them first.

¶ 01 fecero loro intendere, come e' mandassero a Roma otto cittadini, perché avevano a consultare con loro. "Made them understand that they send to Rome eight citizens, because they were to consult with them": the reported request without "should" (D348, D445); avere a, were to (D261); consultare, consult, with consulta, consultation.

¶ 01 avendo coscienza di molte cose fatte contro alla voglia de' Romani. "Having conscience of many things done against the will of the Romans": coscienza, conscience, in its old sense of inward knowledge, kept as the cognate because the knowledge is of their own wrongs and the facing Italian shows the word; voglia, will, in this construction (D240).

¶ 01 fecero consiglio per ordinare chi dovesse ire a Roma. "Made council to order who must go to Rome": fare consiglio, make council, the Latins' council in session, D452's Consiglio, council, extended to the sitting; ordinare, order (glossary); dovere, must (D274), as dovevano (.04); ire, go.

¶ 01 E stando nel consiglio di questa disputa. "And, being in the council on this dispute": the gerund has the Latins as its subject. The 1554 reads in questa disputa (NM-DISC-II15-D001).

¶ 01 Annio loro Pretore. "Annius, their praetor": names.md, as II.13 and II.14.

¶ 01 Ad summam rerum nostrarum ... accomodare rebus verba. Livy 8.4.1 (the opening of Annius' speech; the editor's reference): "I judge that it pertains to the sum of our affairs that you think more what is to be done by us than what is to be said. "In the ambiguity and in the incertitude of that which one wishes to do": incertitudine, incertitude, kept apart from incerto (.01); altri, one, the indefinite pronoun: the uncertainty is of one's own purpose. non si sanno accomodare, "one does not know how to accommodate", sapere with the infinitive.

"I have noted this part the more willingly, inasmuch as I have many times known": quanto, inasmuch as, after the comparative; conoscere, know (D374). The first person of Machiavelli's own experience, which .06 makes concrete.

¶ 02 tale ambiguità avere nociuto alle pubbliche azioni, con danno e con vergogna della Repubblica nostra. "Such ambiguity to have harmed public business, with damage and with shame to our republic": azioni publiche, public business (D389); danno, damage (D106); vergogna, shame; Repubblica nostra, Florence, as the close's simili alla nostra.

"And it will always ever happen ... where spirit is required to deliberate them": sempre mai, always ever, the doubled adverb kept; bisogna, it is required (D260; see the note on bisogni at Machiavelli I.2), here subjunctive; partiti dubbj, doubtful courses (partito, course, D236).

"No less harmful also are slow and tardy deliberations than ambiguous [ones]": the fronted predicate kept, as a contrast answering the ambiguity just treated (D263); ancora, also; tardo, tardy, with tardità, tardiness (.04), and Guicciardini's tardità (Guicciardini .01); the bracket supplies the noun the comparison leaves to che ambigue (D53).

"Proceed either from weakness of spirit and of forces, or from the malignity of those who are to deliberate": debolezza, weakness, with debole; forze, forces (glossary); malignità, malignity (glossary). Guicciardini takes the two sources, weakness and malignity, and redistributes them (Guicciardini .01). The 1554 reads procedano (NM-DISC-II15-D002).

Considerazione sul capitolo XV

Considerazioni II.15

Gli Stati deboli sempre fieno ambigui nel risolversi e sempre le deliberazioni lente sono nocive

Weak states will always be ambiguous in resolving themselves and always slow deliberations are harmful

Italian · 1857
English
2.15.0122 notes1 variant
Da due cagioni procedono le ambiguità delle deliberazioni: l'una da debolezza di quelli che hanno a risolvere, non dico debolezza di forze e di potenza, ma debolezza di prudenza e di ingegno, e questa cagione può cadere così in uno principe come in una republica; e credo che quando il Discorso disse gli Stati deboli, intese deboli di prudenza, benché la debolezza delle forze può in parte accrescere la irresoluzione; perché communemente e' partiti che hanno a pigliare gli Stati deboli, sono communemente più pieni di difficultà e di periculi. L'altra cagione, che è propria delle republiche, è quando sono più uomini che hanno a resolvere, e tra questi sono le opinioni varie; il che può procedere o da malignità, perché abbino diversi fini, o pure sanza malignità, perché e' giudicii degli uomini non si conformino, come accade spesso etiam tra prudenti. E è vero che queste sospensioni communemente sono perniziose, perché mentre stai sospeso non puoi provedere né all'uno caso né all'altro; e se qualche volta sono utili, come sarebbe accaduto a' Lavinii, e' quali se fussino tardati ancora tre o quattro dì più a resolversi, non arebbono patito pena di quello poco viaggio, nondimeno questa è una utilità che risulta più presto per caso che altrimenti. La suspensione è adunque da aborrire, la resoluzione sommamente da laudare, ma s'ha da avvertire che lo stare neutrale può anche procedere per resoluzione, non per suspensione; nel secondo caso la neutralità è reprensibile, nel primo può essere e utile e perniziosa, secondo la qualità de' casi, di che trattare non è ora materia nostra. Il medesimo dico del differire qualche altra azione o esecuzione; ché se la tardità procede da irresoluzione è sempre dannabile, ma se si fa deliberatamente può essere laudabile.
From two causes the ambiguities of deliberations proceed: the one from weakness of those who are to resolve, I do not say weakness of forces and of power, but weakness of prudence and of mind, and this cause can fall as well in a prince as in a republic; and I believe that when the Discourse said weak states, it understood weak in prudence, albeit weakness of forces can in part increase irresolution; because commonly the courses that weak states are to take are commonly more full of difficulties and of dangers. The other cause, which is proper to republics, is when there are several men who are to resolve, and among these the opinions are various; which can proceed either from malignity, because they have diverse ends, or else without malignity, because the judgments of men do not conform [with one another], as happens often even among prudent [men]. And it is true that these suspensions are commonly pernicious, because while you stand suspended you cannot provide for either the one case or the other; and if sometimes they are useful, as would have happened to the Lavinians, who, if they had delayed yet three or four days more in resolving themselves, would not have suffered penalty for that little journey, nevertheless this is a utility that results rather by chance than otherwise. Suspension is then to be abhorred, resolution supremely to be praised, but one has to take heed that standing neutral can also proceed from resolution, not from suspension; in the second case neutrality is reprehensible, in the first it can be both useful and pernicious, according to the quality of the cases, of which to treat is not now our matter. I say the same of deferring some other action or execution; for if the tardiness proceeds from irresolution it is always damnable, but if it is done deliberately it can be laudable.
Commentary · 4

Argument. The reply is one paragraph and works by division. It begins not with a judgment of the Discourse but with an account of where the ambiguities of deliberations come from, and there are two causes. The first is the weakness of those who are to resolve: not weakness of forces and power, Guicciardini says, but of prudence and mind; it can fall as well in a prince as in a republic; and he believes that when the Discourse said "weak states" it understood weak in prudence. He allows, with benché, that weakness of forces can in part increase irresolution, because the courses weak states are to take are commonly fuller of difficulties and dangers. The second cause is proper to republics: when several men are to resolve and their opinions vary; and the variety can proceed either from malignity, because they have diverse ends, or else without malignity, because men's judgments do not conform with one another, as happens often even among the prudent.

Note. Then the judgment, in two steps. "It is true" that these suspensions are commonly pernicious, since while you stand suspended you can provide for neither case; and if they are sometimes useful, as they would have been to the Lavinians had they delayed three or four days more, that utility comes rather by chance than otherwise. Suspension is then to be abhorred, resolution supremely praised, "but" one has to take heed that standing neutral can also proceed from resolution, not from suspension: from suspension it is reprehensible, from resolution it can be both useful and pernicious according to the quality of the cases, a question he says is not now his matter. He says the same of deferring some other action or execution: tardiness from irresolution is always damnable, tardiness done deliberately can be laudable.

Note. In the editor's reading, the reply limits the chapter's conclusion rather than contradicting it: where Machiavelli says slow deliberations are always harmful, Guicciardini says tardiness from irresolution is always damnable, and tardiness done deliberately can be laudable. Whether that contradicts the rubric's second "always" depends on whether a deliberate delay counts as a slow deliberation. The word he chooses for the good case, "deliberately", is the chapter's own word for deciding.

Cross-reference. In the editor's reading, the two causes take up Machiavelli's two sources of slow deliberations, "weakness of spirit and of forces" and "the malignity of those who are to deliberate" Machiavelli .03, applied now to ambiguity: Guicciardini moves the weakness from forces to prudence and mind, keeping a part for forces, and Machiavelli's "spirit" is not his "mind"; and he sets beside malignity a difference of judgments without it. "Standing suspended" is Machiavelli's phrase for what Livy shows the damage of at Syracuse Machiavelli .04, made by Guicciardini into "stand suspended" and the noun "suspension". The Lavinians are Machiavelli's second example Machiavelli .05, taken up inside Guicciardini's concession; his "little journey" answers Milionius' "this little of the path". Standing neutral is what the Florentine orators agreed with Louis XII Machiavelli .06; in the editor's reading, Guicciardini's distinction falls across that case, the course resolved by the orators and suspended by the delay to ratify it, but he does not mention it. "Tardiness" is Machiavelli's word, beside ambiguity, for what Apollonides held was to be detested Machiavelli .04, and "damnable" his word for the Florentine course Machiavelli .06.

"The other cause, which is proper to republics, is when there are several men who are to resolve": proprio, proper, belonging to republics alone; più uomini, several men; resolvere, resolve, in Guicciardini's other spelling.

¶ 01 e tra questi sono le opinioni varie. "And among these the opinions are various": opinione, opinion (D02); vario, various.

¶ 01 il che può procedere o da malignità, perché abbino diversi fini, o pure sanza malignità, perché e' giudicii degli uomini non si conformino. "Which can proceed either from malignity, because they have diverse ends, or else without malignity, because the judgments of men do not conform [with one another]": malignità, malignity (glossary), Machiavelli's word for those who block deliberation (Machiavelli .03), here one of two grounds of variety only; o pure, or else; fine, end; giudicio, judgment; conformarsi, conform, reflexive and reciprocal, with the bracket (D53). The two perché give the two grounds; the subjunctives (abbino, conformino) are rendered as indicatives, the possibility being carried by può procedere.

¶ 01 come accade spesso etiam tra prudenti. "As happens often even among prudent [men]": accadere, happen (D298); etiam, even (latin.md; glossary, D453); prudenti, prudent, the adjective as a noun, with "men" supplied (D53).

¶ 01 E è vero che queste sospensioni communemente sono perniziose. "And it is true that these suspensions are commonly pernicious": E è, as printed (the 1933 edition Ed è); sospensione, suspension, Guicciardini's noun for Machiavelli's stare sospeso (Machiavelli .04) and for both his ambiguity and his slowness: the reply's key word, spelled sospensioni here and suspensione twice below, one word; pernizioso, pernicious (glossary), answering Machiavelli's nocive, harmful (rubric, .03), in a stronger word (his perniciosa, .03, is of the side a popular rush turns to). è vero che, it is true that, opens a concession that nondimeno will limit.

¶ 01 perché mentre stai sospeso non puoi provedere né all'uno caso né all'altro. "Because while you stand suspended you cannot provide for either the one case or the other": stare sospeso, stand suspended, Machiavelli's phrase (Machiavelli .04) turned to the second person; provedere, provide (glossary), here with a, for; caso, case.

¶ 01 e se qualche volta sono utili, come sarebbe accaduto a' Lavinii. "And if sometimes they are useful, as would have happened to the Lavinians": qualche volta, sometimes (D431); utile, useful (the adjective, D135); Lavinii, the Lavinians, Machiavelli's Lavinj (Machiavelli .05; names.md).

¶ 01 e' quali se fussino tardati ancora tre o quattro dì più a resolversi, non arebbono patito pena di quello poco viaggio. "Who, if they had delayed yet three or four days more in resolving themselves, would not have suffered penalty for that little journey": tardare, delay, the root of tardità; ancora, yet; resolversi, resolve themselves, the rubric's reflexive; patire pena, suffer penalty; viaggio, journey, answering Milionius' Questo poco della via (Machiavelli .05). The editor's reading: three or four days more, and the news of the rout would have come before they marched.

¶ 01 nondimeno questa è una utilità che risulta più presto per caso che altrimenti. "Nevertheless this is a utility that results rather by chance than otherwise": nondimeno, nevertheless (glossary), limiting the concession; utilità, utility (D472), kept apart from the adjective utile, useful (D135), and the noun utile, advantage (D283); risultare, result; più presto ... che, rather ... than (D135); per caso, by chance, a sense split from caso, case (D472).

¶ 01 La suspensione è adunque da aborrire, la resoluzione sommamente da laudare. "Suspension is then to be abhorred, resolution supremely to be praised": adunque, then (D74), the conclusion drawn from what precedes; the second member without a verb, kept so; aborrire, abhor; sommamente, supremely; laudare, praise (D422).

¶ 01 ma s'ha da avvertire che lo stare neutrale può anche procedere per resoluzione, non per suspensione. "But one has to take heed that standing neutral can also proceed from resolution, not from suspension": the comma before ma kept, so that the qualification stays in the movement of the conclusion; avere da, has to (D261, the sense necessity); avvertire, take heed; stare neutrale, standing neutral, the orators' accord that the Florentines stessero neutrali, "stand neutral" (Machiavelli .06), with stare held; per, from, of the source.

¶ 01 nel secondo caso la neutralità è reprensibile, nel primo può essere e utile e perniziosa. "In the second case neutrality is reprehensible, in the first it can be both useful and pernicious": the second case is neutrality from suspension, the first neutrality from resolution: the order is reversed, as printed. reprensibile, reprehensible, a recorded split from D173's riprendere, reprensione, reprove, reproof: the adjective takes the cognate (D472).

¶ 01 secondo la qualità de' casi, di che trattare non è ora materia nostra. "According to the quality of the cases, of which to treat is not now our matter": qualità, quality (glossary); trattare, treat (glossary); materia, matter, as Machiavelli's materia (Machiavelli .01, .07). Guicciardini declines the question of neutrality here.

¶ 01 Il medesimo dico del differire qualche altra azione o esecuzione. "I say the same of deferring some other action or execution": plain order (D263); differire, defer (D334), Machiavelli's verb for the Florentines and the Lavinians; azione, action; esecuzione, execution (glossary).

¶ 01 ché se la tardità procede da irresoluzione è sempre dannabile, ma se si fa deliberatamente può essere laudabile. "For if the tardiness proceeds from irresolution it is always damnable, but if it is done deliberately it can be laudable": ché, for; tardità, tardiness, Machiavelli's word (Machiavelli .04); dannabile, damnable, Machiavelli's word for the Florentine course (Machiavelli .06); deliberatamente, deliberately, the adverb of deliberazione: delay that is itself a deliberation; laudabile, laudable (glossary). sempre answers the rubric's sempre twice over: what is always harmful is only the tardiness of irresolution. Canestrini's note on p. 69, keyed after the last word, his summary of Machiavelli's chapter, is not carried (by precedent, D463; D470). The reply ends with Canestrini's stop.
