A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 28 of 39

How the consulship and any other magistracy in Rome was given without respect of age Discourses I.60, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli argues that Rome gave the plebs the consulship regardless of birth, from necessity, since denying men the hope of reward is dangerous, and regardless of age, which Rome never regarded, since a city must be able to use a young man of proven virtù while he is young. Guicciardini answers that the Discourse forgets that Scipio Africanus the younger could not be consul until a special law lifted the age ban, and that Cicero puts the consular age ten years above thirty-three. If it was otherwise with Valerius Corvinus, it must be said, and truly, that the early republic's orders differed from later ones, as with re-election, later barred for ten years. Whether the two laws are useful to republics he leaves to another place, since the Discourse does not treat them.

Discorsi I.60

Come il Consolato e qualunque altro Magistrato in Roma si dava senza rispetto di età.

How the consulship and any other magistracy in Rome was given without respect of age.

Italian · 1824
English
1.60.016 notes2 variants

Il che si vede per il testimone di Valerio Corvino, che fu fatto Consolo nelli ventitré anni; e Valerio detto parlando ai suoi soldati disse, come il Consolato erat praemium virtutis non sanguinis. La qual cosa se fu bene considerata, o no, sarebbe da disputare assai.
It is seen by the order of the history that the Roman republic, after the consulship came into the plebs, granted it to its citizens without respect of age or of blood; even though respect of age never was in Rome, but one always went to find virtù, whether it were in [a] young [man] or in [an] old [one]. Which is seen by the witness of Valerius Corvinus, who was made consul at twenty-three years; and the said Valerius, speaking to his soldiers, said that the consulship erat praemium virtutis non sanguinis. Which thing, whether it was well considered or not, would be [matter] for much disputing.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The last chapter of Book I states a fact and then two grounds for it. The fact: once the consulship came to the plebs, Rome granted it without respect of age or of blood. Machiavelli adds at once that respect of age was never in Rome, which always went to find virtù in young or old, and gives one witness, Valerius Corvinus, consul at twenty-three, who told his soldiers that the consulship was the reward of virtù and not of blood. Whether that was well considered he calls matter for much disputing, and the rest of the chapter takes the two respects in turn, blood and then age.

History. Marcus Valerius Corvus, Machiavelli's Corvinus, was consul in the year conventionally dated 348 BC; Livy gives his age at the election as twenty-three (7.26) and has him recall it in a speech of 342 (7.40). The words to his soldiers are from his speech before the battle at Mount Gaurus in 343 (7.32), where Livy's Corvus says the consulship is now the reward not of birth but of virtus. The plebs had won access to the consulship by the Licinian-Sextian laws, conventionally dated 367 BC (Livy 6.42).

And as to blood, this was granted by necessity, and that necessity which was in Rome would be in every city that wished to make the effects that Rome made, as has been said another time; because one cannot give men hardship without reward, nor can one take away the hope of attaining the reward without danger. And therefore early it was fitting that the plebs should have hope of having the consulship, and on this hope it nourished itself [for] a time without having it. Afterwards the hope was not enough, so that it was fitting that one should come to the effect. Commentary · 2

Argument. Blood is taken first, and the ground is necessity. A city that would make the effects Rome made would meet Rome's necessity: men cannot be given hardship without reward, nor deprived of the hope of reward without danger. So it was fitting that the plebs have the hope of the consulship early; it lived on the hope for a time; then hope was not enough, and it was fitting to come to the thing itself. The necessity is conditional on the ambition: a city that employs its plebs in nothing glorious may treat it as it likes; one that would do what Rome did is not to make the distinction of blood.

Cross-reference. The choice between a republic that employs its plebs and grows, as Rome, and one that does not, as Sparta and Venice, is the argument of I.6, which the editor takes to be what Machiavelli recalls here twice (come altra volta si è detto, come altrove si disputò). Guicciardini does not answer the argument from blood.

And granted that it be so, that of time has no reply, but rather is necessary; because in choosing a young man to a degree that has need of a prudence of [an] old [man], it is fitting, the multitude having to choose him, that some most noble deed of his make him arrive at that degree. And when a young man is of so much virtù that he has made himself known in some notable thing, it would be a most damaging thing that the city could not avail itself of him then, and that it should have to wait until there had grown old with him that vigour of spirit and that promptness of which at that age his fatherland could avail itself; as Rome availed itself of Valerius Corvinus, of Scipio, of Pompey, and of many others who triumphed most young.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Age is the second respect. Granted that it be so (the editor takes this as the argument from blood), quella del tempo, which the sense requires to be the disregard of age though the Italian gives it no clear antecedent, has no reply and is necessary. Where the multitude chooses, it is fitting that some most noble deed carry a young man to a degree that needs an old man's prudence; and when a young man has made himself known in some notable thing, it would be most damaging that the city could not avail itself of him while his vigour and promptness last. Valerius Corvinus, Scipio, Pompey and many others who triumphed most young close the chapter and the book.

History. Corvus triumphed as consul over the Volsci of Satricum in 346 BC, at about twenty-five (Livy 7.27), and over the Samnites in 343 (7.38). Machiavelli's Scipio, on the editor's identification Scipio Africanus, took the Spanish command at about twenty-four (Livy 26.18) and was consul in 205 BC at about thirty, before the lex Villia annalis of 180 BC fixed ages for office (Livy 40.44); but returning from Spain in 206 he did not press for a triumph, since no one had triumphed who held command without a magistracy (28.38), and his triumph came in 201, after Zama, at about thirty-four. Pompey triumphed in his mid-twenties, in a year disputed between 81 and 79 BC, having held no magistracy, and was consul in 70 under the legal age and without the offices that normally came before it.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini sets against the chapter's mai, on the editor's reading, two things the Discourse does not remember: Scipio Aemilianus, who could be consul under age only by a particular law, and the age Cicero gives as consular Guicciardini .01. Corvinus he takes under se: if it was otherwise with him, it must be said that the orders of the early republic were other than those that arose later. He leaves to another place whether the laws of age and of interval are useful to republics, since the Discourse does not treat them, and he does not take up the argument from blood or the claim that disregard of age is necessary.

Considerazioni I.60

Come il consolato e qualunque altro magistrato in Roma si dava senza rispetto di età

How the consulship and any other magistracy in Rome was given without respect of age Canestrini's setting of the chapter title: Machiavelli's rubric with consolato, magistrato lower case and no stop. It is heading apparatus, not Guicciardini's (D32). Non si ricorda il Discorso, che Scipione Africano minore non potette essere fatto consule se per legge particulare non gli fu prima levato il divieto della età; non che Cicerone nel . . . dice, che a chi è di età di trentatré anni manca il tempo di dieci anni a essere consule; e se in Valerio Corvino fu altrimenti, bisogna dire, e così con verità, che altri furono gli ordini nel principio della republica, altri nacquono in progresso di tempo. The Discourse does not remember that Scipio Africanus the younger could not be made consul if by a particular law the ban of age was not first removed from him; nor that Cicero in the . . . says that to one who is of the age of thirty-three years there lacks the time of ten years to be consul; and if in Valerius Corvinus it was otherwise, it is required to say, and so with truth, that other were the orders in the beginning of the republic, others arose in progress of time. As it was too with the time of the magistracies, because in the beginnings there was no prohibition that the consulship could not be continued; and at least he who was consul now could within a little time be chosen consul anew, but afterwards a law was made that between one consulship and the other there should be at least an interval of ten years. Which two laws, that is, of the ban of age and of time, whether they are useful to republics or not, it will be treated in another place, because in this [place] it is not our consideration, they not being treated in the Discourse
Commentary · 3

Argument. He answers Machiavelli's fact, not his reasons. The Discourse does not remember that the younger Scipio Africanus could be consul only once a particular law had lifted the ban of age; nor that Cicero, in a work the text leaves blank, says that a man of thirty-three lacks ten years of the time to be consul. Corvinus is taken under se, neither granted nor disputed: if it was otherwise with him, it must be said, and so with truth, that the orders of the early republic were other than those that arose later. The interval between consulships is a like case: at first nothing forbade continuing in the consulship, and later a law required ten years between one consulship and the next. Whether the two laws, of age and of time, are useful to republics he leaves to another place, since the Discourse does not treat them. He does not take up the argument from blood, or Machiavelli's claim, on the editor's reading of quella del tempo, that disregard of age is necessary.

History. Scipio Aemilianus, adopted son of the elder Africanus's son, was named consul by the people for the year conventionally dated 147 BC while standing for the aedileship, and released from the laws, since by his years he could not lawfully be consul (Livy, Periochae 50, the Latin Library text, consulted 2026-11-19); he triumphed in 146, at about thirty-eight. The ten-year interval is the plebiscite Livy records in the year conventionally dated 342 BC, that no one should hold the same magistracy again within ten years (7.42). Canestrini's note guesses Cicero's De Legibus III. The editor's identification is Philippics 5.47-48 (Latin Library text, consulted 2026-11-19), from the debate of January 43 BC on honours for the young Octavian. At 5.47 Cicero says that the early Romans had no laws fixing the ages for office, which came many years later, Guicciardini's distinction of early and later orders; at 5.48 that Alexander died in his thirty-third year, ten years short of the Roman consular age, which does not match Guicciardini's thirty-three exactly, and he names the Corvini and the elder Africanus as men made consul young, which tells for Machiavelli's examples. Whether Guicciardini had the passage in mind cannot be shown.

Cross-reference. The mai that Guicciardini's two examples answer, on the editor's reading, is Machiavelli's il rispetto della età mai non fusse in Roma Machiavelli .01. His Corvinus is the age witness of Machiavelli .01, named again at Machiavelli .03; his Scipio is the younger, where Machiavelli's, among those who triumphed most young, is presumably the elder.

¶ 01 qualunque ... e così con verità. The 1857 witness reads rubric e [qualunqua/qualunque] altro magistrato (split) ... bisogna dire, e così con verità, che. The 1933 witness reads e qualunque altro magistrato ... bisogna dire, e così è con verità, che. The rubric split between the readers, two forms of one word, read with the 1933 edition (D406). così con verità, read alike by both without doubt, construes as an ellipsis and is kept against the 1933 così è (D38, D408).

¶ 01 consule; ... tempo. ... il consulato; e almanco ... consule, ma di poi. The 1857 witness reads essere consule [, / ;] e se (both doubting) ... di tempo [. / none] Come (both doubting) ... il consulato; e almanco ... consule, ma di poi. The 1933 witness reads essere consule; e se ... di tempo. Come ... el consulato, ed almanco ... consule; ma di poi. Pointing neither reader could weigh read with the 1933 edition; marks both read alike without doubt kept against it (D110).

Orthography, capitalization, word division and punctuation follow the governing witness throughout; no reading is hybridized between witnesses.

Notes

¶ 01 Non si ricorda il Discorso, che Scipione Africano minore non potette essere fatto consule se per legge particulare non gli fu prima levato il divieto della età. "The Discourse does not remember that Scipio Africanus the younger could not be made consul if by a particular law the ban of age was not first removed from him": the fronted verb normalised (D263); il Discorso, the Discourse, his name for Machiavelli's chapter (FG I.58). Scipione Africano minore, Scipio Africanus the younger, Publius Cornelius Scipio Aemilianus (names.md). The Latin Library text of the Periochae of Livy, book 50 (consulted 2026-11-19), records that he was named consul by the people while standing for the aedileship, legibus solutus, released from the laws, for the year conventionally dated 147 BC. minore distinguishes him from the elder Africanus, whom names.md takes to be Machiavelli's Scipione (Machiavelli .03). legge particulare, a particular law, made for one man; levare, remove (D131); divieto, ban, the bar on holding an office, also a term of Florentine government, kept apart from proibizione, prohibition, below. consule, consul, Guicciardini's form of Machiavelli's Consolo.

¶ 01 non che Cicerone nel . . . dice. "Not to say that Cicero in the . . . says": non che, nor that: the opening's non ... che resumed, a second thing the Discourse does not remember; not the scalar non che of NM I.16 (D226), "not to say", "let alone". Canestrini prints a row of points here with a note, the readers counting three and four; the 1933 edition prints five. Neither names the work, and the edition prints the gap as it stands (FG-CONS-I60-D001, D407). Canestrini's note guesses Cicero's De Legibus, book III, which treats the magistracies. The editor's identification is Philippics 5.47-48: the Latin Library text (thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/phil5.shtml, consulted 2026-11-19) says that Alexander of Macedon died in his thirty-third year, an age ten years short of the consular age under Roman law. Guicciardini's di età di trentatré anni does not match that count exactly. The same passage names the Corvini and the elder Africanus as men made consul young, examples that tell for Machiavelli's side; whether Guicciardini had the passage in mind cannot be shown.

¶ 01 che a chi è di età di trentatré anni manca il tempo di dieci anni a essere consule. "That to one who is of the age of thirty-three years there lacks the time of ten years to be consul": the consular age implied, on Guicciardini's figures, is forty-three. età, age, as Machiavelli's rubric.

¶ 01 e se in Valerio Corvino fu altrimenti, bisogna dire, e così con verità, che altri furono gli ordini nel principio della republica, altri nacquono in progresso di tempo. "And if in Valerius Corvinus it was otherwise, it is required to say, and so with truth, that other were the orders in the beginning of the republic, others arose in progress of time": bisogna, it is required (D260; see the note on bisogni at Machiavelli I.2). e così con verità, as Canestrini prints it, an ellipsis, left unsupplied in the English as in the Italian; it may resume dire, said so with truth. The 1933 edition reads e così è con verità, with the verb (FG-CONS-I60-D002, D408). The predicate-first altri furono is kept because the correlative altri ... altri carries the contrast of beginning and progress of time (D263, a contrast). ordini, orders (glossary); principio, beginning (glossary); nascere, arise (D04). Guicciardini takes Machiavelli's example under se, neither granting nor disputing it, and answers his mai (Machiavelli .01) by time: the orders of the beginning were other than those that arose later. The editor reads this as allowing that early Rome may have lacked the ban.

¶ 01 Come ancora fu del tempo de' magistrati, perché ne' principii non vi era proibizione che non si potessi continuare il consulato. "As it was too with the time of the magistracies, because in the beginnings there was no prohibition that the consulship could not be continued": tempo, time, here the interval between tenures, as del tempo below; Machiavelli's quella del tempo (Machiavelli .03) is age, and Guicciardini sets età and tempo apart (del divieto della età e del tempo); "time" is kept in both units; magistrati, magistracies (D93); continuare, continue, hold again without a break. The non after proibizione is the pleonastic negative of a verb of forbidding; the English keeps it as the content of the prohibition, which English can bear: there was no rule that the consulship could not be continued, so nothing forbade continuing it. chi era consule ora, he who was consul now, the moment within the past narrated; di nuovo, anew (glossary); eletto, chosen (glossary, eleggere).

¶ 01 ma di poi fu fatta una legge che tra l'uno consulato e l'altro dovessi essere almanco intervallo di dieci anni. "But afterwards a law was made that between one consulship and the other there should be at least an interval of ten years": di poi, afterwards (D187); almanco, at least (glossary), twice in the period, the first restricting, the second a minimum. Livy records a plebiscite of the year conventionally dated 342 BC that no one hold the same magistracy again within ten years (7.42).

¶ 01 Le quali due leggi, cioè del divieto della età e del tempo, se sono utili alle republiche o no, si tratterà in altro luogo, perché in questo non è nostra considerazione, non essendo trattate nel Discorso. "Which two laws, that is, of the ban of age and of time, whether they are useful to republics or not, it will be treated in another place, because in this [place] it is not our consideration, they not being treated in the Discourse": si tratterà, impersonal: the matter deferred is whether the laws are useful, and Le quali due leggi is left hanging; the English keeps the break. in altro luogo, in another place, against in questo; utili, useful; trattare, treat, twice, the second of what the Discourse treats; considerazione, consideration (glossary), the word of his title, Considerazioni. Canestrini prints no stop at the end, and none is supplied (D40).
