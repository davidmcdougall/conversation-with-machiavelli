A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 35 of 39

That acquisitions in republics not well ordered, and that do not proceed according to Roman *virtù*, are to their ruin, not to their exaltation Discourses II.19, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that acquisitions ruin republics not well ordered and not proceeding by Roman virtù, since one can acquire empire without forces: Venice and Florence grew weaker as they acquired. Guicciardini asks who doubts that the two would be weaker within small borders: dominion makes them harder to assail, richer, and able to hire foreign soldiers, better than no defence. He grants that a republic with its own arms is more powerful and gains more from acquiring, but will not grant that a disarmed one grows weaker the more it acquires, or that Venice would be safer without dominion. If so, he does not know why the Discourse speaks only of republics: an unarmed prince would grow weaker by acquiring, which reasons and experience show to be most false.

Discorsi II.19

Che gli acquisti nelle Repubbliche non bene ordinate, e che secondo la romana virtù non procedono, sono a rovina, non a esaltazione di esse.

Commentary · 3

Argument. The chapter opens on "these opinions contrary to the truth", which in the editor's reading are the opinions of the chapter before, II.18, that set cavalry above infantry. They rest on the ill examples of corrupted centuries, and they make men not think of deviating from the accustomed ways: thirty years back no Italian could have been persuaded that ten thousand foot could fight and vanquish ten thousand horse and as many foot on a plain, as the example of Novara, cited more times, shows.

History. Novara is the battle of June 1513, where Swiss infantry routed a French army with its men-at-arms (the editor's identification). "Thirty years back" sets the persuasion before the French descent of 1494, if the chapter was written about 1517, as is usually supposed (the editor's dating).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not take up infantry or cavalry; his reply begins from Machiavelli's Florence and Venice (.08) Guicciardini .01.

Commentary · 2

Argument. From infantry the argument widens: as the histories' report of infantry has been seen to be true, so all the other ancient orders should be believed true and useful. Were this believed, republics and princes would err less, resist an onset better, not hope in flight, and those who hold a civil living would direct it better, whether by expanding or by maintaining. They would believe that these paths (Rome's, in the editor's reading; see II.3-II.6) make a republic great and acquire empire: increase the city with inhabitants, make companions for themselves and not subjects, send colonies to guard acquired countries, make capital of the spoils, subdue the enemy by raids and battles and not by sieges, keep the public rich and the private poor, and maintain military exercises with supreme study.

Cross-reference. In Guicciardini's own account Florence and Venice depart from two of these orders: they have subjects, not companions, and conduct strangers in place of soldiers of their own Guicciardini .01. His public revenues greater and dominant city "richer in private" stand against "the public rich, the private poor", and his "makes more capital of acquisitions" turns "make capital of the spoils" to the acquisitions themselves (both the editor's reading).

Considerazione sul capitolo XIX

Considerazioni II.19

Che gli acquisti nelle repubbliche non bene ordinate, e che secondo la romana virtù non procedono, sono a rovina, non a esaltazione di esse

That acquisitions in republics not well ordered, and that do not proceed according to Roman *virtù*, are to their ruin, not to their exaltation

Italian · 1857
English
2.19.0117 notes3 variants
Chi dubita che la città di Firenze, che la republica di Vinegia sarebbono più deboli e di minore potenza se avessino rinchiuso il territorio loro tra piccoli confini che non sono? Avendo domato le città vicine, e allargato la loro jurisdizione, non è facile a ogni vicino assaltarle; non per ogni debole accidente si travagliano; tengono, se non viene moto grande, lo inimico fuora del tuorlo del suo Stato; non si accosta facilmente la guerra alle loro mura; lo avere molti sudditi fa in molti modi le entrate publiche maggiori, fa la città dominante in privato più ricca. Co' quali mezzi se bene non sono armate di soldati proprii, conducono de' forestieri, da' quali essere difeso è meglio che non essere difeso da alcuno. Confesso che una republica che ha armi propri[e], è più potente e fa più capitale degli acquisti, ma non confesserò già che una republica disarmata diventi più debole, quanto più acquista, né che Vinegia che ora non teme de' re né degli imperatori, se sanza dominio in terra e in mare, fussi più sicura che non è di presente. Il che se fussi vero, non so perché il Discorso si ristringa solo alle republiche, perché per le medesime cagioni uno principe che non avessi armi proprie, caverebbe degli acquisti e dell'ampliazione del dominio debolezza e non potenza, il che essere falsissimo mostrano largamente e le ragioni e la esperienza
Who doubts that the city of Florence, that the republic of Venice would be weaker and of less power if they had shut in their territory within small borders than they are? Having subdued the neighbouring cities, and enlarged their jurisdiction, it is not easy for every neighbour to assail them; they are not troubled by every weak accident; they hold, if a great motion does not come, the enemy outside the yolk of their state; war does not readily approach their walls; having many subjects makes the public revenues greater in many ways, makes the dominant city richer in private. With which means, although they are not armed with soldiers of their own, they conduct strangers, by whom to be defended is better than not to be defended by anyone. I confess that a republic that has arms of its own is more powerful and makes more capital of acquisitions, but I will not confess that a disarmed republic becomes weaker the more it acquires, nor that Venice, which now fears neither kings nor emperors, if without dominion on land and at sea, would be more secure than it is at present. Which, if it were true, I do not know why the Discourse restricts itself only to republics, because for the same causes a prince who did not have arms of his own would derive from acquisitions and from the expansion of dominion weakness and not power, which both the reasons and the experience show at large to be most false
Commentary · 5

Argument. The reply is one paragraph and does not dispute the chapter's long argument about Roman orders and the German republics. It takes the rubric's claim, that acquisitions in republics not well ordered, and not proceeding by Roman virtù, are to their ruin, at the point where Machiavelli applies it to Florence and Venice, and answers with a question: who doubts that the two would be weaker and of less power, had they shut their territory within small borders, than they are? Then the reasons, as a list without connectives: having subdued their neighbours and enlarged their jurisdiction, it is not easy for every neighbour to assail them; they are not troubled by every weak accident; if a great motion does not come, they hold the enemy outside the yolk of their state; war does not readily come to their walls; having many subjects makes the public revenues greater in many ways, and the dominant city richer in private. With these means, though not armed with soldiers of their own, they conduct strangers, and to be defended by them is better than to be defended by no one.

Note. Then the concession and its limit. "I confess" that a republic with arms of its own is more powerful and makes more capital of acquisitions; "but I will not confess" that a disarmed republic becomes weaker the more it acquires, nor that Venice, which now fears neither kings nor emperors, would be more secure without dominion on land and at sea. The last sentence turns the claim against its own restriction: if it were true, Guicciardini does not know why the Discourse restricts it to republics, since for the same causes a prince without arms of his own would derive weakness and not power from acquisitions and the expansion of dominion; and that, both the reasons and the experience show at large to be most false.

Note. The concession is narrow. In the editor's reading it is the only point at which the reply meets Machiavelli's condition, empire without forces (.08) and mercenary or auxiliary soldiers (.10); Guicciardini puts that condition as arms of one's own and a "disarmed" republic, words not in the chapter. Guicciardini does not deny that acquisition can harm. His opening question holds that dominion has made Florence and Venice stronger than small borders would have; what he will not grant is the general claim that a disarmed republic grows weaker the more it acquires. His "I do not know why" stands under the condition "if it were true": it opens a reductio, not a suspension. If disarmed republics grew weaker by acquiring, so would unarmed princes, and that consequence he calls most false. In the editor's reading, the restriction to republics is the rubric's and .04's; the chapter's general statements of the damage are put to "whoever" (.08) and "those who" avail themselves of mercenaries (.10), so the charge holds against the rubric more than against the whole chapter.

History. Venice's "now" follows the war of the League of Cambrai (1509-1517), after which it recovered most of its mainland dominion; the kings and emperors are the powers of that league, and the Considerazioni are usually dated about 1530 (all the editor's reading).

Cross-reference. The opening question answers Machiavelli's Venetians and Florentines, "much weaker" with Lombardy and Tuscany than with the sea and six miles of borders Machiavelli .08. Guicciardini's "small borders" recall both those six miles and the "few borders" of the quiet republic, which Machiavelli himself judges impossible Machiavelli .04; and his Venice "without dominion on land and at sea" answers the Venice "content with the sea" and goes beyond it, since Machiavelli takes away the land, not the sea (the editor's reading). "Subdued" is the verb of Machiavelli's Roman list ("subdue the enemy"), and "makes more capital of acquisitions" turns "make capital of the spoils" to the acquisitions themselves, in the editor's reading Machiavelli .03; so do his public revenues greater and dominant city "richer in private" stand against "the public rich, the private poor" there, and his "many subjects" against "companions and not subjects". "Soldiers of one's own" and the strangers conducted in their place answer the close, in the editor's reading, where Machiavelli speaks of those who avail themselves of soldiers either mercenary or auxiliary Machiavelli .10. "Weakness and not power", in the editor's reading, answers "empire and not forces" Machiavelli .08. Guicciardini does not take up the German republics, the leagues Machiavelli .07, or the corruption by delights at Capua Machiavelli .09, which Machiavelli gives as a damage that sometimes falls even on a well-ordered republic.
