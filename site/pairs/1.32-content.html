A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 22 of 39

A republic or a prince must not defer benefiting men until their necessities Discourses I.32, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that a republic or a prince must not wait for danger to win the people, because benefits given then are credited to the enemy, create no debt, and hasten the giver's ruin; Rome succeeded for causes that seldom recur. Guicciardini distinguishes. With new benefits in a time of necessity, it is much easier to bind closer a man ordinarily your friend, as with the Romans, than to gain one wholly your enemy, which is very hard; yet even in the easier case it is incomparably more useful to have done it before the need. In either case he does not blame the man who was imprudent not to provide beforehand if, brought to necessity, he tries this late remedy: it has little hope of helping, but no danger of harm.

Niccolò Machiavelli

answered by

NM-DISC.1.32

Capitolo XXXII

Una Repubblica o uno Principe non debbe differire a beneficare gli uomini nelle loro necessitadi.

A republic or a prince must not defer benefiting men until their necessities.

Italian · 1824
English
1.32.01
Ancora che ai Romani succedesse felicemente essere liberali al Popolo, sopravvenendo il pericolo, quando Porsena venne ad assaltare Roma per rimettere i Tarquinj, dove il Senato dubitando della Plebe che non volesse piuttosto accettare i Re che sostenere la guerra, per assicurarsene la sgravò delle gabelle del sale, e d'ogni gravezza dicendo, come i poveri assai operavano in beneficio pubblico, se ei nutrivano i loro figliuoli, e che per questo beneficio quel Popolo si esponesse a sopportare ossidione, fame, e guerra; non sia però alcuno che confidatosi in questo esempio differisca ne' tempi di pericoli a guadagnarsi il Popolo, perché mai gli riuscirà quello che riuscì ai Romani; perché l'universale giudicherà non aver quel bene da te, ma dagli avversarj tuoi; e dovendo temere che, passata la necessità, tu ritolga loro quello che hai forzatamente loro dato, non arà teco obbligo alcuno.
Even though it fell out happily for the Romans to be liberal to the people, the danger supervening, when Porsena came to assault Rome to restore the Tarquins, where the senate, doubting of the plebs whether it would wish rather to accept the kings than to sustain the war, to secure itself of it disburdened it of the gabelles on salt, and of every burden, saying that the poor worked enough for the public benefit if they nourished their children, and [even though] for this benefit that people exposed itself to bear siege, hunger, and war; let there not therefore be anyone who, having confided in this example, defers gaining the people for himself until the times of dangers, for what succeeded for the Romans will never succeed for him; for the generality will judge that it has that good not from you, but from your adversaries; and, having to fear that, the necessity past, you take back from them what you have forcedly given them, it will have no debt whatever with you.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The chapter's first period is a concession and a prohibition in one sentence. Rome, when Porsena came to restore the Tarquins, was liberal to the people in its danger, and it fell out well: the senate, doubting whether the plebs might rather accept the kings than sustain the war, relieved it of the salt gabelles and of every burden, saying that the poor did enough for the public benefit if they raised their children; and, for this benefit, the people exposed itself to bear siege, hunger and war. Even so, no one is to take this example as licence to defer gaining the people until times of danger. Two grounds follow, each opened by "for": what succeeded for the Romans will never succeed for him; and the generality will judge that it has the good not from you but from your adversaries, and, having to fear that once the necessity is past you will take back what you gave under constraint, it will have no debt with you. The period ends on the debt the benefit does not create. Midway the sentence turns from "anyone" to "him" and then to "you", and the second person holds to its end.

History. Livy tells the episode at 2.9. With Porsena approaching, the senate took the sale of salt into public hands and freed the plebs from port dues and tribute, on the ground that the poor paid enough if they raised their children (2.9.6); the concord this bought, he says, held the city together through the siege and hunger that followed (2.9.8). Machiavelli turns Livy's ground into the senate's words, and gives the reliefs in the fiscal vocabulary of his own time, the gabelle on salt and "every burden" (gravezza). Livy's salt measure was a public monopoly of its sale, not the remission of a tax.

1.32.02
E la cagione perché ai Romani tornò bene questo partito, fu perché lo Stato era nuovo, e non per ancora fermo, ed aveva veduto quel Popolo, come innanzi si erano fatte leggi in beneficio suo, come quella della appellazione alla Plebe, in modo che ei potette persuadersi che quel bene che gli era fatto, non era tanto causato dalla venuta dei nimici, quanto dalla disposizione del Senato in beneficarli; oltra di questo la memoria dei Re era fresca, da' quali erano stati in molti modi vilipesi e ingiuriati. E perché simili cagioni accaggiono rade volte, occorrerà ancora rade volte, che simili rimedj giovino.
And the cause why this course turned out well for the Romans was because the state was new, and not as yet firm, and that people had seen that laws had been made before for its benefit, such as that of the appeal to the plebs, in such a way that it could persuade itself that that good which was done to it was not so much caused by the coming of the enemies as by the disposition of the senate to benefit them; besides this, the memory of the kings was fresh, by whom they had been in many ways vilified and injured. And because like causes happen seldom, it will also occur seldom that like remedies help.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Why it worked for Rome, and why it will seldom work for anyone else. The state was new and not yet firm, and the people had already seen laws made for its benefit, the appeal to the plebs among them, so that it could persuade itself that the good was caused not so much by the coming of the enemies as by the senate's disposition to benefit it; besides this, the memory of the kings, who had vilified and injured them, was fresh. The judgment of .01 is turned round in the same words, "that good": there the generality credits it to the adversaries, here the people to the senate. The exception is closed by rarity: like causes happen seldom, so like remedies will seldom help. Machiavelli's "never" of .01, said of whoever trusts the example, has become "seldom", said of the remedy where the causes recur; the chapter does not reconcile the two.

History. The law of appeal is Livy's law of Publius Valerius Publicola, carried in the first year of the republic, which allowed a citizen to appeal to the people against a magistrate (2.8.2). Livy places it before Porsena's war, as Machiavelli's "before" requires. Machiavelli calls it an appeal to the plebs; Livy says the people.

Cross-reference. Of the advantages Machiavelli gives Rome here, a new state, a people already benefited by laws and the fresh memory of the kings, Guicciardini takes up none.

1.32.03
Però debbe qualunque tiene Stato, così Repubblica come Principe, considerare innanzi, quali tempi gli possono venire addosso contrarj, e di quali uomini nei tempi avversi si può avere di bisogno, e dipoi vivere con loro in quel modo che giudica, sopravvenendo qualunque caso, essere necessitato vivere. E quello che altrimente si governa, o Principe o Repubblica, e massime un Principe, e poi in sul fatto crede, quando il pericolo sopravviene, coi benefizj riguadagnarsi gli uomini, se ne inganna; perché non solamente non se ne assicura, ma accelera la sua rovina.
Therefore whoever holds state, a republic as well as a prince, must consider beforehand what contrary times can come upon him, and of what men in adverse times one can have need, and afterwards live with them in that way in which he judges [himself], whatever case supervening, to be necessitated to live. And that one who governs himself otherwise, either prince or republic, and above all a prince, and later on the spot believes, when the danger supervenes, [that he will] regain men for himself with benefits, deceives himself in it; for not only does he not secure himself of them, but he accelerates his ruin.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The precept is drawn from the rarity: therefore whoever holds state, republic or prince, must consider beforehand what contrary times may come upon him and of what men he may have need in adverse times, and then live with them in the way he judges he would be forced to live, whatever case should come. Then its negative case: whoever governs himself otherwise, and a prince above all, and later, on the spot, when danger comes, believes he will regain men with benefits, deceives himself; for not only does he not secure himself of them, he hastens his ruin. Machiavelli gives no reason why the prince is singled out, and none for the ruin beyond the failure itself. For the ruler who has governed himself otherwise and then trusts to benefits, the remedy that worked at Rome does not merely fail to secure the men; Machiavelli says it hastens his ruin.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini answers the last clause directly: the late remedy has little hope of helping, he says, but no danger of doing harm Guicciardini .01. He holds that, even in the easier case, it is beyond comparison more useful to have acted before the need, and divides the men to be won into friends and enemies, a distinction this chapter does not draw.

FG-CONS.1.32

Considerazione sul capitolo XXXII

Una repubblica o uno principe non debbe differire a beneficare gli uomini nelle sue necessitadi.

A republic or a prince must not defer benefiting men until their necessities. Canestrini's setting of the chapter title, in lower case and in Machiavelli's words, except that nelle loro necessitadi becomes nelle sue necessitadi. After a singular republic or prince, sue most readily means the ruler's necessities; it can also stand for loro, as Guicciardini writes it (D211), and mean the men's. "Their" keeps both. Both readers saw necessltati, a broken letter, and split between necessità and necessitadi; read necessitadi, with the 1933 edition and Machiavelli's rubric (D329; FG-CONS-I32-D001). Both doubted nella against nelle, and nelle is read, as the agreement with sue and the 1933 edition require (D330; FG-CONS-I32-D002). It is heading apparatus, not Guicciardini's (D32).

English
1.32.01
Altro è con nuovi beneficii nel tempo della necessità cercare di farsi più amico uno che per lo ordinario ti sia amico, altro è cercare di guadagnarsi uno che totalmente ti sia inimico. Nel primo è molto più facilità, come intervenne a' Romani, il secondo è difficilissimo, e non dimeno nel primo ancora è sanza comparazione più utile averlo fatto innanzi al bisogno. Ma nell'uno caso e l'altro non biasimo chi è stato imprudente a non vi provedere prima, se condotto alla necessità tenta questo remedio; il quale se bene ha poca speranza di giovare, non ha con seco periculo di nuocere.
It is one thing, with new benefits in the time of necessity, to seek to make one who by the ordinary is a friend to you more one's friend, it is another to seek to gain for oneself one who is wholly an enemy to you. In the first there is much more facility, as befell the Romans, the second is most difficult, and nonetheless in the first also it is without comparison more useful to have done it before the need. But in the one case and the other I do not blame him who has been imprudent in not providing for it earlier, if, brought to the necessity, he attempts this remedy; which, although it has little hope of helping, does not have with it danger of doing harm.
Commentary · 3

Argument. A reply of 103 words in three periods. The first divides the men Machiavelli spoke of in general by what they already are to the giver. To seek, with new benefits in a time of necessity, to make one who is ordinarily your friend more your friend is one thing; to seek to gain one who is wholly your enemy is another. The second weighs them: the first much easier, as it was for the Romans, the second most difficult; and even in the first case it is without comparison more useful to have done it before the need. That much of Machiavelli's precept he grants: providing beforehand is beyond comparison more useful, even in the easier case. He grants it as a comparison, not as a rule. The third turns: in either case he does not blame the man who has been imprudent in not providing earlier, if, brought to the necessity, he attempts this remedy, a remedy which, although it has little hope of helping, carries no danger of doing harm. He calls the man imprudent, limits the withholding of blame to that condition, and does not recommend the remedy.

Cross-reference. The last clause answers the last clause of the chapter. Machiavelli's man who has governed himself otherwise and, when danger comes, believes he can regain men with benefits "accelerates his ruin" Machiavelli .03; Guicciardini says the remedy carries no danger of doing harm. He takes up Machiavelli's own words for it, "necessity", "remedy", "help" and "need" Machiavelli .01 Machiavelli .02 Machiavelli .03, and with "little hope" concedes Machiavelli's conclusion that such remedies seldom help, without taking up the ground he gave for it, that like causes happen seldom. He does not take up the grounds Machiavelli gave in his first period for the late benefit's failure, that the generality credits it to your adversaries and, fearing it will be taken back, has no debt with you Machiavelli .01; for the ruin itself Machiavelli gives no separate reason.

Cross-reference. Machiavelli's subject was "the people" and "men" in general, and his example the Roman plebs. Guicciardini's "as befell the Romans" puts that example in his easier case, among friends, without saying why. Machiavelli had the senate doubting whether the plebs would rather accept the kings Machiavelli .01, though he then gives the people reasons to credit the senate's disposition Machiavelli .02.

Altro è con nuovi beneficii nel tempo della necessità cercare di farsi più amico uno che per lo ordinario ti sia amico, altro è cercare di guadagnarsi uno che totalmente ti sia inimico. "It is one thing, with new benefits in the time of necessity, to seek to make one who by the ordinary is a friend to you more one's friend, it is another to seek to gain for oneself one who is wholly an enemy to you": altro è ..., altro è held in one period with "it is" twice, as FG I.5 (D121). beneficii, benefits (glossary); necessità, necessity, Machiavelli's word for the time of danger (passata la necessità, Machiavelli .01); per lo ordinario, by the ordinary (D163, FG I.8), the man who is a friend in the ordinary course of things; guadagnarsi, gain for oneself, Machiavelli's verb (a guadagnarsi il Popolo). The persons shift from the impersonal (farsi, guadagnarsi) to ti, "you", as Machiavelli shifted to te (Machiavelli .01); the English keeps both. inimico, enemy; Machiavelli's form is nimico, and his enemies are Porsena's side (Machiavelli .02), where Guicciardini's is the man to be benefited. farsi is read where both readers doubted farai, which does not construe after cercare di; and sia where both doubted aia (D330; FG-CONS-I32-D002).

The distinction. Machiavelli's subject was "the people" and "men" in general; Guicciardini divides them by what they already are to the giver, friend or enemy, a distinction the chapter does not draw. He gives no reason why the Romans were the easy case.

Nel primo è molto più facilità, come intervenne a' Romani, il secondo è difficilissimo. "In the first there is much more facility, as befell the Romans, the second is most difficult": facilità, facility (glossary, FG I.28); intervenire, befall (D298); difficilissimo, the elative, "most difficult". The two members are joined by a comma, without a conjunction: the comma is the 1933 edition's, where both readers preferred a semicolon and doubted it (D110; D331), and the English keeps the light pointing (D65). Canestrini's a Romani prints no apostrophe; it is supplied as for the plural article (D35).

e non dimeno nel primo ancora è sanza comparazione più utile averlo fatto innanzi al bisogno. "And nonetheless in the first also it is without comparison more useful to have done it before the need": non dimeno, nonetheless (glossary), in two words as Canestrini prints it; ancora, also, even in the easier case; sanza comparazione, without comparison; bisogno, need, Machiavelli's avere di bisogno (Machiavelli .03); innanzi, before, as his considerare innanzi, "consider beforehand" (Machiavelli .03). Here Guicciardini agrees with Machiavelli's precept, to provide beforehand, and says so in his own degree.

Ma nell'uno caso e l'altro non biasimo chi è stato imprudente a non vi provedere prima. "But in the one case and the other I do not blame him who has been imprudent in not providing for it earlier": biasimare, blame (D173); imprudente, imprudent: the man who did not considerare innanzi (Machiavelli .03); provedere, provide; prima, earlier, kept apart from innanzi, "before". The act is a withholding of blame, not praise: the man has been imprudent, and Guicciardini says so.

se condotto alla necessità tenta questo remedio. "If, brought to the necessity, he attempts this remedy": tentare, attempt, as FG I.23; remedio, remedy, Machiavelli's simili rimedj (Machiavelli .02). The condition limits the withholding of blame to the man already brought to necessity. He does not recommend the late remedy.

il quale se bene ha poca speranza di giovare, non ha con seco periculo di nuocere. "Which, although it has little hope of helping, does not have with it danger of doing harm": se bene, although (D31); giovare, help, Machiavelli's verb (che simili rimedj giovino); nuocere, do harm (D106); periculo, danger (glossary). The landing answers Machiavelli's last clause, that the man who relies on such benefits accelera la sua rovina (Machiavelli .03): Guicciardini grants the little hope, taking up Machiavelli's rade volte ... giovino, and states that there is no danger, without engaging Machiavelli's reasons. The reply ends here, with a stop.

What this is

A free, openly licensed, bilingual edition of Guicciardini's 39 Considerazioni and the Discorsi chapters they answer — 78 text units, Italian and English facing, every reading traceable to a named public-domain witness.

It is a reading edition with a transparent apparatus, not a text of record. It does not adjudicate the critical text against Bausi 2001 or the manuscript tradition, and does not claim to.

On the title

Machiavelli had been dead two years when Guicciardini wrote; the Discorsi were unpublished, and the Considerazioni stayed unprinted until 1857. Machiavelli never answers.

“Conversation” is a quotation, not a description. It invokes Machiavelli's letter to Francesco Vettori of 10 December 1513: at evening he puts on royal and curial garments (panni reali e curiali), enters the ancient courts of ancient men (le antiche corti degli antichi uomini), asks them the reason for their actions, and quelli per loro umanità mi rispondono, “they, for their humanity, answer me”. Guicciardini does to Machiavelli what Machiavelli did to Livy — and is denied the reply Machiavelli claimed. The letter is quoted from the 1813 Opere, vol. 8, p. 96, read twice from the page image.

Method

No image-derived reading enters the text on a single reading. Each unit is transcribed twice, independently, by readers who have not seen each other's work; disagreements are adjudicated against the page images and recorded.

Existing English translations of the Considerazioni — Grayson (1965), Atkinson & Sices (2002) — are never used as translation inputs.
