A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 32 of 39

That one comes from low to great fortune more with fraud than with force Discourses II.13, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that men of small fortune seldom or never rise high without force and without fraud, unless their rank is given or inherited; he does not believe force alone is ever enough, says fraud alone will be, and calls fraud, which Rome used in its first growth, always necessary to those rising from small beginnings. Guicciardini grants that, if fraud means any craft or dissimulation even without guile, it can be true that force alone most rarely raises men from low fortune, though he will not say never; but if fraud is properly breaking faith or other guileful dealing, he believes many have won kingdoms and empires without it. Rome's good treatment of the Latins was, he says, prudence, not fraud. Whether fraud is always a good instrument for greatness he leaves open: deceit often brings off fine strokes, and a name for fraud often spoils one's chances.

Niccolò Machiavelli
Discorsi sopra la prima Deca di Tito Livio · written c. 1513–19, printed 1531
answered by
Francesco Guicciardini
Considerazioni intorno ai Discorsi del Machiavelli · written 1529–30, printed 1857

NM-DISC.2.13

Capitolo XIII

Che si viene di bassa a gran fortuna più con la fraude, che con la forza.

That one comes from low to great fortune more with fraud than with force.

Italian · 1824
English
2.13.01
Io stimo essere cosa verissima che rado o non mai intervenga, che gli uomini di piccola fortuna vengano a gradi grandi, senza la forza e senza la fraude, purché quel grado, al quale altri è pervenuto, non ti sia o donato o lasciato per eredità. Né credo si trovi mai che la forza sola basti, ma si troverà bene che la fraude sola basterà; come chiaro vedrà colui che leggerà la vita di Filippo di Macedonia, quella di Agatocle siciliano, e di molti altri simili, che d'infima ovvero di bassa fortuna sono pervenuti o a Regno o ad Imperj grandissimi.
I esteem it to be a most true thing that it seldom or never befalls that men of small fortune come to great degrees without force and without fraud, provided that that degree at which one has arrived is not either donated or left to you by inheritance. Nor do I believe it is ever found that force alone is enough, but it will indeed be found that fraud alone will be enough; as he will clearly see who reads the life of Philip of Macedon, that of Agathocles the Sicilian, and of many others like them, who from lowest or else from low fortune have arrived either at [a] kingdom or at most great empires.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Machiavelli opens on his own judgment, "I esteem it to be a most true thing": men of small fortune seldom or never come to great degrees without force and without fraud, provided the degree was not donated or left to them by inheritance. The proviso sets aside the degree that is donated or inherited; the claim is about rising. He then separates the two means. He does not believe it is ever found that force alone is enough; it will indeed be found, he says, that fraud alone will be enough. The proof is offered as reading, "as he will clearly see who reads", in the lives of Philip of Macedon, Agathocles the Sicilian and many like them, who came from lowest or else from low fortune to a kingdom or to most great empires.

History. Philip II of Macedon (r. 359-336 BC), a younger son of King Amyntas III, spent part of his youth as a hostage at Thebes before he came to the throne; Agathocles, a Syracusan of humble birth, made himself tyrant of Syracuse about 316 BC. Machiavelli names them without telling their stories here; he tells Agathocles' in The Prince, chapter 8. The identifications and dates are the editor's.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini answers the conclusion under a condition: if fraud means every craft or dissimulation used even without guile, it can be true, but he will not say "never", a word too resolute, only that force alone most rarely conducts men from low to great fortune; if fraud is what is properly fraud, he believes many are found who have acquired kingdoms and most great empires without it Guicciardini .01.

2.13.02
Mostra Senofonte nella sua vita di Ciro questa necessità dello ingannare, considerato che la prima ispedizione che fa fare a Ciro contro il Re di Armenia è piena di fraude, e come con inganno e non con forza gli fa occupare il suo Regno. E non conchiude altro per tale azione, se non che ad un Principe che voglia fare gran cose, è necessario imparare a ingannare. Fagli oltra di questo ingannare Ciassare Re dei Medi, suo zio materno, in più modi, senza la quale fraude mostra che Ciro non poteva pervenire a quella grandezza che venne. Né credo che si trovi mai alcuno constituito in bassa fortuna, pervenuto a grande Imperio solo con la forza aperta e ingenuamente, ma sì bene solo con la fraude, come fece Giovanni Galeazzo per tor lo Stato e lo Imperio di Lombardia a messer Bernabò suo zio.
Xenophon in his life of Cyrus shows this necessity of deceiving, considering that the first expedition that he makes Cyrus make against the king of Armenia is full of fraud, and how with deceit and not with force he makes him occupy his kingdom. And he concludes nothing else from such a deed, except that for a prince who wishes to do great things it is necessary to learn to deceive. He makes him, beyond this, deceive Cyaxares, king of the Medes, his maternal uncle, in several ways, without which fraud he shows that Cyrus could not arrive at that greatness to which he came. Nor do I believe that anyone is ever found, constituted in low fortune, [who has] arrived at great empire only with open force and ingenuously, but indeed only with fraud, as Giovanni Galeazzo did, to take away the state and the rule of Lombardy from messer Bernabò his uncle.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The first authority is Xenophon, who, Machiavelli says, shows this necessity of deceiving. Machiavelli reports two things in the life of Cyrus. The first expedition, against the king of Armenia, is full of fraud and takes the kingdom by deceit and not by force; and from that deed, as Machiavelli gives it, Xenophon concludes nothing else than that for a prince who wishes to do great things it is necessary to learn to deceive. Beyond this, Cyrus deceives his maternal uncle Cyaxares in several ways, without which fraud, Machiavelli says Xenophon shows, Cyrus could not have arrived at his greatness. He then restates his own claim in the negative: he does not believe that anyone is ever found, constituted in low fortune, who has arrived at great empire only with open force and ingenuously, but indeed only with fraud; Giovanni Galeazzo, who took the state of Lombardy from his uncle Bernabò, is the modern example.

History. The Cyropaedia tells the Armenian expedition, which Cyrus disguises as a hunt, at the turn of its second and third books, and his dealings with Cyaxares in its fourth and fifth. Giangaleazzo Visconti, who had held the western half of the Visconti dominions from Pavia since his father's death in 1378, seized his uncle Bernabò in May 1385 and held him prisoner until his death that December, uniting the Visconti lordship of Milan. The identifications and dates are the editor's; Machiavelli says only "the life of Cyrus".

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not now have the memory of Xenophon fresh, but believes that Xenophon instructs Cyrus in prudence, industry and just simulation or dissimulation, not in fraud Guicciardini .01.

2.13.03
E quel che sono necessitati fare i Principi ne' principj degli augumenti loro, sono ancora necessitate a fare le Repubbliche, infino che le sieno diventate potenti, e che basti la forza sola. E perché Roma tenne in ogni parte, o per sorte o per elezione, tutt'i modi necessarj a venire a grandezza, non mancò ancora di questo. Né poté usare nel principio il maggior inganno, che pigliare il modo di sopra discorso da noi, di farsi compagni; perché sotto questo nome se gli fece servi, come furono i Latini, ed altri Popoli all'intorno. Perché prima si valse dell'armi loro in domare i Popoli convicini, e pigliare la riputazione dello Stato; dipoi domatili, venne in tanto augumento, che la poteva battere ciascuno. Ed i Latini non si avvidero mai di essere al tutto servi, se non poi che videro dare due rotte ai Sanniti, e costrettili ad accordo.
And what princes are necessitated to do in the beginnings of their augmentations, republics are also necessitated to do, until they have become powerful and force alone is enough. And because Rome held in every part, either by lot or by choice, all the ways necessary to come to greatness, it did not lack this either. Nor could it use in the beginning a greater deceit than to take the way discoursed of above by us, of making companions for itself; because under this name it made them its servants, as were the Latins, and other peoples round about. Because first it availed itself of their arms in subduing the neighbouring peoples, and taking the reputation of the state; afterwards, having subdued them, it came into such augmentation that it could beat each one. And the Latins never perceived that they were wholly servants, except after they saw two routs given to the Samnites, and them constrained to an accord.
Commentary · 3

Argument. What princes are necessitated to do in the beginnings of their augmentations, republics are necessitated to do too, until they are powerful and force alone is enough. Because Rome held in every part, by lot or by choice, all the ways necessary to come to greatness, it did not lack this one either, and it could use no greater deceit in its beginning than the way already discoursed of, making companions: under that name it made others its servants, the Latins and other peoples round about. The mechanism is told in order. Rome first availed itself of their arms to subdue the neighbouring peoples and take the reputation of the state; then, having subdued them, it grew so great that it could beat each one. The Latins did not perceive that they were wholly servants until they saw the Samnites given two routs and constrained to an accord.

History. The "way discoursed of above" is the way of companions that Machiavelli gives to Rome among the three ways of expanding in Discourses II.4. The routs are those of the First Samnite War (343-341 BC), in which Livy tells of three Roman victories (7.32-37), after which the Samnites made peace with Rome (8.1-2). The identifications and dates are the editor's.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not call it fraud if the Romans made such pacts with the Latins that they could patiently tolerate the Roman empire; their patience, he says, was not because they did not perceive from the very beginning that, under the shadow of confederation, what was equal was servitude Guicciardini .01.

2.13.04
La qual vittoria, come ella accrebbe gran riputazione ai Romani con i Principi longinqui, che mediante quella sentirono il nome romano e non l'armi, così generò invidia e sospetto in quelli che vedevano e sentivano l'armi, intra i quali furono i Latini. E tanto poté questa invidia e questo timore, che non solo i Latini, ma le colonie che essi avevano in Lazio, insieme con i Campani stati innanzi difesi, congiurarono contro al nome romano. E mossero questa guerra i Latini nel modo che si dice di sopra che si muovono la maggior parte delle guerre, assaltando non i Romani, ma difendendo i Sidicini contro ai Sanniti, a' quali i Sanniti facevano guerra con licenza de' Romani. E che sia vero che i Latini si movessero per avere conosciuto questo inganno, lo dimostra Tito Livio nella bocca di Annio Setino Pretore latino, il quale nel Consiglio loro disse queste parole: Nam si etiam nunc sub umbra foederis aequi servitutem pati possumus, etc.
Which victory, as it increased great reputation for the Romans with the far-off princes, who by means of it felt the Roman name and not the arms, so generated envy and suspicion in those who saw and felt the arms, among whom were the Latins. And so much could this envy and this fear do, that not only the Latins, but the colonies which they had in Latium, together with the Campanians, before defended [by the Romans], conspired against the Roman name. And the Latins moved this war in the way that is said above that most wars are moved, assailing not the Romans, but defending the Sidicini against the Samnites, on whom the Samnites were making war with licence of the Romans. And that it is true that the Latins moved for having known this deceit, Livy demonstrates it in the mouth of Annius Setinus, Latin praetor, who in their council said these words: Nam si etiam nunc sub umbra foederis aequi servitutem pati possumus, etc.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The victory had two effects, set as and so: with far-off princes, who felt the Roman name and not the arms, it increased Rome's reputation; in those who saw and felt the arms, the Latins among them, it generated envy and suspicion. Envy and fear moved the Latins, the colonies "they" had in Latium (the Italian does not say whose) and the Campanians, whom Rome had defended before, to conspire against the Roman name. They moved the war in the way Machiavelli says most wars are moved, not by assailing the Romans but by defending the Sidicini against the Samnites, who were making war on them with Roman licence. That the Latins moved for having known this deceit, Livy, Machiavelli says, demonstrates in the mouth of Annius Setinus, Latin praetor, in the Latins' council: "For if even now, under the shadow of an equal treaty, we can bear servitude".

History. Rome defended Capua against the Samnites in 343 BC (Livy 7.29-31); the Sidicini were attacked by the Samnites after Rome renewed its treaty with the Samnites, and the Latins and Campanians took up the Sidicini's cause (Livy 8.1-2). Annius of Setia, one of the two Latin praetors, both men of Roman colonies (Livy 8.3.9), spoke at the council of the Latins before the war of 340-338 BC (8.4); the sentence is Livy 8.4.2. The "way said above" in which wars are moved is the subject of Discourses II.9. The identifications and dates are the editor's.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini takes up Annius' "shadow" in his own "under the shadow of confederation", and gives the Latins' waiting other causes, their impotence and a treatment that gave them no cause to despair; they waited, he does not say until they had uncovered the Romans' end, which they would have been very gross not to have known from the beginning, but until, grown in number of men and well expert in military discipline, they had hope of contending on even terms with the Roman people Guicciardini .01.

2.13.05
Vedesi pertanto i Romani nei primi augumenti loro non essere mancati etiam della fraude; la quale fu sempre necessaria ad usare a coloro che di piccoli principj vogliono a sublimi gradi salire, la quale è meno vituperevole quanto è più coperta, come fu questa de' Romani.
One sees, on that account, that the Romans in their first augmentations did not lack even fraud; which was always necessary to use for those who from small beginnings wish to climb to sublime degrees, which is the less vituperable the more it is covered, as was this of the Romans.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The conclusion is drawn "on that account": the Romans in their first augmentations did not lack even fraud. Two general claims close the chapter, each attached by "which" to fraud. It was always necessary for those to use who from small beginnings wish to climb to sublime degrees; and it is the less vituperable the more it is covered, as Rome's was. The second is a claim about blame, not about success, and it is limited by the comparative: covered fraud is less vituperable, not blameless.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's last sentence answers "always necessary" with a question he leaves open: it can be disputable whether fraud is always a good instrument for arriving at greatness, because often with deceit many fine strokes are made, and often also having the name of fraudulent takes away the occasion of attaining one's intents. Against fraud that is less vituperable the more it is covered, his Caesar rises always uncovering his ambition Guicciardini .01.

Notes

Io stimo essere cosa verissima. "I esteem it to be a most true thing": stimare, esteem; kept apart from riputare, deem (D259), and giudicare, judge. verissimo, most true, the superlative kept, as Guicciardini's credo sia verissimo (Guicciardini .01).

rado o non mai intervenga. "It seldom or never befalls": intervenire, befall (D298). non mai, never, is the word Guicciardini declines to say (non dico mai, Guicciardini .01), with the second mai of Né credo si trovi mai.

gli uomini di piccola fortuna vengano a gradi grandi. "Men of small fortune come to great degrees": grado, degree (glossary), rank here, held at quel grado and sublimi gradi (.05). piccola, small; bassa, low; infima, lowest: three words for the starting point, kept apart.

senza la forza e senza la fraude. "Without force and without fraud": forza, force, and fraude, fraud, through the chapter. "Fraud" is kept although the English word now names a crime: Guicciardini's reply turns on how narrowly fraude is to be taken, setting quella che è proprio fraude apart from every craft or dissimulation (Guicciardini .01).

quel grado, al quale altri è pervenuto, non ti sia o donato o lasciato per eredità. "That degree at which one has arrived is not either donated or left to you by inheritance": the Italian moves from the indefinite altri to ti, and the English keeps both. donare, donate (D253); eredità, inheritance (glossary).

basti, basterà. "Is enough", "will be enough": the present under Né credo (si trovi, basti) against the confident future (si troverà bene, basterà), kept as the Italian has them, here and at Né credo che si trovi mai alcuno (.02); bastare, be enough (D205), here and at che basti la forza sola (.03).

d'infima ovvero di bassa fortuna. "From lowest or else from low fortune": ovvero, or else; whether the second member corrects the first or is an alternative to it, the English does not choose.

o a Regno o ad Imperj grandissimi. "Either at [a] kingdom or at most great empires": regno, kingdom; Imperio, empire (D124), dominion over other peoples, here and at a grande Imperio (.02); lo Imperio di Lombardia (.02) is rule, the lordship of a province (D124). grandissimi, most great, the superlative kept (D411).

Mostra Senofonte nella sua vita di Ciro. "Xenophon in his life of Cyrus shows": the Cyropaedia, the editor's identification; Machiavelli calls it a life. The expedition against the king of Armenia is told at the turn of its second and third books, the dealings with Cyaxares in its fourth and fifth (the editor's references). mostrare, show, here and at mostra che Ciro non poteva.

questa necessità dello ingannare. "This necessity of deceiving": ingannare, deceive; inganno, deceit (D374), through the chapter: con inganno (.02), il maggior inganno (.03), questo inganno (.04). necessità, necessity; necessario, necessary; necessitare, necessitate: one root held (glossary), as at sono necessitati, sono ancora necessitate (.03).

considerato che. "Considering that": the participle as Machiavelli uses it, introducing the ground.

ispedizione. "Expedition": glossary, espedizione.

E non conchiude altro per tale azione. "And he concludes nothing else from such a deed": conchiudere, conclude; azione of a single deed done, "deed" (D410).

ad un Principe che voglia fare gran cose, è necessario imparare a ingannare. "For a prince who wishes to do great things it is necessary to learn to deceive": the maxim is Xenophon's conclusion as Machiavelli reports it, and stays inside non conchiude altro ... se non che.

Fagli oltra di questo ingannare Ciassare. "He makes him, beyond this, deceive Cyaxares": oltra di questo, beyond this, as NM II.proem and II.12; in più modi, in several ways. Ciassare, Cyaxares, king of the Medes, Cyrus' maternal uncle in the Cyropaedia.

senza la quale fraude mostra che Ciro non poteva pervenire a quella grandezza che venne. "Without which fraud he shows that Cyrus could not arrive at that greatness to which he came": pervenire, arrive at, against venire, come, in the same clause, as the rubric's si viene; grandezza, greatness (glossary).

alcuno constituito in bassa fortuna, pervenuto a grande Imperio. "Anyone ... constituted in low fortune, [who has] arrived at great empire": si trovi, present, as in .01; constituire, constitute (glossary). The 1554 reads poſto, placed (NM-DISC-II13-D001, D449).

solo con la forza aperta e ingenuamente. "Only with open force and ingenuously": solo, adverb, only (D298), here and at solo con la fraude, "alone" staying for the adjective (la forza sola); aperto, open, against the coperta, covered, of the close (.05); ingenuamente, ingenuously, in its older sense, frankly, as a free-born man (Latin ingenuus) acts, without guile. The two members, a noun and an adverb, are kept unlike.

ma sì bene solo con la fraude. "But indeed only with fraud": sì bene, indeed, answering Né credo.

come fece Giovanni Galeazzo per tor lo Stato e lo Imperio di Lombardia a messer Bernabò suo zio. "As Giovanni Galeazzo did, to take away the state and the rule of Lombardy from messer Bernabò his uncle": Giangaleazzo Visconti, who seized his uncle Bernabò in 1385 (the editor's note). tor, take away (D131). Stato, state (D265); Imperio, rule (D124). The 1554 names the uncle M.Bernardo (NM-DISC-II13-D001, D449).

ne' principj degli augumenti loro. "In the beginnings of their augmentations": principio, beginning (glossary), here and at nel principio (.03) and di piccoli principj (.05); augumento, augmentation (D69), here, at in tanto augumento (.03) and nei primi augumenti loro (.05), kept apart from accrescere, increase (.04).

sono ancora necessitate a fare le Repubbliche. "Republics are also necessitated to do": ancora, also. The participle agrees with Repubbliche.

o per sorte o per elezione. "Either by lot or by choice": sorte, lot, what falls to one; elezione, choice (glossary).

il modo di sopra discorso da noi, di farsi compagni. "The way discoursed of above by us, of making companions for itself": the Roman way among the three ways of expanding that Machiavelli sets out in Discourses II.4 (the editor's reference); compagni, companions, the Latin socii, allies in name. "For itself" resolves the reflexive farsi, which the Italian leaves to the context: Rome makes others its companions. discorrere, discourse (glossary).

sotto questo nome se gli fece servi. "Under this name it made them its servants": "its" renders the se, which answers farsi; servo, servant (glossary), here and at al tutto servi. nome, name, the name of companion against the thing, as il nome romano (.04) against the arms.

ed altri Popoli all'intorno. "And other peoples round about": all'intorno, round about, of place. The 1554 reads a l'incontro (NM-DISC-II13-D002, D449).

si valse dell'armi loro in domare i Popoli convicini. "It availed itself of their arms in subduing the neighbouring peoples, and taking the reputation of the state": the one in governs both infinitives; valersi di, avail oneself of (D162); domare, subdue, here and at domatili; convicino, neighbouring.

pigliare la riputazione dello Stato. "Taking the reputation of the state": pigliare, take (glossary); riputazione, reputation (glossary), here and at gran riputazione (.04).

dipoi domatili. "Afterwards, having subdued them": dipoi, afterwards (D187).

che la poteva battere ciascuno. "That it could beat each one": battere, beat (D120); la is Rome, the subject (the old subject pronoun, as le, the republics, at le sieno diventate), and ciascuno the object.

al tutto servi. "Wholly servants": al tutto, wholly.

due rotte ai Sanniti, e costrettili ad accordo. "Two routs given to the Samnites, and them constrained to an accord": rotta, rout (D431); costringere, constrain, kept apart from necessitare; accordo, accord. costrettili carries its object, the Samnites, in li; its agent, like that of dare, is left implicit, and the English leaves it so. The figures are Machiavelli's (Livy tells of three Roman victories) and are not corrected.

come ella accrebbe gran riputazione ai Romani ... così generò invidia e sospetto. "As it increased great reputation for the Romans ... so generated envy and suspicion": come ... così, as ... so, kept, with ella in the first member and no pronoun in the second; accrescere, increase (D69); generare, generate (glossary); invidia, envy; sospetto, suspicion (glossary).

con i Principi longinqui. "With the far-off princes": longinquo, far-off (D444). The 1554 reads lontani (NM-DISC-II13-D002, D449).

sentirono il nome romano e non l'armi; vedevano e sentivano l'armi. "Felt the Roman name and not the arms"; "saw and felt the arms": sentire, hear and feel: the far-off princes hear of the name, the near peoples feel the arms; one English verb is kept for the echo, and "felt the Roman name" means heard it.

FG-CONS.2.13

Considerazione sul capitolo XIII

Che si viene di bassa a gran fortuna più con la fraude, che con la forza

That one comes from low to great fortune more with fraud than with force

Italian · 1857
English
2.13.01
Se lo Scrittore chiama fraude ogni astuzia o dissimulazione che si usa e[ti]am sanza dolo, può essere vera la conclusione sua, che la forza sola, non dico mai, che è vocabolo troppo resoluto, ma rarissime volte conduca gli uomini da bassa a grande fortuna. Ma se chiama fraude quella che è proprio fraude, cioè il mancamento di fede, o altro procedere doloso, credo si trovino molti ch[e] hanno sanza fraude acquistato r[e]gni e imperii grandissimi. Di questi fu Alessandro Magno, di questi Cesare, che di cittadino privato con altre arti che di fraude si condusse a tanta grandezza, scoprendo sempre la ambizione s[u]a o lo appetito del dominare. Non ho ora fresca la memoria di Zenofonte, ma credo che instruisca Ciro di prudenza, d'industria, di simulazione o dissimulazione giuste, non di fraude. Né chiamo fraude se e' Romani fecion[o] tali patti ai Latini ch[e] potettono pazientemente tollerare lo imperio loro, il che non fu perché non si accorgessino insino dal principio, che, sotto ombra di confederazione, equale era servitù; ma il trovarsi impotenti, né essere trattati in modo che non avessino causa di desperarsi, gli fece aspettare insino a t[a]nto, non dico che ebbono scoperto il fine de' Romani, il quale sarebbono stati bene grossi se non avessino cognosciuto da principio, ma che cresciuti di numero di uomini, e bene esperti di disciplina militare, ebbono speranza poter[e] contendere del pari col populo romano. Fu anche prudenza quella de' Romani, non fraude, a trattare bene e' Latini; e credo sia verissimo che sanza simili industrie e prudenti modi di governarsi non solo rarissime volte si salga da bassa fortuna a alta, ma ancora difficilmente si conservi la grandezza. Ma, quanto alla fraude, può essere disputabile se sia sempre buono instrumento di pervenire alla grandezza; perché spesso con lo inganno si fanno di molti belli tratti, spesso anche l'avere nome di fraudolent[o] toglie occasione di conseguire gli intenti suoi
If the Writer calls fraud every craft or dissimulation that is used even without guile, his conclusion can be true, that force alone, I do not say never, which is too resolute a word, but most rarely conducts men from low to great fortune. But if he calls fraud that which is properly fraud, that is, the failing of faith, or other guileful proceeding, I believe many are found who have without fraud acquired kingdoms and most great empires. Of these was Alexander the Great, of these Caesar, who from private citizen, with other arts than [those] of fraud, conducted himself to such greatness, always uncovering his ambition or the appetite for dominating. I do not now have the memory of Xenophon fresh, but I believe that he instructs Cyrus in prudence, in industry, in just simulation or dissimulation, not in fraud. Nor do I call it fraud if the Romans made such pacts with the Latins that they could patiently tolerate their empire, which was not because they did not perceive from the very beginning that, under the shadow of confederation, [what was] equal was servitude; but finding themselves impotent, and not being treated in such a way that they had cause to despair, made them wait until such time, I do not say that they had uncovered the end of the Romans, which they would have been very gross [men] if they had not known from the beginning, but that, grown in number of men, and well expert in military discipline, they had hope to be able to contend on even [terms] with the Roman people. It was also prudence, that of the Romans, not fraud, to treat the Latins well; and I believe it is most true that without like industries and prudent ways of governing oneself not only does one most rarely climb from low fortune to high, but also greatness is conserved with difficulty. But, as to fraud, it can be disputable whether it is always a good instrument for arriving at greatness; because often with deceit many fine strokes are made, often also having the name of [being] fraudulent takes away the occasion of attaining his intents
Commentary · 3

Argument. The reply is one paragraph, and it turns on a definition. If the Writer calls fraud every craft or dissimulation that is used even without guile, Machiavelli's conclusion can be true, with one correction: Guicciardini will not say that force alone never conducts men from low to great fortune, "never" being a word too resolute, only that it does so most rarely. If fraud is what is properly fraud, the failing of faith or other guileful proceeding, then he believes many are found who have acquired kingdoms and most great empires without it. Of these, he says, was Alexander the Great, of these Caesar, who rose from private citizen with other arts than those of fraud, always uncovering his ambition or the appetite for dominating. He goes through Machiavelli's authorities in turn. Of Xenophon he says that he does not now have the memory fresh, but believes that Xenophon instructs Cyrus in prudence, in industry, in just simulation or dissimulation, not in fraud. Of Rome and the Latins he says that he does not call it fraud if the Romans made pacts the Latins could patiently tolerate. Their patience, he says, was not because they did not perceive from the very beginning that, under the shadow of confederation, what was equal was servitude; what made them wait was finding themselves impotent and not being treated so as to have cause to despair, and they waited, he does not say until they had uncovered Rome's end, which they would have been very gross not to have known from the beginning, but until, grown in number of men and well expert in military discipline, they had hope of contending on even terms with the Roman people. The Romans' good treatment of the Latins was also prudence, not fraud. He then believes it most true that without like industries and prudent ways of governing oneself not only does one most rarely climb from low fortune to high, but also greatness is conserved with difficulty. On fraud itself he leaves the question open: it can be disputable whether it is always a good instrument for arriving at greatness, since deceit often makes fine strokes and the name of fraudulent often takes away the occasion of attaining one's intents. The reply ends there, without a stop.

History. Alexander came to a kingdom his father Philip left him, the case Machiavelli's proviso sets aside, before he acquired an empire; Caesar rose through the offices of the republic to the dictatorship. These are the editor's observations; Guicciardini gives the two names without their histories. Annius' sentence in Livy speaks of an equal treaty (foederis aequi), the treaty that bound the Latins to Rome; Guicciardini's "confederation" answers it, and Canestrini's comma, which this edition keeps (D110), sets equale with "servitude" rather than with the confederation, where the 1933 edition, without the comma, has it.

Cross-reference. The condition at the head answers Machiavelli's rubric and his opening judgment Machiavelli .01, and "I do not say never" answers his "seldom or never" and "Nor do I believe it is ever found". Xenophon and Cyrus are Machiavelli's first authority Machiavelli .02; the Romans' pacts with the Latins, the Latins' perceiving, and "the shadow of confederation" answer his account of the companions and Annius' words Machiavelli .03 Machiavelli .04. Read beside Machiavelli's close, Caesar's uncovered ambition stands against fraud that is less vituperable the more it is covered, the climb from low fortune takes up his "climb to sublime degrees", and "always a good instrument" takes up "always necessary" Machiavelli .05. The etiam of the first sentence is the word of that close.

What this is

A free, openly licensed, bilingual edition of Guicciardini's 39 Considerazioni and the Discorsi chapters they answer — 78 text units, Italian and English facing, every reading traceable to a named public-domain witness.

It is a reading edition with a transparent apparatus, not a text of record. It does not adjudicate the critical text against Bausi 2001 or the manuscript tradition, and does not claim to.

On the title

Machiavelli had been dead two years when Guicciardini wrote; the Discorsi were unpublished, and the Considerazioni stayed unprinted until 1857. Machiavelli never answers.

“Conversation” is a quotation, not a description. It invokes Machiavelli's letter to Francesco Vettori of 10 December 1513: at evening he puts on royal and curial garments (panni reali e curiali), enters the ancient courts of ancient men (le antiche corti degli antichi uomini), asks them the reason for their actions, and quelli per loro umanità mi rispondono, “they, for their humanity, answer me”. Guicciardini does to Machiavelli what Machiavelli did to Livy — and is denied the reply Machiavelli claimed. The letter is quoted from the 1813 Opere, vol. 8, p. 96, read twice from the page image.

Method

No image-derived reading enters the text on a single reading. Each unit is transcribed twice, independently, by readers who have not seen each other's work; disagreements are adjudicated against the page images and recorded.

Existing English translations of the Considerazioni — Grayson (1965), Atkinson & Sices (2002) — are never used as translation inputs.
