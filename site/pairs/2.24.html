Fortresses generally are much more damaging than useful Discourses II.24, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli holds that fortresses are generally much more damaging than useful: against subjects they invite hatred through ill treatment and fail in war; against outside enemies they are unnecessary with good armies and useless without them. Guicciardini warns against praising antiquity so far as to blame every modern practice Rome lacked. Since peoples, even well treated, are often not very reasonable and eager for change, every ruler, loved or not, needs, he says, some foundation in force; the charge that fortresses make princes insolent he calls very frivolous. He grants that in many cases fortresses do not help and other remedies are perhaps sometimes more useful, but holds them often useful against conspiracies and rebellions and for recovering lost towns; therefore our times use them not without cause.

Commentary · 3

Argument. The chapter opens on the objection it will answer: to the wise men of the present it will seem ill considered that the Romans, securing themselves of the Latins and of Privernum, built no fortress there as a rein to hold them in faith, the more so since a Florentine saying, which those wise men cite, holds that Pisa and like cities must be held with fortresses. Machiavelli's reply is that the Romans were of another virtù, judgment and power: while Rome lived free and kept its orders and virtuous constitutions it never built one to hold cities or provinces, though it saved some already built.

History. The settlement of the Latins (338 BC) and of Privernum (329 BC) is Livy 8.13-14 and 8.19-21; the chapter before, II.23, discusses both judgments (the editor's references). The Florentine saying appears in The Prince, chapter 20, with Pistoia held by parties beside Pisa held by fortresses (the editor's reference).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini opens on the same comparison, refusing to praise antiquity so much that every modern order the Romans did not use is blamed; the Romans' not building fortresses in the subject cities is not, for him, a reason to blame whoever builds them now Guicciardini .01. The Romans' saving "some of those built" is the ground of his last word (Tarentum and the other places where they found fortresses made and did not unwall them), in the editor's reading Guicciardini .03.

Commentary · 2

Argument. The question is put: whether it is good to build fortresses, and whether they do damage or advantage to the builder. They are made either to defend oneself from enemies or from subjects; in the first case they are not necessary, in the second they are damaging. The rest of the chapter follows this division: the second case first (.03-.12), the first at the end (.13-.14).

Commentary · 4

Argument. Why fortresses against subjects are damaging. A prince or republic that fears its subjects must first be hated by them; the hatred comes from its ill carriages; these come either from believing it can hold the subjects by force or from little prudence in governing; and one of the things that makes it believe it can force them is having fortresses on their backs. So the ill treatment that causes the hatred arises in good part from the fortresses, which, when this is true, are far more harmful than useful. The condition, "when this is true", is Machiavelli's own.

Note. Two reasons follow. First, the fortresses make the ruler more audacious and violent toward the subjects. Second, there is not the security within that he persuades himself of: of all the forces used to hold a people only two avail, a good army always ready for the field, as Rome had, or scattering and disjoining the people so that it cannot combine; for impoverished men keep arms (Juvenal), disarmed men find them in fury (Virgil), and heads cut off grow again like the Hydra's. Fortresses are useful in peace, because they give more spirit to do the subjects ill (in the editor's reading a use that is itself the harm), and most useless in war, when enemy and subjects assail them together; and in our times artillery makes small places, and places where one cannot withdraw with ramparts, impossible to defend.

History. The two Latin tags are Juvenal, Satires 8.124, and Virgil, Aeneid 1.150 (the editor's references). The earlier discussion of artillery is II.17 (the editor's reading of "as we discoursed above").

Cross-reference. Guicciardini takes up two of these phrases: the "ill carriages", in the reason he calls very frivolous, and the spirit the fortress gives, which he answers with medicine, whose security may make men careless and is not on that account refused Guicciardini .01. Against the hatred that ill treatment causes he sets peoples who, "even well treated", are often unreasonable Guicciardini .01. The 1554 omits the negative in "there is not that security" and reads "useless" for "useful" in peace (variant notes).

Commentary · 3

Argument. The freshest and most notable example, apt to show the inutility of building fortresses and the utility of unmaking them, is Genoa. After its rebellion of 1507 Louis XII came in person, recovered it, and built a fortress stronger than any known, set on a point of hill over the sea, the Codefa, commanding the port and much of the town. In 1512, the French driven from Italy, Genoa rebelled notwithstanding the fortress; Ottaviano Fregoso took the state, reduced the fortress by hunger in sixteen months, and, against the counsel of many that he keep it as a refuge, ruined it, knowing that the will of men, not fortresses, keeps princes in their states. History. The fortress is the Briglia, begun by Louis XII on the Capo di Faro in 1507 and surrendered to Ottaviano Fregoso in 1514 (the editor's identification and dates). Considerazione sul capitolo XXIV

Considerazioni II.24

Le fortezze generalmente sono molto più dannose che utili

Fortresses generally are much more damaging than useful

Italian · 1857
English
2.24.0126 notes2 variants
Non si debbe laudare tanto la antiquità, che l'uomo biasimi tutti gli ordini moderni che non erano in uso appresso a' Romani; perché la esperienza ha scoperte molte cose che non furono considerate dagli antichi, e, per essere inoltre e' fondamenti diversi, convengono o sono necessarie a una delle cose che non convenivano, o non erano necessarie all'altre. Però se e' Romani nella città suddite non usorono di edificare fortezze, non è per questo che erri chi oggidì ve le edifica; perché accaggiono molti casi, per e' quali è molto utile avere le fortezze, e a uno principe o vero tiranno co' cittadini medesimi, e a uno signore co' sudditi suoi, e a uno potentato co' forestieri. Le ragioni mi paiono sì manifeste, che io mi maraviglio che questa opinione abbia contradittori, perché principalmente se, quali sono gli imperii, tali fussino sempre e' sudditi, cioè che quando sono bene trattati amassino il principe suo, io confesso che quanto a loro sarebbono a ogni principe che governassi bene inutili le fortezze; perché basterebbe a difenderlo da' cittadini e sudditi suoi lo amore de' populi. Ma considerato quanto molte volte e' populi, eziamdio bene trattati, sono spesso poco ragionevoli, quanto desiderosi di cose nuove, quanto possi valere in loro la memoria dello antico principe se ora sono sotto uno imperio nuovo, quanto lo appetito della libertà se sono usati a averla, e quanto spesso per questo e per altri rispetti uno principe o tiranno è sforzato governare e' cittadini o sudditi suoi con qualche ingiuria, dico che e a quelli che possono avere e' populi amici, e a quelli che non possono sperare di conseguire questa benevolenza, è necessario fare qualche fondamento in sulla forza, in sul tenere [e]' populi suoi in qualche terrore; altrimenti sarebbe troppo spesso in preda della leggerezza, della malignità, del giusto odio de' sudditi suoi. E quella ragione che si adduce nel Discorso, che le fortezze danno animo a' principi a essere insolenti e fare mali portamenti, è molto frivola, perché se s'avessi a considerare questo, arebbe uno principe a stare sanza guardia, sanza armi, sanza eserciti, per avere tanto più a cercare di vivere in modo che fussi grato a' populi quanto più si trovassi esposto alla loro discrezione. Di poi le cose che in sé sono utili non si debbono fuggire, se bene la sicurtà che tu trai di loro ti possa dare animo a essere cattivo: verbi gratia, hassi egli a biasimare la medicina, perché gli uomini sotto la fidanza di quella si possono guardare manco da' disordini e dalle cagioni che fanno infermare? Non è questa buona ragione né da fare rifiutare il bene, quando il male che ne può seguire è in potestà tua se seguiti o no.
One must not praise antiquity so much that a man blame all the modern orders that were not in use among the Romans; because experience has uncovered many things that were not considered by the ancients, and, through the foundations' being besides different, [things] are fitting or are necessary to one of the things which were not fitting, or were not necessary, to the others. Therefore, if the Romans did not use to build fortresses in the subject cities, it is not for this that he err who builds them there nowadays; because many cases happen in which it is very useful to have fortresses, both to a prince or else a tyrant with the citizens themselves, and to a lord with his subjects, and to a potentate with strangers. The reasons seem to me so manifest that I marvel that this opinion has contradictors, because, principally, if, such as the rules are, such the subjects always were, that is, that when they are well treated they loved their prince, I confess that as to them fortresses would be useless to every prince that governed well; because, to defend him from his citizens and subjects, the love of the peoples would be enough. But considering how many times the peoples, even well treated, are often little reasonable, how desirous of new things, how much the memory of the old prince can be worth in them if they are now under a new rule, how much the appetite for liberty, if they are used to having it, and how often, for this and for other respects, a prince or tyrant is forced to govern his citizens or subjects with some injury, I say that both for those who can have the peoples [as] friends and for those who cannot hope to attain this benevolence, it is necessary to make some foundation upon force, upon holding their peoples in some terror; otherwise he would be too often a prey to the levity, to the malignity, to the just hatred of his subjects. And that reason which is adduced in the Discourse, that fortresses give princes spirit to be insolent and to make ill carriages, is very frivolous, because if one were to consider this, a prince would have to stand without guard, without arms, without armies, so as to have so much the more to seek to live in such a way that he were pleasing to the peoples, the more he found himself exposed to their discretion. This is not a good reason, nor [one] to make the good be refused, when the ill that can follow from it is in your power, whether it follow or not.
Commentary · 4

Argument. The reply opens on method before it reaches fortresses: antiquity must not be praised so much that every modern order the Romans did not use is blamed, because experience has uncovered things the ancients did not consider, and, the foundations being different, what suits one condition did not suit the other. Note. Guicciardini marvels that the opinion has contradictors, and gives his reasons. ¶ 02 se non vede esercito forestiere inimico del principe in quella provincia. "Unless it sees a foreign army, enemy to the prince, in that province": forestiere (adjective), foreign (glossary); provincia, province (glossary).

¶ 02 Né sono buoni gli esempli di Milano e gli altri che lui allega. "Nor are they good, the examples of Milan and the others that he cites": the order kept, so that the relative che ... perderono follows its antecedent, Milan and the others; benché, albeit (D31); Machiavelli .05-.07; allegare, cite (D431); lui, he, Machiavelli.

¶ 02 perché non per rebellione de' populi soli, ma per occasione di guerra potente. "Because [they lost them] not through rebellion of the peoples alone, but through occasion of powerful war": the verb understood (D53); rebellione, rebellion, in Guicciardini's spelling.

¶ 02 e si potria dire che se non l'avessino avute, l'arebbono perdute forse molto prima etiamdio ne' tempi della pace. "And it could be said that, if they had not had them, they would have lost them perhaps much earlier, even in the times of peace": si potria dire, it could be said, the conditional of D264's si può dire, a possibility offered, not his judgment; forse, perhaps, limiting molto prima, not the loss; prima, earlier (D334); etiamdio, even (D488). Machiavelli's counterfactual (Machiavelli .05: without the fortress the Sforza would have seen the danger sooner) turned the other way, in the editor's reading.

¶ 02 E se per virtù della fortezza non si recupera sempre la terra persa. "And if by virtù of the fortress the lost town is not always recovered": virtù (D16), as Machiavelli's la virtù delle Fortezze (Machiavelli .09), ironic in the editor's reading; recuperare, recover (glossary).

¶ 02 si è anche visto qualche volta recuperarne. "One has also seen, sometimes, some recovered": impersonal; qualche volta, sometimes (D431); -ne, some of them, lost towns.

¶ 02 come intervenne a monsignore di Fois a Brescia. "As befell Monsignor de Foix at Brescia": intervenire, befall (D298); the objector's modern example, which Machiavelli answers (Machiavelli .11-.12): there the fortress needed Foix's army; here the army needed the fortress, in the editor's reading; Gaston de Foix, February 1512 (names.md); ancora che, even though (D31).

¶ 02 non era bastante a recuperare Brescia. "Was not enough to recover Brescia": bastante, enough, with bastare (D205); "sufficient" stays sufficiente's.

¶ 02 è il timore della fortezza bastante a tenere impegnati li inimici sanza farci altra offesa insino l'abbino acquistata. "The fear of the fortress is enough to keep the enemies engaged, without doing other offence there, until they have acquired it": timore, fear (glossary); impegnare, engage; offesa, offence (glossary offendere); ci, there, in the editor's reading; "to us" is possible.

¶ 02 il quale intervallo di tempo può essere causa di gran beneficio a chi si truova assaltato. "Which interval of time can be the cause of great benefit to whoever finds himself assailed": causa, cause (glossary, a recorded collapse with cagione); beneficio, benefit (D334); assaltare, assail (D444).

¶ 03 E quanto allo esemplo che si allega de' Romani, posposto lo esemplo del duca Guido, di Ottaviano e degli altri. "And as to the example of the Romans that is cited, setting aside the example of Duke Guido, of Ottaviano and of the others": posporre, set aside (glossary); Guidobaldo of Urbino and Ottaviano Fregoso (Machiavelli .07, .08; names.md); esemplo, example (glossary).

¶ 03 la autorità de' quali non basta a confondere la autorità di tanti altri. "Whose authority is not enough to confound the authority of so many others": autorità, authority (glossary), Machiavelli's l'autorità de' Romani (Machiavelli .10) answered with the authority of numbers, in the editor's reading.

¶ 03 due potettono essere le cause. "The causes could have been two": the past of possibility; the order normalised (D263).

¶ 03 come altrove ha detto lo Autore. "As the Author has said elsewhere": Autore, Author, capitalised as Canestrini prints it, beside Scrittore, the Writer (glossary). The place is Discourses II.4, on Rome's companions, in the editor's reading.

¶ 03 ne' principii dello imperio loro non usorono ridurre le città in espressa servitù, ma tenerle sotto ombra di libertà e di confederazione equale. "In the beginnings of their empire they did not use to bring cities into express servitude, but to keep them under a shadow of liberty and of equal confederation": principio, beginning (glossary); imperio, empire (D124), the first sense; ridurre, bring (D314); servitù, servitude (glossary); ombra, shadow (glossary); confederazione, confederation (D452); equale, equal (D262).

¶ 03 il quale instituto non comportava lo edificarvi fortezze. "Which institute did not bear the building of fortresses there": instituto, institute, kept apart from instituzione, institution (FG I.10) (D488); comportare, bear (glossary).

¶ 03 trovandosi sempre con gli eserciti ordinati e potentissimi, e in molti luoghi con le colonie. "Finding themselves always with ordered and most powerful armies, and in many places with colonies": ordinato, ordered (glossary); colonie, colonies.

¶ 03 massime che erano consueti distruggere più presto le città, le quali reputavano inimicissime. "Above all since they were accustomed sooner to destroy the cities which they deemed most inimical": massime, above all (glossary); più presto without che, sooner (D464); reputare, deem (D259); inimicissimo, most inimical, of inimico; the comma before le quali is Canestrini's (D110), the relative read as restrictive by sense.

¶ 03 perché così sarebbe inutile una fortezza edificata da altri, come quella che fussi edificata da te. "Because a fortress built by others would be as useless as one that were built by you": così ... come, as ... as (D334); the order normalised (D263).

¶ 03 Confesso adunque che in molti casi e in molti tempi le fortezze non giovano. "I confess, then, that in many cases and in many times fortresses do not help": the second Confesso (D431), a real concession; adunque, then (D74); giovare, help (D334), Machiavelli's le quali non ti giovano in alcuna parte (Machiavelli .05). Both readers doubted the conjunction against o, both preferring e; read e, as the 1933 edition's ed (D486).

¶ 03 che alla sicurtà dello Stato tuo sono degli altri remedii, forse qualche volta più utili e più gagliardi che le fortezze. "That for the security of your state there are other remedies, perhaps sometimes more useful and harder than fortresses": remedio, remedy (glossary); gagliardo, hard (D313), of force, not "more difficult"; forse and qualche volta both kept.

¶ 03 per assicurarsi dalle congiure, per fuggire le rebellioni, e per recuperare le terre perdute. "To secure himself from conspiracies, to flee rebellions, and to recover lost towns": assicurarsi, secure oneself (D219), Machiavelli's verb (Machiavelli .01, .14); congiure, conspiracies (D452's congiurare); fuggire, flee (D325); terre, towns (D488).

¶ 03 Però non sanza cagione e' tempi nostri le adoperano. "Therefore it is not without cause that our times employ them": però, therefore (D22); adoperare, employ (glossary); the fronted litotes kept as a landing (D263), answering the opening's non è per questo che erri. Both readers' split at le (A) and la (B) read le, the fortresses, with the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-II24-D002; D486).

¶ 03 furono in uso apresso agli antichi, e i Romani a Taranto, e negli altri luoghi che le trovorono fatte, non le smurorono. "They were in use among the ancients, and the Romans at Tarentum, and in the other places where they found them made, did not unwall them": in uso, in use, the reply's opening phrase (non erano in uso) returned; Taranto, Tarentum (names.md); smurare, unwall, Machiavelli's smuravano e non muravano (Machiavelli .10) answered in his own verb. The reply ends with Canestrini's printed stop.
