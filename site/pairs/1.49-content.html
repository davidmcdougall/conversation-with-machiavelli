A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 26 of 39

If those cities that have had a free beginning, like Rome, have difficulty in finding laws that may maintain them, those that have it immediately servile have almost an impossibility of it Discourses I.49, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli argues that if even Rome, with a free beginning, kept needing new orders such as the censors, cities with a servile beginning, like Florence, can never order themselves to live civilly. Guicciardini says this chapter and many others show what he has said elsewhere against Machiavelli: military discipline aside, the Roman government was in many parts defective, since one man alone could stop public business, and though the tribune checked the consuls, nothing checked a tribune who wished such disorder. It was also an error that the censors, indeed one alone, could remove Mamercus from the senate for so good a work. He does not believe Mamercus had any remedy but an unrecorded law of the people or restoration by later censors, and is not certain they could restore him, though he believes it.

Capitolo XLIX

Discorsi I.49

Se quelle Città che hanno avuto il principio libero, come Roma, hanno difficultà a trovare leggi che le mantengano; quelle che lo hanno immediate servo, ne hanno quasi una impossibilità.

If those cities that have had a free beginning, like Rome, have difficulty in finding laws that may maintain them, those that have it immediately servile have almost an impossibility of it.

1.49.015 notes1 variant
Quanto sia difficile nello ordinare una Repubblica provvedere a tutte quelle leggi che la mantengano libera, lo dimostra assai bene il processo della Repubblica romana, dove non ostante che fussero ordinate di molte leggi da Romolo prima, dipoi da Numa, da Tullo Ostilio e Servio, e ultimamente dai Dieci cittadini creati a simile opera, nondimeno sempre nel maneggiare quella Città si scoprivano nuove necessità, ed era necessario creare nuovi ordini; come intervenne quando crearono i Censori, i quali furono uno di quelli provvedimenti che ajutarono a tenere Roma libera quel tempo che la visse in libertà. Perché diventati arbitri de' costumi di Roma, furono cagione potissima che i Romani differissero più a corrompersi.
How difficult it is, in ordering a republic, to provide for all those laws that may maintain it free, the process of the Roman republic demonstrates it very well, where, notwithstanding that many laws were ordered, by Romulus first, afterwards by Numa, by Tullus Hostilius and Servius, and lastly by the Ten citizens created for a like work, nonetheless always, in handling that city, new necessities were discovered, and it was necessary to create new orders; as befell when they created the censors, who were one of those provisions that aided in holding Rome free [for] that time that it lived in liberty. Because, become arbiters of the customs of Rome, they were the chief cause that the Romans deferred longer corrupting themselves.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The claim comes first and Rome is its demonstration: how difficult it is, in ordering a republic, to provide for all the laws that may keep it free. Rome had laws from four kings and from the Ten, and still, in handling the city, new necessities kept being discovered and new orders had to be created. The censors are the instance, one of the provisions that aided in holding Rome free for as long as it lived in liberty, and the reason given is that, as arbiters of its customs, they were the principal cause that the Romans deferred longer corrupting themselves.

History. Livy places the beginning of the censorship in the year conventionally dated 443 BC (4.8). The kings named are four of the seven; the Ten are the decemvirs of 451-449 BC, Machiavelli's subject at I.40.

1.49.026 notes
Fecero bene nel principio della creazione di tal Magistrato uno errore, creando quello per cinque anni; ma dipoi non molto tempo, fu corretto dalla prudenza di Mamerco Dittatore, il qual per nuova legge ridusse detto Magistrato a diciotto mesi; il che i Censori che vegghiavano ebbero tanto per male, che privarono Mamerco del Senato; la qual cosa e dalla Plebe, e dai Padri fu assai biasimata: e perché la Istoria non mostra che Mamerco se ne potesse difendere, conviene o che lo Istorico sia difettivo, o gli ordini di Roma in questa parte non buoni; perché non è bene che una Repubblica sia in modo ordinata, che un cittadino per promulgare una legge conforme al viver libero, ne possa essere senza alcun rimedio offeso.
They indeed made an error in the beginning of the creation of such a magistracy, creating it for five years; but not much time afterwards it was corrected by the prudence of Mamercus the dictator, who by a new law brought the said magistracy to eighteen months; which the censors who were [then] in office took so ill that they deprived Mamercus of the senate; which thing was much blamed both by the plebs and by the Fathers: and because the history does not show that Mamercus could defend himself from it, it is fitting either that the historian be defective, or the orders of Rome in this part not good; because it is not good that a republic be ordered in such a way that a citizen, for promulgating a law conformable to free living, can be offended for it without any remedy.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The new order had a first error, a term of five years, corrected by Mamercus as dictator with a law of eighteen months; the censors then in office took it ill and deprived him of the senate. The blame of plebs and fathers is recorded, and the inference is put as an alternative Machiavelli does not decide: either the historian is defective, since he does not show that Mamercus could defend himself, or the orders of Rome were not good in this part. The ground of the dilemma is general: it is not good that a republic be ordered so that a citizen can be offended without remedy for promulgating a law conformable to free living. The editor's reading: the episode serves the chapter's claim, since the remedy of one error exposed something the orders may not have provided for; Machiavelli does not draw the link himself.

History. Livy 4.24: Mamercus, dictator in the year conventionally dated 434 BC, carried a law limiting the censorship to a year and a half; the censors moved him from his tribe and made him an aerarius with an eightfold assessment. Livy does not say he was removed from the senate; the phrase is not Livy's. Livy has the leading senators offended at the censors' harshness and the people's indignation great.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not take up the historian's silence. He judges the censors' power itself an error, Fu anche [e]rrore Guicciardini .01, and on the remedy does not believe there was any but a law of the people or restoration by later censors, which he is not certain they could do, though he believes it. His defettivo, of the Roman government, echoes Machiavelli's difettivo of the historian.

Ma tornando al principio di questo discorso, dico che si debbe per la creazione di questo nuovo Magistrato considerare, che se quelle Città che hanno avuto il principio libero, e che per sé medesimo si è r[e]tto, come Roma, hanno difficultà grande a trovar leggi buone per mantenerle libere, non è maraviglia che quelle Città, che hanno avuto il principio loro immediate servo, abbiano, non che difficultà, ma impossibilità ad ordinarsi mai in modo che le possano vivere civilmente e quietamente: come si vede che è intervenuto alla Città di Firenze, la quale per aver avuto il principio suo sottoposto all'Imperio romano, ed essendo vivuta sempre sotto governo d'altri, stette un tempo soggetta, e senza pensare a sé medesima; dipoi, venuta l'occasione di respirare, cominciò a fare i suoi ordini, i quali sendo mescolati con gli antichi, ch'erano tristi, non poterono esser buoni; e così è ita maneggiandosi per dugento anni, che si ha di vera memoria, senza avere mai avuto Stato, per il quale ella possa veramente essere chiamata Repubblica. E queste difficultà che sono state in lei, sono state sempre in tutte quelle Città che hanno avuto i principj simili a lei. E benché molte volte per suffragj pubblici e liberi si sia dato ampia autorità a pochi cittadini di potere riformarla, non pertanto mai l'hanno ordinata a comune utilità, ma sempre a proposito della parte loro; il che ha fatto non ordine, ma maggiore disordine in quella Città.
But, returning to the beginning of this discourse, I say that one must consider, through the creation of this new magistracy, that if those cities that have had a free beginning, and which by itself has governed itself, like Rome, have great difficulty in finding good laws to maintain them free, it is no marvel that those cities that have had their beginning immediately servile have, not merely difficulty, but impossibility of ever ordering themselves in such a way that they can live civilly and quietly: as one sees [it] has befallen the city of Florence, which, through having had its beginning subjected to the Roman empire, and having lived always under the government of others, stayed a time subject, and without thinking of itself; afterwards, the occasion of breathing having come, it began to make its orders, which, being mixed with the ancient ones, which were wicked, could not be good; and so it has gone on handling itself for two hundred years, of which there is true memory, without ever having had a state by which it can truly be called a republic. And these difficulties that have been in it have been always in all those cities that have had beginnings like it. And albeit many times, by public and free suffrages, ample authority has been given to a few citizens of being able to reform it, they have for all that never ordered it to common utility, but always to the purpose of their party; which has made not order, but greater disorder in that city.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The return to the beginning of the discourse turns the Roman case into a comparison. If cities with a free beginning, and which by itself has governed itself, like Rome, have great difficulty in finding laws to keep them free, it is no marvel that cities with a servile beginning have not difficulty but impossibility of ever ordering themselves to live civilly and quietly. The rubric's quasi una impossibilità becomes impossibilità here. Florence is the instance: subjected to the Roman empire and always under others' government, it began its own orders when it could breathe, mixed them with old wicked ones, and has handled itself for two hundred years of true memory without a state that could truly be called a republic. The difficulties are general to cities with such beginnings, and reform by few citizens given ample authority has always served their party and made greater disorder. The argument runs from the greater to the less, from Rome's difficulty to the servile cities' impossibility, and Machiavelli extends it to all cities with such beginnings: sono state sempre in tutte.

Cross-reference. The emended si è r[e]tto answers Florence sotto governo d'altri in the same period and Rome ordinata da sé medesima at the close Machiavelli .06; the 1824's rotto would break both echoes. Florence's beginning is Machiavelli's subject at I.1.

History. The grants of ample authority to a few citizens describe what Florence called balìe; Machiavelli uses neither the word nor a date, and does not date the start of the two hundred years.

1.49.044 notes1 variant
E per venire a qualche esempio particolare, dico come tra l'altre cose che si hanno a considerare da uno ordinatore d'una Repubblica, è, esaminare nelle mani di quali uomini ei ponga l'autorità del sangue contro de' suoi cittadini: questo era bene ordinato in Roma, perché ei si poteva appellare al Popolo ordinariamente; e se pure fusse occorsa cosa importante, dove il differire la esecuzione mediante l'appellazione fusse pericoloso, avevano il rifugio del Dittatore, il quale eseguiva immediate, al qual rimedio non rifuggivano mai, se non per necessità.
And, to come to some particular example, I say that among the other things that are to be considered by an orderer of a republic is to examine into the hands of which men he sets the authority of blood against his citizens: this was well ordered at Rome, because one could appeal to the people ordinarily; and if yet some important thing had occurred, where the deferring of the execution by means of the appeal were dangerous, they had the refuge of the dictator, who executed immediately, tn which remedy they never took refuge, except through necessity.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The particular example is one thing an orderer of a republic must examine: in whose hands he sets the authority of blood. Rome ordered it well by an ordinary appeal to the people, and for the case where deferring execution by appeal was dangerous, it had the refuge of the dictator, who executed at once, a remedy used only of necessity. The two Roman provisions answer each other: the ordinary appeal, and, where delay was dangerous, the refuge of the dictator, used only of necessity. The dictator is not named as a danger here.

Cross-reference. The appeal to the plebs is mentioned at I.32 and is at issue with the Ten at I.40; the dictator is the matter of I.34, which this edition does not pair.

Commentary · 3

Argument. Florence and the cities born like it, being servile, had this authority placed in a stranger sent by the prince; free, they maintained it in a stranger called the Captain, which, since he could readily be corrupted by powerful citizens, was a most pernicious thing; then, through the change of states, eight citizens took the office, and the order went from bad to very bad, because the few were always ministers of the few and of the most powerful. Venice guarded itself against this: its ten citizens may punish without appeal, and because ten would not be enough against the powerful, it added the Quarantie and gave the Pregadi power to chastise them, so that neither accuser nor judge fails. Rome and Venice are set against Florence and the cities born like it. Venice's Ten punish without appeal, and Machiavelli approves the arrangement because the Quarantie and the Pregadi ensure there is a judge for the powerful. The editor's observation: the Roman safeguard was the appeal, the Venetian the number of judges.

Cross-reference. The cause said "at other times" is said at I.7, among other places: pochi sempre fanno a modo de' pochi, of accusing a powerful man before eight judges. Guicciardini's reply does not take up the Florentine or Venetian examples.

History. The eight citizens are presumably the Otto di Guardia; Machiavelli does not name the magistracy. The ten citizens of Venice are its Council of Ten. The Pregadi were the Venetian senate; Machiavelli's il Consiglio maggiore may be comparative, larger than the Ten and the Forty, and is not Venice's Great Council (Maggior Consiglio).

1.49.062 notes
Non è adunque maraviglia (veggendo come in Roma, ordinata da sé medesima e da tanti uomini prudenti, surgevano ogni dì nuove cagioni, per le quali si aveva a far nuovi ordini in favor del vivere libero), se nelle altre Città, che hanno più disordinato principio, vi surgano tali difficultà, che le non si possano riordinare mai.
It is no marvel, then (seeing how at Rome, ordered by itself and by so many prudent men, new causes rose every day, for which new orders had to be made in favour of free living), if in the other cities, which have a more disordered beginning, such difficulties rise there that they can never be reordered.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The close draws the comparison again from the Roman side: if Rome, ordered by itself and by so many prudent men, had new causes rising every day that required new orders for free living, it is no marvel that cities with a more disordered beginning meet difficulties such that they can never be reordered. The parenthesis carries the ground, and the conclusion is stated without the rubric's quasi.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not answer the comparison of beginnings at all; his reply Guicciardini .01 uses this chapter, with many others, as evidence that, military discipline set aside, the Roman government was in many parts defective. The consuls he cites are those of I.50, the editor's identification.

The 1554 also reads il principio loro libero (NM-DISC-I49-D003).

¶ 03 hanno difficultà grande a trovar leggi buone per mantenerle libere, non è maraviglia che quelle Città, che hanno avuto il principio loro immediate servo, abbiano, non che difficultà, ma impossibilità ad ordinarsi mai in modo che le possano vivere civilmente e quietamente. "Have great difficulty in finding good laws to maintain them free, it is no marvel that those cities that have had their beginning immediately servile have, not merely difficulty, but impossibility of ever ordering themselves in such a way that they can live civilly and quietly": the rubric's propositions return, the second raised from quasi una impossibilità to impossibilità; non che ... ma, not merely ... but; civilmente, civilly, with vivere civile (glossary). le is the pronoun, the cities.

¶ 03 come si vede che è intervenuto alla Città di Firenze, la quale per aver avuto il principio suo sottoposto all'Imperio romano, ed essendo vivuta sempre sotto governo d'altri, stette un tempo soggetta, e senza pensare a sé medesima. "As one sees [it] has befallen the city of Florence, which, through having had its beginning subjected to the Roman empire, and having lived always under the government of others, stayed a time subject, and without thinking of itself": intervenire, befall (D298); Imperio romano, the Roman empire (D124); sottoposto, subjected, kept apart from soggetta, subject; governo, government (D262). Machiavelli's account of Florence's beginning is I.1.

¶ 03 dipoi, venuta l'occasione di respirare, cominciò a fare i suoi ordini, i quali sendo mescolati con gli antichi, ch'erano tristi, non poterono esser buoni. "Afterwards, the occasion of breathing having come, it began to make its orders, which, being mixed with the ancient ones, which were wicked, could not be good": occasione, occasion (glossary); tristi, wicked (D03).

¶ 03 e così è ita maneggiandosi per dugento anni, che si ha di vera memoria, senza avere mai avuto Stato, per il quale ella possa veramente essere chiamata Repubblica. "And so it has gone on handling itself for two hundred years, of which there is true memory, without ever having had a state by which it can truly be called a republic": maneggiarsi, handle itself, the verb of Rome at .01; Stato, state (D265), here a form of government, set against Repubblica; vera memoria, true memory, and veramente, truly. The 1554 prints the years as CC.

¶ 03 E benché molte volte per suffragj pubblici e liberi si sia dato ampia autorità a pochi cittadini di potere riformarla, non pertanto mai l'hanno ordinata a comune utilità, ma sempre a proposito della parte loro. "And albeit many times, by public and free suffrages, ample authority has been given to a few citizens of being able to reform it, they have for all that never ordered it to common utility, but always to the purpose of their party": benché, albeit (D31); autorità, authority (glossary); riformare, reform (D272); pochi cittadini, "a few citizens", since bare "few" would deny that there were any to speak of; non pertanto, the set adversative, "for all that", kept apart from pertanto, on that account (D98), nondimeno, nonetheless, and pure, yet; parte, of a faction, "party" (D389), where in questa parte (.02) stays "in this part". The 1554 prints ampla. Florence called such grants balìe; Machiavelli uses neither the word nor a date.

¶ 03 il che ha fatto non ordine, ma maggiore disordine in quella Città. "Which has made not order, but greater disorder in that city": ordine / disordine, order / disorder (glossary).

¶ 04 E per venire a qualche esempio particolare, dico come tra l'altre cose che si hanno a considerare da uno ordinatore d'una Repubblica, è, esaminare nelle mani di quali uomini ei ponga l'autorità del sangue contro de' suoi cittadini. "And, to come to some particular example, I say that among the other things that are to be considered by an orderer of a republic is to examine into the hands of which men he sets the authority of blood against his citizens": particolare, particular (D374); avere a, be to (D261), destinative; ordinatore, orderer (glossary); porre, set (D115), kept apart from collocata, placed, at .05; l'autorità del sangue, the authority of blood, the power of capital judgment.

¶ 04 questo era bene ordinato in Roma, perché ei si poteva appellare al Popolo ordinariamente. "This was well ordered at Rome, because one could appeal to the people ordinarily": appellare, appeal (glossary); ordinariamente, ordinarily, by the orders (D151). The appeal to the plebs is mentioned at I.32 and is at issue with the Ten at I.40.

¶ 04 e se pure fusse occorsa cosa importante, dove il differire la esecuzione mediante l'appellazione fusse pericoloso, avevano il rifugio del Dittatore, il quale eseguiva immediate. "And if yet some important thing had occurred, where the deferring of the execution by means of the appeal were dangerous, they had the refuge of the dictator, who executed immediately": pure, yet (D191); occorrere, occur (glossary, the currere family); differire, defer (D334); appellazione, appeal (D348); pericoloso, dangerous, in the root of pericolo, danger, agreeing with il differire: the 1554 has pericolosa, agreeing with la esecuzione, and the 1824 is kept (NM-DISC-I49-D002, D380). immediate, immediately, the rubric's adverb; eseguire, execute, the verb of esecuzione: he carried out the sentence.

¶ 04 al qual rimedio non rifuggivano mai, se non per necessità. "Tn which remedy they never took refuge, except through necessity": rifuggire, take refuge, the verb of rifugio, refuge, just before (D389); "have recourse" stays ricorrere's (D209).

¶ 05 Ma Firenze, e l'altre Città nate nel modo di lei, sendo serve, avevano questa autorità collocata in un forestiero, il quale mandato dal Principe faceva tale ufficio. "But Florence, and the other cities born in the way of it, being servile, had this authority placed in a stranger, who, sent by the prince, did such office": modo, way (glossary); serve, servile, the rubric's word (D388), in the old sense, in servitude, not of manner; collocare, place (D115); forestiero, stranger (D57); Principe, prince (glossary), the ruler who sent the foreign official, not named; ufficio, office (D56). The 1554 prints posta for collocata (NM-DISC-I49-D003).

¶ 05 Quando dipoi vennero in libertà, mantennero questa autorità in un forestiero, il quale chiamavano Capitano. "When afterwards they came into liberty, they maintained this authority in a stranger, whom they called the Captain": mantenere, maintain (glossary); Capitano, the Captain, a Florentine magistrate with criminal jurisdiction (names.md, as NM I.8), not the captain of an army (NM I.29). Machiavelli does not say which magistracy he means, and the note does not settle it.

¶ 05 Il che, per potere essere facilmente corrotto da' cittadini potenti, era cosa perniciosissima. "Which, for being able to be readily corrupted by powerful citizens, was a most pernicious thing": facilmente, readily (D27); corrompere, corrupt (glossary), the verb of corrompersi (.01); potente, powerful (D06); pernizioso, pernicious (glossary). The infinitive's subject is unexpressed, the Captain or the arrangement, and the English leaves it so.

¶ 05 Ma dipoi mutandosi per la mutazione degli Stati questo ordine, crearono otto cittadini che facessero l'uffizio di quel Capitano. "But afterwards, this order changing through the change of states, they created eight citizens who should do the office of that Captain": mutare, mutazione, change (D272), one root; Stati, states (D265), changes of government; otto cittadini, eight citizens, presumably the Otto di Guardia, the Eight (names.md, as FG I.10); Machiavelli does not name the magistracy.

¶ 05 Il quale ordine di cattivo diventò pessimo, per le cagioni che altre volte sono dette, che i pochi furono sempre ministri de' pochi, e de' più potenti. "Which order from bad became very bad, for the causes that have been said at other times, that the few were always ministers of the few, and of the most powerful": cattivo / pessimo, bad / very bad (D03, glossary); ministri, ministers, servants; de' più potenti, the relative superlative, "the most powerful". The 1554 prints ministri di pochi (NM-DISC-I49-D003). The cause is said at I.7, of accusing a powerful man before eight judges: pochi sempre fanno a modo de' pochi.

¶ 05 Da che si è guardata la Città di Vinegia, la quale ha dieci cittadini, che senza appello possono punire ogni cittadino. "From which the city of Venice has guarded itself, which has ten citizens who can punish every citizen without appeal": guardarsi, guard oneself; dieci cittadini, ten citizens, the Venetian Council of Ten (names.md), not the Roman Ten of .01 or the Florentine Ten of I.39; appello, appeal (glossary).

¶ 05 E perché ei non basterebbono a punire i potenti, ancora che n'avessero autorità, v'hanno constituito le Quarantie. "And because they would not be enough to punish the powerful, even though they had authority for it, they have constituted the Quarantie there": bastare, be enough (D205); ancora che, even though (glossary); costituire, constitute (glossary). le Quarantie, the Venetian courts of forty (names.md), kept in Italian; the Florentine Quarantia of FG I.7 shares the name.

¶ 05 e di più hanno voluto che il Consiglio de' Pregadi, che è il Consiglio maggiore, possa gastigarli. "And moreover they have wished that the council of the Pregadi, which is the greater council, can chastise them": volere, wish (D117); Pregadi, the Venetian senate, kept in Italian; il Consiglio maggiore, the greater council: perhaps comparative, greater than the Ten and the Forty; Venice's Maggior Consiglio, the Great Council, was a different body, and the English keeps his words. gastigare, chastise (glossary).

¶ 05 In modo che non vi mancando l'accusatore, non vi manca il Giudice a tenere gli uomini potenti a freno. "In such a way that, the accuser not failing there, the judge does not fail there to hold powerful men reined in": mancare, fail (D206), twice; accusatore, accuser (D159); tenere ... a freno, hold reined in, as NM I.3 tenevano la Nobiltà a freno (D389). freno is the word of Guicciardini's il freno del tribuno (Guicciardini .01).

¶ 06 Non è adunque maraviglia (veggendo come in Roma, ordinata da sé medesima e da tanti uomini prudenti, surgevano ogni dì nuove cagioni, per le quali si aveva a far nuovi ordini in favor del vivere libero). "It is no marvel, then (seeing how at Rome, ordered by itself and by so many prudent men, new causes rose every day, for which new orders had to be made in favour of free living)": adunque, then (D74); ordinata da sé medesima answers the emended per sé medesimo si è r[e]tto of .03; prudenti, prudent (glossary); surgere, rise (D358); avere a in the passive, of necessity, "had to" (D261); vivere libero, free living (glossary). The parenthesis is the witness's.

¶ 06 se nelle altre Città, che hanno più disordinato principio, vi surgano tali difficultà, che le non si possano riordinare mai. "If in the other cities, which have a more disordered beginning, such difficulties rise there that they can never be reordered": disordinato, disordered, and riordinare, reorder (glossary), the family of ordine; surgere rise again (D358). The close returns to the rubric's second case, and states it without quasi.

Guicciardini

Considerazione sul capitolo XLIX

Considerazioni I.49

Se quelle città che hanno avuto il principio libero, come Roma, hanno difficultà a trovare leggi che le mantenghino, quelle che lo hanno immediate servo, ne hanno quasi una impossibilità.

If those cities that have had a free beginning, like Rome, have difficulty in finding laws that may maintain them, those that have it immediately servile have almost an impossibility of it. Canestrini's setting of the chapter title: Machiavelli's rubric with città lower case, mantenghino for mantengano, and a comma for the semicolon. servo, "servile", as in Machiavelli's rubric (D388). It is heading apparatus, not Guicciardini's (D32). Both readers doubted whether ne carries an accent; ne hanno, "have ... of it", is the pronoun, as the 1933 edition and the 1824 rubric read (FG-CONS-I49-D004, D383).

Commentary · 3

Argument. He does not answer the chapter's comparison of free and servile beginnings. He uses the chapter, with many others, as evidence for a judgment he says he has made elsewhere against Machiavelli's opinion: that, military discipline set aside, the Roman government was in many parts defective. Two defects follow. The first is a question without a main verb: what more absurd thing than that one man alone could stop the public business or not let a deliberation of the city have effect, as those consuls did; and though the tribune was a rein on the consuls, against the tribune who wished to make like disorder there was no remedy whatever. The second is the censors' power to deprive Mamercus Aemilius of the senate for so good a work, and, correcting himself upward, the power of one alone. He ends on the remedy: he does not believe Mamercus had any but a law of the people, which one does not read was made, or restoration by the following censors when they chose the senate, which he is not certain they could do, though he believes it. Belief and suspension are kept apart, and the reply ends on the belief, without a stop.

Cross-reference. The consuls are those of I.50, which Guicciardini does not head separately: Titus Quinctius Cincinnatus and Gnaeus Julius Mento, who being disunited avevano ferme tutte le azioni di quella Repubblica, until the senate had recourse to the tribunes; I.50's rubric is that no council or magistrate should be able to stop the actions of the city, and Machiavelli praises the tribunate there as useful a frenare ambition. Guicciardini's fermare le azioni publiche and il freno del tribuno take up both, and his tribune without a remedy may answer Machiavelli's praise of the tribunate, an inference of the editor's. From I.49 he takes Mamercus and the censors, and his errore and defettivo echo Machiavelli's Machiavelli .02: Machiavelli left open whether the historian was defective or the orders not good; Guicciardini does not choose between them in those terms. He finds the error in the censors' power, and leaves the remedy to what he believes. Machiavelli's senza alcun rimedio becomes the question of what remedy Mamercus had. The judgment that the Roman government was, military discipline set aside, in many parts defective is close to his reply to I.4, where Rome's military discipline was eccellentissima, e la virtù sua sostenne tutti gli altri difetti del governo; he does not say that is the place he means.

History. In Livy (4.24) the censors moved Mamercus from his tribe and made him an aerarius; Livy does not say he was removed from the senate. The consuls of I.50 are Livy's T. Quinctius ... Cincinnatus ... et Cn. Iulius Mento (4.26, the Latin Library text, consulted 2026-11-02); the tribunes threatened to have them led to prison if they resisted the senate, and Quinctius named Aulus Postumius Tubertus dictator.

Notes

¶ 01 E questo Discorso e molti altri mostrano quello che io, contro alla opinione dello Scrittore, ho detto in altro luogo. "And this Discourse and many others show that which I, against the opinion of the Writer, have said in another place": il Discorso, the Discourse, his name for Machiavelli's chapter; lo Scrittore, the Writer, Machiavelli, capitalised in Canestrini, as at FG I.29; opinione, opinion (D02). io was doubtful to both readers (lo, to); the pronoun, as the 1933 edition reads (FG-CONS-I49-D004, D383). The other place is not named. The nearest in this edition is his reply to I.4, where military discipline was eccellentissima, e la virtù sua sostenne tutti gli altri difetti del governo; whether he means that reply or another text, the note does not settle.

¶ 01 che posposta la disciplina militare, il governo romano era in molte parti defettivo. "That, military discipline set aside, the Roman government was in many parts defective": posporre, set after, set aside: military discipline is excepted from the judgment, not judged; governo, government (D262); defettivo, defective, Machiavelli's difettivo of the historian (Machiavelli .02), turned on the Roman orders, Machiavelli's other alternative.

¶ 01 perché, che più assurda cosa che fussi in potestà di uno uomo solo fermare le azioni publiche, o non lasciare ch[e] una deliberazione della città abbia effetto, come feciono quelli consuli?. "Because, what more absurd thing than that it should be in the power of one man alone to stop the public business, or not to let a deliberation of the city have effect, as those consuls did?": the question is rhetorical and has no main verb, and the English supplies none. potestà, power (D78); uno uomo solo, one man alone (D172); azioni publiche, public business, what the state does, a third sense beside azioni of an office, "functions" (D348), and of narrated doings, "proceedings" (D358) (D389); deliberazione, deliberation (glossary). Canestrini prints cha, read by both readers with che as the alternative; the 1933 edition reads che: emended (FG-CONS-I49-D001, D381). quelli consuli are the consuls of the next chapter, I.50, Titus Quinctius Cincinnatus and Gnaeus Julius Mento, who sendo disuniti avevano ferme tutte le azioni di quella Repubblica and agreed only in refusing to create a dictator; I.50's rubric is Non debbe uno Consiglio o uno Magistrato potere fermare le azioni della Città. Guicciardini has no heading for I.50; his reply to I.49 takes it in, and the edition's treatment of I.50 is D390.

¶ 01 A' quali se bene vi fu il freno del tribuno, nondimanco al tribuno, quando voleva fare simile disordine, non vi era remedio alcuno. "To whom, although there was the rein of the tribune, nonetheless to the tribune, when he wished to make like disorder, there was no remedy whatever": se bene, although (D31); freno, rein, as Machiavelli's tenere ... a freno (Machiavelli .05) and NM I.3 (D389); in I.50 the senate had recourse to the tribunes, whose office was useful a frenare l'ambizione. nondimanco, nonetheless, the form of nondimeno (D45); simile, like (glossary); disordine, disorder (glossary); remedio, remedy (glossary), Machiavelli's senza alcun rimedio (Machiavelli .02); alcuno after its noun, "whatever" (D349). The tribune's veto is the case; Guicciardini names no tribune.

¶ 01 Fu anche [e]rrore che in potestà de' dua censori fussi privare del senato per sì buona opera Mamerco Emilio cittadino onoratissimo e tanto benemerito della republica. "It was also an error that it should be in the power of the two censors to deprive of the senate, for so good a work, Mamercus Aemilius, a most honoured citizen and so well deserving of the republic": errore, error, Machiavelli's word for the censorship's first form (Machiavelli .02), here turned on the censors' power. Canestrini prints arrore, read by both readers with errore as the alternative; the 1933 edition reads errore: emended (FG-CONS-I49-D001, D381). dua, two; opera, work (D253), his law; sì, so, on which the readers split (A sì, B si), read with the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-I49-D002, D382). benemerito, well deserving, as meritamente is "deservedly" (D374). Machiavelli's privarono Mamerco del Senato is taken as given.

¶ 01 anzi era in potestà di uno solo. "Indeed it was in the power of one alone": anzi correcting upward, "indeed" (D147): not two censors but one could do it. Canestrini's comma before it is a semicolon in the English, which cannot run the clauses together (D65). uno solo, one alone (D172), answering uno uomo solo above. The readers split at the stop after solo; it is supplied from the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-I49-C001, D384).

¶ 01 Né credo che lui vi avessi altro remedio, che o una legge del populo che fussi restituito al senato, la quale non si legge che fussi fatta. "Nor do I believe that he had other remedy for it than either a law of the people that he should be restored to the senate, which one does not read was made": credo, I believe (glossary), negated, and governing both remedies; vi, for it, against the deprivation; populo, people; restituire, restore; non si legge, one does not read, the histories' silence, as Machiavelli's la Istoria non mostra (Machiavelli .02).

¶ 01 o che e' seguenti censori, quando eleggevano il senato, lo restituissino. "Or that the following censors, when they chose the senate, should restore him": e', the plural article (D35), read where both readers doubted the letter (è, i), as the 1933 edition reads (FG-CONS-I49-D004, D383); eleggere, choose (glossary), here the censors' revision of the senate's roll.

¶ 01 il che anche non sono certo potessino fare, benché lo credo. "Which too I am not certain they could do, albeit I believe it": anche adds this doubt to the unrecorded law; non sono certo, I am not certain, a suspension, set against non è dubio, certo ("has no doubt", register); benché, albeit (D31); lo credo, I believe it. The readers doubted il (el) and lo (io), both read with the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-I49-D004, D383); with lo the belief is in their power to restore him, with io it would be the bare act. The reply ends without a stop, and so does the English (D40, D384): one reader saw a faint trace, the other none.
