A Conversation with Machiavelli · Pair 20 of 39

Which is more ungrateful, either a people or a prince Discourses I.29, and Guicciardini's reply

Editor's summary Machiavelli argues that ingratitude arises from avarice, which has no excuse, or from suspicion, which merits some, and that peoples, having less cause to suspect, are less ungrateful than princes. Guicciardini adds ignorance and envy, and does not believe a people the less ungrateful. He believes that a people, as a rule, suspects more lightly, though he grants that princes strike harder, and that through ignorance and envy it is beyond comparison more ungrateful. He says Rome erred much more than the Discourse allows, marvels that Scipio's case is excused as suspicion, and calls very far from the truth the claim that ingratitude is useful to liberty in a republic not yet corrupted: distrusting the good is a lesser error, he grants, but no good where there is no need to choose.

NM-DISC.1.29

Capitolo XXIX

Quale sia più ingrato, o un Popolo, o un Principe.

Which is more ungrateful, either a people or a prince.

Italian · 1824
English
1.29.01
Egli mi pare a proposito della soprascritta materia da discorrere quale usi con maggiori esempj questa ingratitudine, o un Popolo, o un Principe. E per disputare meglio questa parte dico: come questo vizio della ingratitudine nasce o dalla avarizia, o dal sospetto.
It seems to me to the purpose of the above-written matter to discourse which uses this ingratitude with greater examples, either a people or a prince. And to dispute this part better I say that this vice of ingratitude arises either from avarice or from suspicion.
Commentary · 1

Argument. The question promised at the end of I.28: which uses ingratitude with greater examples, a people or a prince. Machiavelli disputes it by dividing the cause. The vice arises from one of two things, avarice or suspicion, and the chapter keeps the two apart to the last sentence.

1.29.02
Perché quando o un Popolo o un Principe ha mandato fuori un suo Capitano in una espedizione importante, dove quel Capitano, vincendola, ne abbia acquistata assai gloria, quel Principe o quel Popolo è tenuto allo incontro a premiarlo; e se in cambio di premio, o ei lo disonora, o ei l'offende, mosso dalla avarizia, non volendo ritenuto da questa cupidità satisfargli, fa uno errore che non ha scusa, anzi si tira dietro una infamia eterna. Pure si trovano molti Principi che ci peccano, e Cornelio Tacito dice con questa sentenza la cagione: Proclivius est injuriae, quam beneficio vicem exsolvere, quia gratia oneri, ultio in quaestu habetur. Ma quando ei non lo premia, o a dir meglio l'offende, non mosso da avarizia, ma da sospetto, allora merita e il Popolo e il Principe qualche scusa.
For when either a people or a prince has sent out a captain of his on an important expedition, where that captain, winning it, has acquired much glory from it, that prince or that people is bound in return to reward him; and if in place of a reward either he dishonours him or he offends him, moved by avarice, not wishing, held back by this cupidity, to satisfy him, he makes an error that has no excuse, indeed draws after him an eternal infamy. Yet many princes are found who sin in it, and Cornelius Tacitus tells the cause with this sentence: Proclivius est injuriae, quam beneficio vicem exsolvere, quia gratia oneri, ultio in quaestu habetur. But when he does not reward him, or, to say better, offends him, moved not by avarice but by suspicion, then both the people and the prince merit some excuse.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The two causes are graded. When a people or a prince has sent a captain on an important expedition and he has won glory, the one who sent him is bound to reward him. To dishonour or offend him instead out of avarice is an error "that has no excuse", indeed an eternal infamy; yet many princes commit it, and Tacitus gives the reason, that repaying an injury is readier than repaying a benefit, since gratitude is a burden and revenge a gain. To fail to reward him, or rather to offend him, out of suspicion earns "some excuse", for people and prince alike. The distinction between no excuse and some excuse carries the rest of the chapter.

History. The Tacitus is Histories 4.3, written of the Flavian victors in the last days of 69, who punished Capua for its loyalty to Vitellius and gave nothing to Tarracina, which had suffered for their side.

1.29.03
E di queste ingratitudini usate per tal cagione se ne leggono assai; perché quello Capitano, il quale virtuosamente ha acquistato uno Imperio al suo Signore, superando i nimici, e riempiendo sé di gloria, e gli suoi soldati di ricchezze, di necessità e con i soldati suoi, e con i nimici, e con i sudditi proprj di quel Principe acquista tanta riputazione, che quella vittoria non può sapere di buono a quel Signore che lo ha mandato. E perché la natura degli uomini è ambiziosa e sospettosa, e non sa porre modo a nissuna sua fortuna, è impossibile che quel sospetto, che subito nasce nel Principe dopo la vittoria di quel suo Capitano, non sia da quel medesimo accresciuto per qualche suo modo o termine usato insolentemente. Talché il Principe non può pensare ad altro che assicurarsene; e per fare questo pensa o di farlo morire, o di torgli la riputazione, che egli si ha guadagnato nel suo esercito, e nei suoi Popoli, e con ogn'industria mostrare che quella vittoria è nata non per la virtù di quello, ma per fortuna, o per viltà dei nimici, o per prudenza degli altri Capitani che sono stati seco in tale fazione.
And of these ingratitudes used for such a cause many are read; for that captain who has virtuously acquired an empire for his lord, overcoming the enemies, and filling himself with glory and his soldiers with riches, of necessity, both with his own soldiers and with the enemies and with the subjects proper to that prince, acquires so much reputation that that victory cannot taste good to that lord who has sent him. And because the nature of men is ambitious and suspicious, and does not know how to set a measure to any fortune of its own, it is impossible that that suspicion which arises at once in the prince after the victory of that captain of his should not be increased by that same man, through some way or bearing of his used insolently. So that the prince cannot think of anything other than securing himself of him; and to do this he thinks either of having him die, or of taking away from him the reputation which he has gained for himself in his army and in his peoples, and, with every industry, of showing that that victory arose not through his virtù but through fortune, or through the cowardice of the enemies, or through the prudence of the other captains who were with him in such an action.
Commentary · 1

Argument. Why suspicion follows victory. A captain who has won an empire for his lord gains, of necessity, so much reputation with his own soldiers, the enemy and the prince's subjects that the victory turns sour to the prince. Men's nature is ambitious and suspicious and sets no measure to its fortune: the prince's suspicion arises at once, and the captain's own insolence is bound to feed it. So the prince can think only of securing himself, either by having the captain die or by taking his reputation away and showing that the victory came from fortune, the enemy's cowardice or the other captains' prudence. The chain is offered as necessity, and the excuse granted at .02 rests on it.

1.29.04
Poi che Vespasiano sendo in Giudea fu dichiarato dal suo esercito Imperadore, Antonio Primo, che si trovava con un altro esercito in Illiria, prese le parti sue, e ne venne in Italia contro a Vitellio, il quale regnava a Roma, e virtuosissimamente ruppe due eserciti Vitelliani, e occupò Roma: talché Muziano mandato da Vespasiano, trovò, per la virtù d'Antonio, acquistato il tutto, e vinta ogni difficultà. Il premio che Antonio ne riportò, fu che Muziano gli tolse subito la ubbidienza dello esercito, e a poco a poco lo ridusse in Roma senza alcuna autorità; talché Antonio ne andò a trovare Vespasiano, il quale era ancora in Asia, dal quale fu in modo ricevuto, che in breve tempo ridotto in nessun grado, quasi disperato morì. E di questi esempj ne sono piene le Istorie.
After Vespasian, being in Judaea, was declared emperor by his army, Antonius Primus, who was with another army in Illyria, took his part, and came thence into Italy against Vitellius, who was reigning at Rome, and most virtuously broke two Vitellian armies and occupied Rome; so that Mucianus, sent by Vespasian, found, through the virtù of Antonius, the whole acquired and every difficulty vanquished. The reward that Antonius carried away from it was that Mucianus at once took away from him the obedience of the army, and little by little brought him, in Rome, to [being] without any authority; so that Antonius went to find Vespasian, who was still in Asia, by whom he was received in such a way that, in a short time brought to no degree, he died almost in despair. And the histories are full of these examples.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The first proof, from antiquity. Antonius Primus took Vespasian's part, broke two Vitellian armies and occupied Rome, so that Mucianus, sent by Vespasian, found the work done. His reward: Mucianus took the army from him and left him in Rome without authority, and Vespasian received him so that he died, in Machiavelli's account, almost in despair.

History. Tacitus, Histories 3-4. Antonius led the Danube legions into Italy in the autumn of 69, won the second battle of Bedriacum near Cremona, and took Rome in December. Mucianus, governor of Syria, arrived after and took control; Antonius went to Vespasian at Alexandria and was received less warmly than he had hoped, though not with hostility (Histories 4.80). Tacitus does not record his death; he is known to have lived on into old age. Vespasian was in Egypt, not Asia.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini does not deny the story; he denies that it shows a prince's ingratitude. Mucianus is a fellow servant, not the prince, and what Vespasian failed to do arose not from suspicion but from his dislike of Antonius' insolent nature and, much more, his respect for Mucianus Guicciardini .04.

1.29.05
Nei nostri tempi, ciascuno che al presente vive sa con quanta industria e virtù Consalvo Ferrante, militando nel Regno di Napoli contro ai Francesi per Ferrando re di Ragona, conquistasse e vincesse quel regno, e come per premio di vittoria ne riportò, che Ferrando si partì da Ragona, e venuto a Napoli, in prima gli levò la ubbidienza delle genti d'arme, e dipoi gli tolse le Fortezze, ed appresso ne lo menò seco in Spagna, dove poco tempo dopo inonorato morì. È tanto dunque naturale questo sospetto nei Principi, che non se ne possono difendere, ed è impossibile ch'egli usino gratitudine a quelli, che con vittoria hanno fatto sotto le insegne loro grandi acquisti.
In our times, each one who lives at present knows with how much industry and virtù Consalvo Ferrante, soldiering in the Kingdom of Naples against the French for Ferdinand, king of Aragon, conquered and won that kingdom, and how, for the reward of victory, he carried away from it that Ferdinand departed from Aragon and, having come to Naples, first removed from him the obedience of the men-at-arms, and afterwards took away from him the fortresses, and thereupon led him away with him into Spain, where after a little time he died unhonoured. So natural, then, is this suspicion in princes, that they cannot defend themselves from it, and it is impossible that they should use gratitude to those who, with victory, have made great acquisitions under their ensigns.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The second proof, from "our times". Consalvo won the Kingdom of Naples for Ferdinand of Aragon; Ferdinand came to Naples, took the men-at-arms and then the fortresses from him, and took him back to Spain, where he died unhonoured. Machiavelli's conclusion lands on the prince: the suspicion is so natural that princes cannot defend themselves from it, and cannot use gratitude to those who have won great acquisitions under their ensigns.

History. Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, the Great Captain, defeated the French at Cerignola and the Garigliano in 1503 and governed Naples as viceroy. Ferdinand came in the autumn of 1506, left in June 1507 taking Gonzalo with him, and did not employ him again; Gonzalo died at Granada in 1515.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini answers that Ferdinand remunerated Gonzalo with states, had just suspicion of him, and that Consalvo lived on in Spain rich and most honoured Guicciardini .04.

1.29.06
E da quello che non si difende un Principe, non è miracolo né cosa degna di maggiore considerazione, se un Popolo non se ne difende. Perché avendo una Città, che vive libera duoi fini, l'uno l'acquistare, l'altro il mantenersi libera, conviene che nell'una cosa e nell'altra per troppo amore erri. Quanto agli errori nello acquistare, se ne dirà, nel luogo suo. Quanto agli errori per mantenersi libera, sono intra gli altri questi, di offendere quei cittadini che la dovrebbe premiare, avere sospetto di quelli in cui si dovrebbe confidare. E benché questi modi in una Repubblica venuta alla corruzione siano cagione di grandi mali, e che molte volte piuttosto la viene alla tirannide, come intervenne a Roma di Cesare, che per forza si tolse quello che la ingratitudine gli negava; nondimeno in una Repubblica non corrotta, sono cagione di gran beni, e fanno che la ne vive libera più, mantenendosi per paura di punizione gli uomini migliori e meno ambiziosi.
And from what a prince does not defend himself, it is no miracle, nor a thing worthy of greater consideration, if a people does not defend itself from it. For a city that lives free having two ends, the one to acquire, the other to maintain itself free, it is fitting that in the one thing and in the other it should err through too much love. As to the errors in acquiring, it will be said of them in its place. As to the errors for maintaining itself free, they are, among the others, these: to offend those citizens whom it ought to reward, to have suspicion of those in whom one ought to confide. And albeit these ways, in a republic come to corruption, are the cause of great ills, and many times it rather comes to tyranny, as befell at Rome with Caesar, who by force took for himself what ingratitude denied him; nonetheless, in a republic not corrupted, they are the cause of great goods, and make it live the more free for it, men maintaining themselves better and less ambitious through fear of punishment.
Commentary · 3

Argument. The people's case follows from the prince's: what a prince cannot defend himself from, it is no wonder that a people cannot. A free city has two ends, to acquire and to maintain itself free, and out of too much love it must err in both. Among the errors for liberty are to offend the citizens it ought to reward and to suspect those it ought to trust. Their effects are double. In a corrupted republic they are the cause of great ills, and often bring it to tyranny, as with Caesar, who took by force what ingratitude denied him. In a republic not corrupted they are the cause of great goods: the city lives the more free, and men keep themselves better and less ambitious for fear of punishment. Neither edge cancels the other, and the condition, corrupted or not, decides which holds.

History. Caesar's grievance in 50-49 BC was the Senate's demand that he lay down his command before standing for a second consulship; Machiavelli reads his seizure of power as taking by force what ingratitude denied him.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini takes up this second edge, as he reads it, at the close of his reply and calls it very far from the truth Guicciardini .06.

1.29.07
Vero è che infra tutti i Popoli che mai ebbero Imperio, per le cagioni di sopra discorse, Roma fu la meno ingrata. Perché della sua ingratitudine si può dire che non ci sia altro esempio che quello di Scipione; perché Coriolano e Camillo furono fatti esuli per ingiuria, che l'uno e l'altro aveva fatto alla Plebe. Ma all'uno non fu perdonato, per aversi sempre riserbato contro al Popolo l'animo nimico; l'altro non solamente fu richiamato, ma per tutto il tempo della sua vita adorato come Principe.
It is true that, among all the peoples that ever had empire, for the causes discoursed above, Rome was the least ungrateful. For of its ingratitude it can be said that there is no other example than that of Scipio; for Coriolanus and Camillus were made exiles for an injury that the one and the other had done to the plebs. But the one was not pardoned, for having always reserved against the people a hostile spirit; the other was not only recalled, but for the whole time of his life adored as a prince.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Rome is the least ungrateful of all peoples that held empire, "for the causes discoursed above", I.28's less cause to suspect. Of its ingratitude, it can be said, there is no example but Scipio's. Coriolanus and Camillus are set aside: each was exiled for an injury he had done to the plebs, and Camillus was recalled and adored as a prince for the rest of his life.

History. Coriolanus went into exile in 491 BC and led the Volsci against Rome (Livy 2.35-40). Camillus was exiled in 391 over the spoils of Veii and recalled to save Rome from the Gauls (Livy 5.32, 5.46-55).

Cross-reference. Guicciardini puts Camillus back among Rome's examples, with Fabius Maximus, Cicero, Metellus and Rutilius Guicciardini .05.

1.29.08
Ma l'ingratitudine usata a Scipione nacque da un sospetto che i cittadini cominciarono avere di lui, che degli altri non si era avuto, il quale nacque dalla grandezza del nimico che Scipione aveva vinto, dalla riputazione che gli aveva data la vittoria di sì lunga e pericolosa guerra, dalla celerità di essa, dai favori che la gioventù, la prudenza, e le altre sue memorabili virtù gli acquistavano. Le quali cose furono tante, che, non che altro, i Magistrati di Roma temevano della sua autorità; la qual cosa spiaceva agli uomini savj, come cosa inconsueta in Roma. E parve tanto straordinario il vivere suo, che Catone Prisco, riputato santo, fu il primo a fargli contra, e a dire che una Città non si poteva chiamare libera, dove era un cittadino che fusse temuto dai Magistrati. Talché se il Popolo di Roma seguì in questo caso la opinione di Catone, merita quella scusa che di sopra ho detto meritare quelli Popoli e quelli Principi, che per sospetto sono ingrati.
But the ingratitude used towards Scipio arose from a suspicion that the citizens began to have of him, which had not been had of the others, which arose from the greatness of the enemy that Scipio had vanquished, from the reputation that the victory of so long and dangerous a war had given him, from its celerity, from the favours that the youth, the prudence and his other memorable virtù acquired for him. Which things were so many that, not to speak of others, the magistrates of Rome feared his authority; which thing displeased wise men, as a thing unaccustomed in Rome. And his living seemed so extraordinary that Cato Priscus, deemed holy, was the first to act against him, and to say that a city could not be called free where there was a citizen who should be feared by the magistrates. So that if the people of Rome followed the opinion of Cato in this case, it merits that excuse which I have said above those peoples and those princes merit who are ungrateful through suspicion.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Scipio's case told as suspicion, with its causes listed: the greatness of the enemy he had vanquished, the reputation of the victory in so long and dangerous a war, its speed, and the favour his youth, prudence and virtù won him. The magistrates feared his authority, which displeased wise men as a thing unaccustomed in Rome, and Cato, deemed holy, was the first to act against him, saying that a city could not be called free where a citizen was feared by the magistrates. The excuse is conditional: if the people followed Cato, it merits the excuse of .02.

History. The attacks on the Scipios came in 187-184 BC, after the war with Antiochus; Africanus withdrew to Liternum and died there, and his brother Lucius Scipio Asiaticus was condemned and fined (Livy 38.50-60). Livy names Cato among those who pressed them.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini marvels that the case is excused: in his account Scipio's greatness was not fearsome, wise men were never displeased by it, and Cato acted from enmity or his hostility to the nobility, as he showed by pursuing Asiaticus after Scipio's death Guicciardini .05.

1.29.09
Conchiudendo adunque questo discorso, dico; che usandosi questo vizio della ingratitudine o per avarizia o per sospetto, si vedrà come i Popoli non mai per l'avarizia l'usarono, e per sospetto assai manco che i Principi, avendo meno cagione di sospettare, come di sotto si dirà.
Concluding, then, this discourse, I say that, this vice of ingratitude being used either through avarice or through suspicion, it will be seen how peoples never used it through avarice, and through suspicion much less than princes, having less cause to suspect, as will be said below.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The conclusion is a prospect, not a proof: it "will be seen" that peoples never used ingratitude through avarice, and through suspicion much less than princes, "having less cause to suspect", the phrase of I.28; the reason is deferred, "as will be said below". The answer to the rubric's question is given for each cause separately.

Cross-reference. Guicciardini's reply keeps the division, adds ignorance and malignity rooted in envy, and does not believe a people less ungrateful than a prince Guicciardini .01. Through suspicion he believes a people suspects more lightly, though he confesses that princes bite harder Guicciardini .02; through ignorance and envy he believes it beyond comparison more ungrateful Guicciardini .03. His "as will be said below", of Rome's errors, repeats Machiavelli's closing words Guicciardini .02.

Egli mi pare a proposito della soprascritta materia. "It seems to me to the purpose of the above-written matter": soprascritto, above-written (glossary, I.16), the matter of I.28; egli, the pleonastic subject, not rendered.

da discorrere. "To discourse": discorrere, discourse (D01), the first of the chapter's currere words; it returns at per le cagioni di sopra discorse (.07) and in questo discorso (.09). The 1554 edition reads di discorrere.

quale usi con maggiori esempj questa ingratitudine. "Which uses this ingratitude with greater examples": usare, use, the chapter's verb for ingratitude (ingratitudini usate, usino gratitudine, l'ingratitudine usata, usandosi, l'usarono), kept as one English verb. maggiori, greater: more and weightier examples.

E per disputare meglio questa parte dico: come questo vizio della ingratitudine nasce. "And to dispute this part better I say that this vice of ingratitude arises": disputare, dispute, to argue a question; come after dico, that, introducing the claim; nascere, arise (D04).

dalla avarizia, o dal sospetto. "From avarice or from suspicion": the chapter's two causes, held as "avarice" and "suspicion" throughout and into Guicciardini's reply. sospetto, suspicion (glossary, I.28).

ha mandato fuori un suo Capitano in una espedizione importante. "Has sent out a captain of his on an important expedition": Capitano, captain, the commander of an army.

vincendola, ne abbia acquistata assai gloria. "Winning it, has acquired much glory from it": assai, much: of degree, where D254's "very" cannot take a noun; a scoped exception, recorded in the glossary (D312), and held at assai manco, "much less" (.09). gloria, glory (glossary). The subjunctive marks the case as general; English cannot mark it without a modal that would hedge it. The 1554 edition reads uincendo, without the object, and acquistato, unagreed; escalated as a word at a letter the scans break and kept (D304; NM-DISC-I29-D003).

è tenuto allo incontro a premiarlo. "Is bound in return to reward him": allo incontro, in return, of what is owed back; kept apart from al rincontro, "on the other side" (D24), which is adversative; premiare, premio, reward (glossary).

gli tolse subito la ubbidienza dello esercito. il quale era ancora in Asia. "Who was still in Asia": Vespasian was at Alexandria in Egypt; Machiavelli's "Asia" is his. Tacitus (Histories 4.80) has Antonius go to Vespasian and be received less warmly than he hoped; he says nothing of a death in despair, and Antonius in fact lived into old age.

le genti d'arme. "The men-at-arms": the heavy cavalry, the established English term for the compound; genti alone is "troops" (glossary, I.23).

The closing se ne difende keeps its ne, "defend itself from it", the same suspicion.

conviene che nell'una cosa e nell'altra per troppo amore erri. "It is fitting that in the one thing and in the other it should err through too much love": conviene, it is fitting (D85), of what follows necessarily, not of what is proper; the error is an excess of love, for empire and for liberty.

English
1.29.01
Se bene la ingratitudine si usa qualche volta per avarizia, qualche volta per sospetto, si usa anche per altra cagione, come è per ignoranza e per malignità, che ha per radice la invidia; e considerando bene tutte queste origini sue, non credo ne sia più alieno uno populo che uno principe, anzi tutto il contrario. Parliamo, come dice lo Scrittore, di quella ingratitudine che si usa contro a coloro che si sono maneggiati in faccende publiche, la quale è in dua modi: o non gli premiando come meritano, o offendendogli in cambio del remunerargli, questa è più perniziosa, quella è più frequente; e nell'una e l'altra chi esaminerà diligentemente troverà il populo non errare manco che 'l principe, anzi a giudicio mio più. E prima, quanto alla avarizia, la quale rarissime volte causa ingratitudine in altro che in remunerare, credo che se poco ci pecca il populo, il quale per instinto suo è raro e piccolo remuneratore, che anche non molto ci pecchi il principe, perché ha infinite occasioni di remunerare gli uomini sanza toccare la borsa sua, e di cose ancora che non ritengono in sé, ma sono soliti dare agli altri. E sanza dubio, se bene e' principi lascino spesso per avarizia o per essere di natura ingrati, che è un'altra cagione che si può aggiugnere alle preallegate, di premiare chi ha bene servito, sono anche, a comparazione delle remunerazioni de' populi, infiniti gli esempli de' principi che hanno remunerato. Né mi si alleghi in questa parte e' magistrati che il populo spesso dà successivamente a' suoi cittadini quando si sono portati bene, perché lo fa più per opinione o speranza di esserne bene servito, che per gratitudine dei beneficii ricevuti.
Although ingratitude is used sometimes through avarice, sometimes through suspicion, it is used also for another cause, as it is through ignorance and through malignity, which has envy for its root; and considering well all these origins of it, I do not believe a people is more alien from it than a prince, on the contrary, wholly the contrary. Let us speak, as the Writer says, of that ingratitude which is used against those who have had the handling of public affairs, which is in two ways: either not rewarding them as they merit, or offending them in place of remunerating them, this is more pernicious, that is more frequent; and in the one and the other whoever will examine diligently will find the people not to err less than the prince, indeed, in my judgment, more. And first, as to avarice, which very seldom causes ingratitude in anything other than in remunerating, I believe that, if the people, which by its instinct is a rare and small remunerator, sins little in it, that the prince too does not sin much in it, because he has infinite occasions to remunerate men without touching his purse, and with things, too, which they do not retain in themselves, but are accustomed to give to others. And without doubt, although princes often omit, through avarice or through being ungrateful by nature, which is another cause that can be added to the aforesaid ones, to reward whoever has served well, there are also, in comparison with the remunerations of peoples, infinite examples of princes who have remunerated. Nor let there be alleged to me in this part the magistracies that the people often gives successively to its citizens when they have borne themselves well, because it does it more through opinion or hope of being well served by them than through gratitude for the benefits received.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Guicciardini grants Machiavelli's two causes, avarice and suspicion, and adds two: ignorance, and malignity rooted in envy. Considering all four, he does not believe a people more alien from ingratitude than a prince; on the contrary. He keeps to Machiavelli's ground, men who have served in public affairs, and to his two forms, not rewarding and offending. Of these the second is more pernicious and the first more frequent, and in both, he says, whoever examines will find the people erring no less than the prince, and "in my judgment" more.

Note. Then avarice, which very seldom causes ingratitude except in not rewarding. If the people, by its instinct a rare and small remunerator, sins little in it, he believes the prince too does not sin much, since a prince can reward without touching his purse, with things he is accustomed to give away. Princes do often omit to reward, through avarice or through being ungrateful by nature, a further cause he adds in passing; but "without doubt" there are infinite examples of princes who have rewarded, compared with the rewards of peoples. The magistracies a people gives its citizens are not to be counted, since it gives them more in hope of good service than in gratitude.

Cross-reference. Machiavelli's division is the frame Machiavelli .01; his two acts, not rewarding and offending, are restated in his words Machiavelli .02. Where Machiavelli said it will be seen that peoples never used ingratitude through avarice Machiavelli .09, Guicciardini believes that, if the people sins little in it, the prince does not sin much either; his "without doubt" is about the princes' rewards.

1.29.02
Quanto al sospetto, credo che per lo ordinario molto più leggermente e con minori fondamenti insospettisca uno populo che uno principe, perché usa manco diligenza e ha minore modo di riscontrare una calunnia falsa, e come comincia a insospettire, disonora sanza rispetto di chi ha sospetto, sanza usarci dentro arte o circumspezione alcuna; dove uno principe che non sia al tutto imprudente va qualche volta simulando, e se si astiene di confidarsi di lui in quelle cose che gli potrebbono fare periculo, non si guarda dalle altre, avendo avvertenza di non lo disperare. E certo infiniti sono gli esempli e delle republiche e de' principi che per sospetto hanno usato ingratitudine; e se errò in questo Roma manco che le altre republiche, ci errò molto più che non dice il Discorso, come di sotto si dirà; né gli esempli di Cammillo e di Scipione sono escusabili per quella via. Confesso bene che in questo caso sono più gagliardi e' morsi de' principi, perché più facilmente assai vengono al coltello e alle esecuzioni forti, che non fa il populo.
As to suspicion, I believe that by the ordinary a people grows suspicious much more lightly and with lesser foundations than a prince, because it uses less diligence and has less way to check a false calumny, and as soon as it begins to grow suspicious, it dishonours without respect him of whom it has suspicion, without using in it any art or circumspection; whereas a prince who is not wholly imprudent goes sometimes simulating, and if he abstains from confiding in him in those things that could do him danger, he does not guard himself from the others, having care not to drive him to despair. And certainly infinite are the examples, both of republics and of princes, which have used ingratitude through suspicion; and if Rome erred in this less than the other republics, it erred in it much more than the Discourse says, as will be said below; nor are the examples of Camillus and of Scipio excusable by that path. I confess indeed that in this case the bites of princes are harder, because they come much more readily to the knife and to strong executions than the people does.
Commentary · 2

Argument. Suspicion. He believes a people, as a rule, grows suspicious much more lightly and on lesser grounds than a prince, because it uses less diligence and has less means to check a false calumny, and once suspicious it dishonours without regard or art. A prince who is not wholly imprudent at times dissembles: he withholds trust where it would be dangerous and keeps it elsewhere, taking care not to drive the man to despair. He has no doubt that examples of ingratitude through suspicion are infinite, in republics and princes; if Rome erred in it less than other republics, it erred much more than the Discourse says. The examples of Camillus and Scipio cannot be excused by that path. Then a real concession: in this case princes' bites are harder, since they come more readily to the knife.

Cross-reference. Set beside Machiavelli's mechanism, suspicion arising "at once" and impossible not to grow Machiavelli .03, Guicciardini's prudent prince manages the suspicion he feels. The excuse Machiavelli granted Rome through suspicion, if its people followed Cato's opinion Machiavelli .08, is the path Guicciardini closes. His "as will be said below" repeats Machiavelli's closing words Machiavelli .09; compare also Machiavelli's peoples who "bite more fiercely", in I.28.

Quanto agli altri duo capi della ignoranza e della malignità fondata in su la invidia, credo che sanza comparazione il populo sia più ingrato, perché, e per essere distratti gli uomini a varie faccende, e per altre cagioni, manco intende, manco distingue, e manco cognosce che non fa uno principe, e quanto alla invidia, cade più facilmente negli uomini populari, a' quali ogni grandezza punto eminente o di nobilità o di ricchezze o di virtù o di reputazione è ordinariamente molesta, né cosa alcuna dispiace loro che vedere altri cittadini che abbino più qualità di loro, e questi sempre desiderano abbassare. Non interviene così in uno principe, che non gli accade avere invidia a chi è inferiore di lui, e però dove la grandezza degli altri non sia tale che gli generi sospetto, non gli sarà molesta, né la batterà per questa malignità.
As to the other two heads, of ignorance and of malignity founded on envy, I believe that beyond comparison the people is more ungrateful, because, both through men's being distracted to various affairs and through other causes, it understands less, distinguishes less and knows less than a prince does, and as to envy, it falls more readily in popular men, to whom every greatness at all eminent, whether of nobility or of riches or of virtù or of reputation, is ordinarily irksome, nor does anything displease them [more] than to see other citizens who have more quality than they, and these they always desire to lower. It does not befall so in a prince, for it does not happen to him to have envy of whoever is inferior to him, and therefore, where the greatness of the others is not such that it generates suspicion in him, it will not be irksome to him, nor will he beat it through this malignity.
Commentary · 1

Argument. Ignorance and envy, the two heads he added. Here he believes the people beyond comparison more ungrateful. Distracted by its affairs, it understands, distinguishes and knows less than a prince; and envy falls more readily in popular men, to whom any eminent greatness, of nobility, riches, virtù or reputation, is ordinarily irksome, and who always wish to lower those who have more quality than they. A prince has no envy of inferiors; therefore, unless another's greatness breeds suspicion in him, it will not irk him and he will not strike at it from malignity. The exception keeps Machiavelli's case of suspicion in its place.

1.29.04
Restano gli esempli allegati nel Discorso; perché quello che fece Muziano contro Antonio Primo non è esemplo di ingratitudine di uno principe verso il suddito; ma di dua che vivono sotto uno principe, de' quali ciascuno cerca tirare a sé proprio la reputazione delle cose fatte; e il non vi avere provisto Vespasiano non nacque da sospetto che avessi di Antonio Primo, ma dal dispiacergli la natura insolente di Antonio, e molto più dal rispetto grande che aveva a Muziano. Non serve ancora al discorso nostro lo esemplo di Consalvo Ferrante, al quale il re Don Ferrando non si potette chiamare ingrato, avendolo remunerato in modo che di povero cavaliere aveva Stati per trentamila scudi; e se gli tolse il governo del regno, ne fu causa che per molte ragioni ebbe giusto sospetto di lui per le discordie che nella successione del regno potevano nascere da lui e gli eredi del re Filippo; e inoltre è certo che Consalvo governava il regno con tanta autorità, che al re non ne restava altro che il nome regio. In modo che non si chiama ingrato quello principe che provede che chi l'ha beneficato non lo possa offendere, e di godersi lui quello che ha acquistato per mezzo suo, facendolo con quello modo che fece il re Don Ferrando, perché Consalvo visse di poi sempre in Spagna ricco e onoratissimo tra gli altri grandi.
There remain the examples alleged in the Discourse; because what Mucianus did against Antonius Primus is not an example of the ingratitude of a prince towards the subject, but of two who live under one prince, of whom each seeks to draw to himself the reputation of the things done; and Vespasian's not having provided in it arose not from suspicion that he had of Antonius Primus, but from Antonius' insolent nature displeasing him, and much more from the great respect he had to Mucianus. Nor does the example of Consalvo Ferrante serve our discourse either, whom King Don Ferdinand could not be called ungrateful to, having remunerated him in such a way that from a poor knight he had states of thirty thousand scudi; and if he took from him the government of the kingdom, the cause of it was that for many reasons he had just suspicion of him, because of the discords that could arise, in the succession to the kingdom, from him and the heirs of King Philip; and besides, it is certain that Consalvo governed the kingdom with so much authority that nothing was left to the king but the royal name. In such a way that that prince is not called ungrateful who provides that whoever has benefited him cannot offend him, and to enjoy himself what he has acquired by his means, doing it in the way that King Don Ferdinand did, because Consalvo lived afterwards always in Spain, rich and most honoured among the other great [men].
Commentary · 3

Argument. The examples. Antonius Primus is not a case of a prince's ingratitude to a subject but of two men under one prince, each drawing the credit to himself; Vespasian's failure to act arose not from suspicion but from dislike of Antonius' insolence and, much more, respect for Mucianus. Consalvo is no better: Ferdinand rewarded him with states, took the government from him for just suspicion over the succession, and, he says it is certain, Consalvo ruled with such authority that the king had only the royal name. A prince is not ungrateful who prevents his benefactor from harming him and keeps what he has acquired through him, provided he does it as Ferdinand did: Consalvo lived on in Spain, rich and most honoured.

History. Philip I of Castile, Ferdinand's son-in-law and his rival for the government of Castile, died in September 1506; Gonzalo was suspected of leaning to Philip's side, and with the succession open a viceroy of his standing was a danger. Ferdinand came to Naples in November 1506 and left in June 1507 with Gonzalo, who never again held a command and died in 1515. Machiavelli says he died "unhonoured"; Guicciardini, that he lived "rich and most honoured among the other great".

Cross-reference. Against Machiavelli's two proofs Machiavelli .04 Machiavelli .05. Guicciardini disputes chiefly what the events show, though against Machiavelli's "unhonoured" he sets a Consalvo rich and most honoured. His rule, that a prince who secures himself against his benefactor is not ungrateful if he does it as Ferdinand did, rewarding and honouring him, stands against Machiavelli's prince who can think only of securing himself Machiavelli .03.

1.29.05
Quanto agli esempli della ingratitudine di Roma, se in quella se ne truova manco che nelle altre republiche, ne è causa che ebbe il governo più ordinato che molte altre, benché anche quella non manca degli esempli suoi, come in Cammillo, lo esilio del quale si può male scusare, come in Fabio Massimo, che per avere preso il vero modo di difendere Roma da Annibale, fu con tanta ignominia fatto pari al maestro de' cavalieri; come in Cicerone oppressore della conjurazione di Catilina, come in Metello, Publio Rutilio, e in molti altri uomini clari e innocenti, che furono in varii tempi condannati o mandati in esilio. E mi maraviglio che il Discorso scusi il caso di Scipione, volendo attribuire al sospetto quello che nacque meramente da invidia e da ignoranza; perché nel tempo suo Roma si reggeva in modo che non aveva da temere di alcuno cittadino, né la grandezza di Scipione fu spaventosa, non essendo fondata in su sètte, né ség[u]ito di uomini, ma in quella autorità che gli dava nella città la virtù ed e' meriti suoi. La quale non fu mai tale, né che fussi padrone delle deliberazioni publiche, né che a modo suo si creassino e' magistrati; in modo che mai non dispiacquono agli uomini savii e' progressi suoi, e se Catone gli fu opposito, nacque o da inimicizia particulare, o da quella inclinazione che lui ebbe sempre contro alla nobilità, non da utilità publica; la santità di chi non scusa questa ingratitudine, perché e' costumi di Catone furono santi, per essere pieno di quella antica severità e austerità, ma non mancò già di nota di ambizioso, di persecutore della nobilità, di lingua immoderata, e di acerbità di natura; e lo mostrò in questa cosa, che morto ancora Scipione, e così cessato ogni colore di potere allegare il sospetto, fu più acerbo contro Asiatico suo fratello.
As to the examples of the ingratitude of Rome, if fewer of them are found in that one than in the other republics, the cause of it is that it had its government more ordered than many others, albeit that one too does not lack its examples, as in Camillus, whose exile can be ill excused, as in Fabius Maximus, who, for having taken the true way of defending Rome from Hannibal, was with so much ignominy made even with the master of the horse; as in Cicero, the oppressor of the conspiracy of Catiline, as in Metellus, Publius Rutilius, and in many other men illustrious and innocent, who at various times were condemned or sent into exile. And I marvel that the Discourse should excuse the case of Scipio, wishing to attribute to suspicion what arose merely from envy and from ignorance; because in his time Rome was governed in such a way that it did not have to fear any citizen, nor was the greatness of Scipio frightening, not being founded on sects, nor [on] a following of men, but on that authority which his virtù and his merits gave him in the city. Which was never such, either that he was master of the public deliberations, or that the magistrates were created after his own way; in such a way that his progresses never displeased wise men, and if Cato was opposed to him, it arose either from particular enmity, or from that inclination which he always had against the nobility, not from public utility; whose holiness does not excuse this ingratitude, because the customs of Cato were holy, through his being full of that ancient severity and austerity, but he did not indeed lack the mark of an ambitious man, of a persecutor of the nobility, of an immoderate tongue and of bitterness of nature; and he showed it in this thing, that, even Scipio being dead, and so every colour of being able to allege suspicion having ceased, he was more bitter against Asiaticus his brother.
Commentary · 3

Argument. Rome. If fewer examples are found in it than in other republics, the cause is that its government was more ordered than many others, the cause he gave in I.28; but it has its examples: Camillus, whose exile can ill be excused, Fabius Maximus, Cicero, Metellus, Rutilius and other illustrious and innocent men. And he marvels that the Discourse excuses Scipio's case as suspicion, when it arose merely from envy and ignorance, his two added heads. In Scipio's time Rome had no citizen to fear; Scipio's greatness rested not on sects or a following but on the authority his virtù and merits gave him, and he was never master of the public deliberations or the elections, so that wise men were never displeased by his progress. Cato's opposition came from private enmity or his hostility to the nobility, not from public utility; his holy customs do not excuse it, since he was marked as ambitious, a persecutor of the nobility, immoderate of tongue and bitter of nature, as he showed by turning more bitterly on Scipio's brother Asiaticus after Scipio was dead, when every colour of alleging suspicion had ceased.

History. Fabius, dictator in 217 BC, saw his master of the horse, Minucius, given equal command by the people (Livy 22.25-26). Cicero, who put down Catiline's conspiracy in 63, was exiled in 58; Metellus Numidicus went into exile in 100 rather than swear to Saturninus' law; Rutilius Rufus was condemned for extortion in 92 on a charge held to be false. For the trials of the Scipios, Livy 38.50-60, who reports that the date of Africanus' death was disputed in his own day.

Cross-reference. Machiavelli set Camillus aside as exiled for an injury he had done Machiavelli .07 and told Scipio's case as suspicion with its causes, the greatness of the enemy he had vanquished, the reputation of the war, its speed Machiavelli .08. Guicciardini answers his sentence on the wise men nearly in its words: Machiavelli's "which thing displeased wise men" becomes "his progresses never displeased wise men"; and Machiavelli's Cato "deemed holy" becomes a holiness that does not excuse.

1.29.06
Né voglio pretermettere che quello che dice il Discorso è molto alieno dalla verità, che in una republica non ancora corrotta sia utile alla libertà che il populo qualche volta offenda chi doverrebbe premiare, e sospetti di chi doverrebbe confidare, perché ogni ingratitudine, ogni ingiustizia è sempre perniziosa, e la republica debbe essere temperata in modo che sempre e' buoni siano onorati e gl'innocenti non spaventati. Confesso bene questo essere minore errore, lo astenersi qualche volta per sospetto di confidare de' buoni, che non è il rimettersi in mano de' cattivi; ma questa ragione non fa che il minore male sia bene, quando non s'ha necessità di eleggere o l'uno o l'altro.
Nor do I wish to pass over that what the Discourse says is very alien from the truth, that in a republic not yet corrupted it is useful to liberty that the people should sometimes offend whom it ought to reward, and suspect whom it ought to confide in, because every ingratitude, every injustice is always pernicious, and the republic must be tempered in such a way that the good are always honoured and the innocent not frightened. I confess indeed this to be a lesser error, the abstaining sometimes through suspicion from confiding in the good, than is the putting oneself in the hands of the bad; but this reason does not make the lesser ill a good, when there is no necessity to choose either the one or the other.
Commentary · 2

Argument. The close. What the Discourse says, that in a republic not yet corrupted it is useful to liberty that the people should sometimes offend those it ought to reward and suspect those it ought to trust, is very far from the truth: every ingratitude and injustice is pernicious, and a republic must be tempered so that the good are always honoured and the innocent not frightened. He grants that withholding trust at times from the good through suspicion is a lesser error than putting oneself in the hands of the bad. But the lesser ill is not a good, "when there is no necessity to choose". The condition limits the denial to cases where there is no necessity to choose.

Cross-reference. Machiavelli's claim was double: in a corrupted republic these errors are the cause of great ills, in an uncorrupted one of great goods Machiavelli .06. Guicciardini answers the second half and adds "not yet" (non ancora) and "sometimes" (qualche volta) in restating it. Machiavelli held that in an uncorrupted republic these ways are the cause of great goods; Guicciardini, that every ingratitude and injustice is always pernicious.

"I confess indeed that in this case the bites of princes are harder": a real concession. gagliardo, hard, kept apart from forte, strong, in the next clause. morsi, bites, as the peoples who mordono più fieramente in Machiavelli's previous chapter (I.28 .03).

vengono al coltello e alle esecuzioni forti. "They come to the knife and to strong executions": Machiavelli's farlo morire (Machiavelli .03).

Quanto agli altri duo capi della ignoranza e della malignità fondata in su la invidia. "As to the other two heads, of ignorance and of malignity founded on envy": capo, head (glossary), a head of the argument.

credo che sanza comparazione il populo sia più ingrato. "I believe that beyond comparison the people is more ungrateful": the reply's verdict on the rubric's question, given as belief, and for these two causes.

e per essere distratti gli uomini a varie faccende. "Both through men's being distracted to various affairs": the nominal infinitive with its subject (D109).

manco intende, manco distingue, e manco cognosce. "It understands less, distinguishes less and knows less": the anaphora kept. Both readers doubted the e and cognosce; read with the 1933 edition (D307).

ogni grandezza punto eminente. "Every greatness at all eminent": punto, at all; grandezza, greatness (glossary).

cade più facilmente negli uomini populari. "It falls more readily in popular men": envy arises in them; "on" would make them its objects.

è ordinariamente molesta. "Is ordinarily irksome": molesto, irksome; it returns of the prince (non gli sarà molesta).

né cosa alcuna dispiace loro che vedere. "Nor does anything displease them [more] than to see": the comparative is understood in the Italian and supplied in brackets (D53).

e questi sempre desiderano abbassare. "And these they always desire to lower": questi, those citizens, object fronted as the Italian fronts it, a landing (D263); desiderare, desire (glossary).

Non interviene così in uno principe. "It does not befall so in a prince": intervenire, befall (D298).

e però dove la grandezza degli altri non sia tale che gli generi sospetto. "And therefore, where the greatness of the others is not such that it generates suspicion in him": però, therefore (D22), consecutive. Guicciardini grants the case Machiavelli made, greatness that does breed suspicion.

né la batterà per questa malignità. "Nor will he beat it through this malignity": battere, beat (D120), to strike at or bring down; Guicciardini's verb of I.28.

Restano gli esempli allegati nel Discorso. "There remain the examples alleged in the Discourse": Antonius and Consalvo (Machiavelli .04, .05).

non è esemplo di ingratitudine di uno principe verso il suddito; ma di dua che vivono sotto uno principe. "Is not an example of the ingratitude of a prince towards the subject, but of two who live under one prince": Mucianus and Antonius were both Vespasian's men; Machiavelli makes Mucianus the agent and Vespasian the receiver.

e il non vi avere provisto Vespasiano. "And Vespasian's not having provided in it": the nominal infinitive with its subject after it (D109).

dal dispiacergli la natura insolente di Antonio. "From Antonius' insolent nature displeasing him": Tacitus (Histories 3.49, 4.80) describes Antonius' arrogance. insolente, insolent, Machiavelli's usato insolentemente (Machiavelli .03).

dal rispetto grande che aveva a Muziano. "From the great respect he had to Mucianus": rispetto a, respect to (glossary).

Non serve ancora al discorso nostro. "Nor does the example ... serve our discourse either": discorso, discourse (D01), the argument he and Machiavelli share.

al quale il re Don Ferrando non si potette chiamare ingrato. "Whom King Don Ferdinand could not be called ungrateful to": Ferdinand II of Aragon; Don, the Spanish title, kept.

di povero cavaliere aveva Stati per trentamila scudi. "From a poor knight he had states of thirty thousand scudi": Stati, states, here lordships yielding an income; scudi, the coin, left in Italian. Gonzalo was a younger son of a noble house.

e se gli tolse il governo del regno. "And if he took from him the government of the kingdom": Gonzalo had governed Naples as viceroy from 1503; Ferdinand came in 1506 and took him to Spain in 1507. Canestrini's note on the kingdom of Naples, keyed here, is his and is not carried.

per le discordie che nella successione del regno potevano nascere da lui e gli eredi del re Filippo. "Because of the discords that could arise, in the succession to the kingdom, from him and the heirs of King Philip": Philip I of Castile, the Handsome, who died in 1506, husband of Ferdinand's daughter Joanna; his heirs were Ferdinand's grandsons. The 1933 edition reads le differenzie ... tra lui e gli eredi, "the differences ... between him and the heirs"; both readers read Canestrini's words without doubt, and they are kept (D310; FG-CONS-I29-D007).

che al re non ne restava altro che il nome regio. "That nothing was left to the king but the royal name": the governor holding the substance of rule, the king only the name.

In modo che non si chiama ingrato quello principe che provede ... e di godersi lui. "In such a way that that prince is not called ungrateful who provides ... and to enjoy himself": the second object after provede changes construction from che to di + infinitive; the English keeps the change.

ricco e onoratissimo tra gli altri grandi. "Rich and most honoured among the other great [men]": grandi, the great (D118); the noun is supplied in brackets. Against Machiavelli's inonorato (Machiavelli .05).

ne è causa che ebbe il governo più ordinato che molte altre. "The cause of it is that it had its government more ordered than many others": causa, cause (glossary); ordinato, ordered, the ordine family; the explanation of FG I.28, the government.

benché anche quella non manca degli esempli suoi. "Albeit that one too does not lack its examples": benché, albeit (D31); mancare di with a noun, lack, a scoped exception to D206's "fail", recorded (D312), and held at non mancò già di nota below; quella, that one, Rome, as in se in quella se ne truova manco, "if fewer of them are found in that one", where ne is the examples.

come in Cammillo, lo esilio del quale si può male scusare. "As in Camillus, whose exile can be ill excused": Machiavelli's second case (Machiavelli .07), whom he did not count as ingratitude because Camillus had injured the plebs.

come in Fabio Massimo ... fu con tanta ignominia fatto pari al maestro de' cavalieri. "As in Fabius Maximus ... was with so much ignominy made even with the master of the horse": in 217 BC, while Fabius as dictator was wearing Hannibal down by delay, the people voted his master of the horse, Minucius, a command equal to his (Livy 22.25-26). pari, even (D262); maestro de' cavalieri, master of the horse, the established English for magister equitum.

come in Cicerone oppressore della conjurazione di Catilina. "As in Cicero, the oppressor of the conspiracy of Catiline": opprimere, oppress (glossary), here to put down. Cicero suppressed the conspiracy in 63 BC and was exiled in 58.

come in Metello, Publio Rutilio. "As in Metellus, Publius Rutilius": Quintus Caecilius Metellus Numidicus, who went into exile in 100 BC rather than swear to Saturninus' agrarian law; Publius Rutilius Rufus, condemned for extortion in 92 BC on a charge held to be false.

uomini clari e innocenti. "Men illustrious and innocent": claro, illustrious. Both readers doubted the e before in molti and before innocenti; read with the 1933 edition (D307).

E mi maraviglio che il Discorso scusi il caso di Scipione. "And I marvel that the Discourse should excuse the case of Scipio": scusare, excuse (D173), Machiavelli's grant of quella scusa to Rome (Machiavelli .08).

quello che nacque meramente da invidia e da ignoranza. "What arose merely from envy and from ignorance": meramente, merely, purely (D298): the case is put under his two new heads (.03), not under suspicion.

Roma si reggeva in modo che non aveva da temere di alcuno cittadino. "Rome was governed in such a way that it did not have to fear any citizen": reggere, govern (D64); avere da, have to (D61, D261).

non essendo fondata in su sètte, né ség[u]ito di uomini. "Not being founded on sects, nor [on] a following of men": sètte, sects, factions of partisans. spaventoso, frightening, with spaventati, frightened (.06), one root. Both readers read ségnito, not a word; emended from the 1933 edition (D309; FG-CONS-I29-D006).

ma in quella autorità che gli dava nella città la virtù ed e' meriti suoi. "But on that authority which his virtù and his merits gave him in the city": virtù left in Italian (D16); meriti, merits (D249).

La quale non fu mai tale, né che fussi padrone ... né che a modo suo si creassino e' magistrati. "Which was never such, either that he was master of the public deliberations, or that the magistrates were created after his own way": la quale, the authority; né ... né after a negative, "either ... or". a modo suo, after his own way, modo kept as "way" (glossary).

in modo che mai non dispiacquono agli uomini savii e' progressi suoi. "In such a way that his progresses never displeased wise men": the answer, nearly word for word, to Machiavelli's la qual cosa spiaceva agli uomini savj (Machiavelli .08): spiaceva, dispiacquono, both "displeased". progressi, progresses, his advancement.

e se Catone gli fu opposito, nacque o da inimicizia particulare. "And if Cato was opposed to him, it arose either from particular enmity": opposito, opposed; particulare, particular, of one's own (D93); inimicizia, enmity (D128).

non da utilità publica. "Not from public utility": the one ground that would have justified Cato.

la santità di chi non scusa questa ingratitudine. "Whose holiness does not excuse this ingratitude": santità, holiness, so that the root of Machiavelli's riputato santo, "deemed holy", and of furono santi below stays one; di chi, of whom, Cato; Canestrini glosses it in a note as del quale, which is not carried. The answer to Machiavelli's riputato santo (Machiavelli .08).

perché e' costumi di Catone furono santi. "Because the customs of Cato were holy": costumi, customs (D41). The concession is followed by ma: holy in his customs, but marked for ambition.

non mancò già di nota di ambizioso. "He did not indeed lack the mark of an ambitious man": nota, mark, a stigma, the word of the censor's mark, turned on the censor; già, indeed, with the negative.

e di acerbità di natura. "And of bitterness of nature": acerbità, bitterness (glossary); it returns as più acerbo, "more bitter", in the last clause.

che morto ancora Scipione, e così cessato ogni colore di potere allegare il sospetto. "That, even Scipio being dead, and so every colour of being able to allege suspicion having ceased": ancora, even; morto intransitive, dead (D262); colore, colour, pretext; allegare, allege.

fu più acerbo contro Asiatico suo fratello. "He was more bitter against Asiaticus his brother": Lucius Cornelius Scipio Asiaticus, prosecuted over the money of King Antiochus, condemned and fined (Livy 38.54-60). Livy places Africanus' death at about the same time and reports the dispute over its date; Guicciardini puts Cato's attack after it. The 1933 edition reads contro a Asiatico.

Né voglio pretermettere che quello che dice il Discorso è molto alieno dalla verità. "Nor do I wish to pass over that what the Discourse says is very alien from the truth": pretermettere, pass over; alieno, alien, the word of .01. The 1933 edition reads dica; escalated as a word at a letter the scan breaks and kept (D308; FG-CONS-I29-D005).

che in una republica non ancora corrotta sia utile alla libertà. "That in a republic not yet corrupted it is useful to liberty": Machiavelli's in una Repubblica non corrotta, sono cagione di gran beni, e fanno che la ne vive libera più (Machiavelli .06), restated as Guicciardini reads it; non ancora, not yet, is his.

che il populo qualche volta offenda chi doverrebbe premiare, e sospetti di chi doverrebbe confidare. "That the people should sometimes offend whom it ought to reward, and suspect whom it ought to confide in": Machiavelli's two errors of a free city (Machiavelli .06), in his words; qualche volta, sometimes, is Guicciardini's.

la republica debbe essere temperata in modo che. "The republic must be tempered in such a way that": dovere, must (D274); temperato, tempered (glossary).

Confesso bene questo essere minore errore. "I confess indeed this to be a lesser error": a second real concession, the accusative with infinitive kept.

lo astenersi qualche volta per sospetto di confidare de' buoni, che non è il rimettersi in mano de' cattivi. "The abstaining sometimes through suspicion from confiding in the good, than is the putting oneself in the hands of the bad": the two nominal infinitives kept parallel; cattivi, bad (D03).

ma questa ragione non fa che il minore male sia bene. "But this reason does not make the lesser ill a good": male, ill; bene, good (glossary).

quando non s'ha necessità di eleggere o l'uno o l'altro. "When there is no necessity to choose either the one or the other": eleggere, choose (glossary). The condition limits the denial to cases where there is no necessity to choose.

What this is

A free, openly licensed, bilingual edition of Guicciardini's 39 Considerazioni and the Discorsi chapters they answer — 78 text units, Italian and English facing, every reading traceable to a named public-domain witness.

It is a reading edition with a transparent apparatus, not a text of record. It does not adjudicate the critical text against Bausi 2001 or the manuscript tradition, and does not claim to.

On the title

Machiavelli had been dead two years when Guicciardini wrote; the Discorsi were unpublished, and the Considerazioni stayed unprinted until 1857. Machiavelli never answers.

“Conversation” is a quotation, not a description. It invokes Machiavelli's letter to Francesco Vettori of 10 December 1513: at evening he puts on royal and curial garments (panni reali e curiali), enters the ancient courts of ancient men (le antiche corti degli antichi uomini), asks them the reason for their actions, and quelli per loro umanità mi rispondono, “they, for their humanity, answer me”. Guicciardini does to Machiavelli what Machiavelli did to Livy — and is denied the reply Machiavelli claimed. The letter is quoted from the 1813 Opere, vol. 8, p. 96, read twice from the page image.

Method

No image-derived reading enters the text on a single reading. Each unit is transcribed twice, independently, by readers who have not seen each other's work; disagreements are adjudicated against the page images and recorded.

Existing English translations of the Considerazioni — Grayson (1965), Atkinson & Sices (2002) — are never used as translation inputs.
