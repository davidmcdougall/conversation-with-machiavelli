## FG-CONS.1.29.01

**Argument.** Guicciardini grants Machiavelli's two causes, avarice and suspicion, and adds two: ignorance, and malignity rooted in envy. Considering all four, he does not believe a people more alien from ingratitude than a prince; on the contrary. He keeps to Machiavelli's ground, men who have served in public affairs, and to his two forms, not rewarding and offending. Of these the second is more pernicious and the first more frequent, and in both, he says, whoever examines will find the people erring no less than the prince, and "in my judgment" more.

Then avarice, which very seldom causes ingratitude except in not rewarding. If the people, by its instinct a rare and small remunerator, sins little in it, he believes the prince too does not sin much, since a prince can reward without touching his purse, with things he is accustomed to give away. Princes do often omit to reward, through avarice or through being ungrateful by nature, a further cause he adds in passing; but "without doubt" there are infinite examples of princes who have rewarded, compared with the rewards of peoples. The magistracies a people gives its citizens are not to be counted, since it gives them more in hope of good service than in gratitude.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's division is the frame [[NM-DISC.1.29.01]]; his two acts, not rewarding and offending, are restated in his words [[NM-DISC.1.29.02]]. Where Machiavelli said it will be seen that peoples never used ingratitude through avarice [[NM-DISC.1.29.09]], Guicciardini believes that, if the people sins little in it, the prince does not sin much either; his "without doubt" is about the princes' rewards.

## FG-CONS.1.29.02

**Argument.** Suspicion. He believes a people, as a rule, grows suspicious much more lightly and on lesser grounds than a prince, because it uses less diligence and has less means to check a false calumny, and once suspicious it dishonours without regard or art. A prince who is not wholly imprudent at times dissembles: he withholds trust where it would be dangerous and keeps it elsewhere, taking care not to drive the man to despair. He has no doubt that examples of ingratitude through suspicion are infinite, in republics and princes; if Rome erred in it less than other republics, it erred much more than the Discourse says. The examples of Camillus and Scipio cannot be excused by that path. Then a real concession: in this case princes' bites are harder, since they come more readily to the knife.

**Cross-reference.** Set beside Machiavelli's mechanism, suspicion arising "at once" and impossible not to grow [[NM-DISC.1.29.03]], Guicciardini's prudent prince manages the suspicion he feels. The excuse Machiavelli granted Rome through suspicion, if its people followed Cato's opinion [[NM-DISC.1.29.08]], is the path Guicciardini closes. His "as will be said below" repeats Machiavelli's closing words [[NM-DISC.1.29.09]]; compare also Machiavelli's peoples who "bite more fiercely", in I.28.

## FG-CONS.1.29.03

**Argument.** Ignorance and envy, the two heads he added. Here he believes the people beyond comparison more ungrateful. Distracted by its affairs, it understands, distinguishes and knows less than a prince; and envy falls more readily in popular men, to whom any eminent greatness, of nobility, riches, *virtù* or reputation, is ordinarily irksome, and who always wish to lower those who have more quality than they. A prince has no envy of inferiors; therefore, unless another's greatness breeds suspicion in him, it will not irk him and he will not strike at it from malignity. The exception keeps Machiavelli's case of suspicion in its place.

## FG-CONS.1.29.04

**Argument.** The examples. Antonius Primus is not a case of a prince's ingratitude to a subject but of two men under one prince, each drawing the credit to himself; Vespasian's failure to act arose not from suspicion but from dislike of Antonius' insolence and, much more, respect for Mucianus. Consalvo is no better: Ferdinand rewarded him with states, took the government from him for just suspicion over the succession, and, he says it is certain, Consalvo ruled with such authority that the king had only the royal name. A prince is not ungrateful who prevents his benefactor from harming him and keeps what he has acquired through him, provided he does it as Ferdinand did: Consalvo lived on in Spain, rich and most honoured.

**History.** Philip I of Castile, Ferdinand's son-in-law and his rival for the government of Castile, died in September 1506; Gonzalo was suspected of leaning to Philip's side, and with the succession open a viceroy of his standing was a danger. Ferdinand came to Naples in November 1506 and left in June 1507 with Gonzalo, who never again held a command and died in 1515. Machiavelli says he died "unhonoured"; Guicciardini, that he lived "rich and most honoured among the other great".

**Cross-reference.** Against Machiavelli's two proofs [[NM-DISC.1.29.04]] [[NM-DISC.1.29.05]]. Guicciardini disputes chiefly what the events show, though against Machiavelli's "unhonoured" he sets a Consalvo rich and most honoured. His rule, that a prince who secures himself against his benefactor is not ungrateful if he does it as Ferdinand did, rewarding and honouring him, stands against Machiavelli's prince who can think only of securing himself [[NM-DISC.1.29.03]].

## FG-CONS.1.29.05

**Argument.** Rome. If fewer examples are found in it than in other republics, the cause is that its government was more ordered than many others, the cause he gave in I.28; but it has its examples: Camillus, whose exile can ill be excused, Fabius Maximus, Cicero, Metellus, Rutilius and other illustrious and innocent men. And he marvels that the Discourse excuses Scipio's case as suspicion, when it arose merely from envy and ignorance, his two added heads. In Scipio's time Rome had no citizen to fear; Scipio's greatness rested not on sects or a following but on the authority his *virtù* and merits gave him, and he was never master of the public deliberations or the elections, so that wise men were never displeased by his progress. Cato's opposition came from private enmity or his hostility to the nobility, not from public utility; his holy customs do not excuse it, since he was marked as ambitious, a persecutor of the nobility, immoderate of tongue and bitter of nature, as he showed by turning more bitterly on Scipio's brother Asiaticus after Scipio was dead, when every colour of alleging suspicion had ceased.

**History.** Fabius, dictator in 217 BC, saw his master of the horse, Minucius, given equal command by the people (Livy 22.25-26). Cicero, who put down Catiline's conspiracy in 63, was exiled in 58; Metellus Numidicus went into exile in 100 rather than swear to Saturninus' law; Rutilius Rufus was condemned for extortion in 92 on a charge held to be false. For the trials of the Scipios, Livy 38.50-60, who reports that the date of Africanus' death was disputed in his own day.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli set Camillus aside as exiled for an injury he had done [[NM-DISC.1.29.07]] and told Scipio's case as suspicion with its causes, the greatness of the enemy he had vanquished, the reputation of the war, its speed [[NM-DISC.1.29.08]]. Guicciardini answers his sentence on the wise men nearly in its words: Machiavelli's "which thing displeased wise men" becomes "his progresses never displeased wise men"; and Machiavelli's Cato "deemed holy" becomes a holiness that does not excuse.

## FG-CONS.1.29.06

**Argument.** The close. What the Discourse says, that in a republic not yet corrupted it is useful to liberty that the people should sometimes offend those it ought to reward and suspect those it ought to trust, is very far from the truth: every ingratitude and injustice is pernicious, and a republic must be tempered so that the good are always honoured and the innocent not frightened. He grants that withholding trust at times from the good through suspicion is a lesser error than putting oneself in the hands of the bad. But the lesser ill is not a good, "when there is no necessity to choose". The condition limits the denial to cases where there is no necessity to choose.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's claim was double: in a corrupted republic these errors are the cause of great ills, in an uncorrupted one of great goods [[NM-DISC.1.29.06]]. Guicciardini answers the second half and adds "not yet" (`non ancora`) and "sometimes" (`qualche volta`) in restating it. Machiavelli held that in an uncorrupted republic these ways are the cause of great goods; Guicciardini, that every ingratitude and injustice is always pernicious.
