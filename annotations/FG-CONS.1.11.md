## FG-CONS.1.11.01

**Argument.** A grant, a suspension, an inclination and two reasons. The grant is whole (on the emended text, D200): it is certain that both arms and religion are principal foundations of republics and kingdoms, each so necessary that, failing either, one can say the vital and substantial parts fail. Then he suspends: he does not know whether it is true that, in the dispute Machiavelli posed, Numa merits the first praise, or that Numa's difficulties were greater. Machiavelli does not say the second in so many words; Guicciardini draws it from the "difficulty" of the asymmetry and of the orderers' recourse to God. He would incline rather to the opposite, and it seems to him this can be shown with a very powerful reason. The first reason is the order of time. Had Numa been first, the city would in its beginnings have been oppressed by its neighbours, and Numa would not have left Romulus room for arms as Romulus left Numa room for religion; so Romulus was the more necessary at the beginnings. The second reason starts from Machiavelli's own concession, that the times were full of religion, and extends it to the neighbouring cities, whose example and likeness made the Roman people easy to dispose to it. The proof is the choice of Numa itself. The people, still most ferocious and used to arms, chose of their own will not a warrior but a king venerable for justice, religion and the arts of peace, and went to fetch him out of the neighbouring cities; so the Romans were inclined of themselves to order themselves with religion and good laws, and Numa found the spirits already disposed. He ends without choosing between prudence and fortune, or both, as the cause of Rome's having two most excellent kings, one of war and one of peace, and fixes only the order: the one of war first, because arms gave the city life enough to wait for Numa and for whoever should order it with religion.

**Cross-reference.** The question is Machiavelli's, in his words, "to which prince Rome was more indebted, either to Romulus or to Numa" [[NM-DISC.1.11.03]]. The concession Guicciardini borrows is the first half of Machiavelli's, that those times were full of religion; the gross men he leaves out [[NM-DISC.1.11.05]]. "More necessary" turns the word Machiavelli used to say Numa, and not Romulus, needed God's authority [[NM-DISC.1.11.04]]. `Ferocissimo` is Machiavelli's word for the people Numa found [[NM-DISC.1.11.01]]; Guicciardini keeps it and makes it the proof: a people still most ferocious chose a king of peace of its own will. Machiavelli's heavens put the choice of Numa into the senate's breast; Guicciardini gives it to the people (`volontariamente`, `desiderò`, `per sé medesimi`). The grant of arms and religion as two foundations, each necessary, answers Machiavelli's asymmetry, that where there is religion arms can readily be introduced, and where there are arms and not religion, religion only with difficulty [[NM-DISC.1.11.03]]. He does not call the rule false, but his first reason turns its first half: at a city's beginnings Numa would not have left Romulus room for arms, because the neighbours would have oppressed the city first. The question becomes what comes first. And where Machiavelli derived Rome's fortune from Numa's religion, religion causing good orders and good orders good fortune [[NM-DISC.1.11.06]], Guicciardini leaves open whether prudence or fortune, or both, gave Rome its two kings. What the reply leaves alone: the forced oaths, Numa's simulation, the rule that orderers of extraordinary laws have recourse to God, the kingdoms that fail with one man's *virtù*, and Savonarola. The dispute is over rank and order, and over who chose Numa.

**History.** In Livy (1.17–18) the choice lies with the senate, as in Machiavelli, and not with the people: after Romulus' death and a year's interregnum, the people, offered the choice, left it to the senate, which chose Numa of Cures, renowned for justice and religion (`inclita iustitia religioque`). The heavens are Machiavelli's addition; the people's own will is Guicciardini's, though in Livy the people did defer of their own accord. On Numa's character Guicciardini follows Livy even where he departs from him on who chose. The Sabines were Rome's neighbours, and since the union under Titus Tatius partly its own people.
