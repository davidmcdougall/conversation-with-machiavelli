## NM-DISC.3.24.01

**Argument.** The chapter opens on a diagnosis, conditioned: "if one considers well" the proceeding of the Roman republic, two things will be seen to have been the cause of its dissolution, the contentions that arose from the Agrarian law and the prolongation of commands. The rubric names only the second, and the chapter treats only the second; the first is the subject of an earlier chapter (I.37, the editor's cross-reference). The counterfactual that closes the sentence is bounded twice: had the two things been known well from the beginning and remedied, free living would have been longer, and, "it may be", more quiet. Machiavelli does not say that the republic would have lasted, only that its free living would have been longer (the editor's reading).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not mention the Agrarian law. He grants the prorogation of commands to have been a great occasion to whoever wished to occupy the republic, and sets the principal foundation of the ills elsewhere, in the corruption of the city [[FG-CONS.3.24.01]].

## NM-DISC.3.24.02

**Argument.** The second cause is set apart from the first by its effect: one does not see that any tumult ever arose in Rome over the prolongation of command, where the Agrarian contentions, by implication, made tumult (the editor's reading). Its harm is seen "in fact", in the authority that the citizens took through such deliberations. The paragraph's conditional names the remedy that was lacking: had the other citizens to whom the magistracy was prorogued been wise and good, as Lucius Quinctius was, the inconvenience would not have been incurred. The fault is placed in the men who accepted prorogation, not in the institution alone (the editor's reading).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's first sentence names what prorogation gave the ambitious: it was an instrument for making friends of the soldiers and a following with kings and in foreign nations or provinces [[FG-CONS.3.24.01]]. That it answers Machiavelli's harm without tumult is the editor's reading.

## NM-DISC.3.24.03

**Argument.** Quinctius' goodness is the example. The plebs prolonged the command to the tribunes for a year, judging them able to resist the ambition of the nobles; the Senate, out of rivalry with the plebs and so as not to seem less than it, wished to prolong his consulship; he wholly denied it, on the ground that bad examples are to be extinguished, not increased with a worse one, and wished that new consuls be made. The paragraph closes on a second counterfactual, wider than the first: had that goodness and prudence been in all the Roman citizens, the usage of prolonging magistracies would not have been introduced, and from it the prolongation of commands would not have followed, which in time ruined the republic. The chain runs from magistracies prolonged at Rome to commands prolonged in the field (the editor's reading).

**History.** Livy places the episode in the year of Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus' consulship, he having been made consul in place of the dead Valerius (Livy 3.19); the next year's consuls are named at 3.22. He reports a compromise, the tribunes promising to bow to the senate's authority, after which the senate resolved that for the future the continuing of magistrates and the re-election of the same tribunes was against the public interest; the tribunes were re-elected over the consuls' protests; the senate, so as not to yield to the plebs, re-elected Quinctius; he refused, in a speech that ironically bids the fathers imitate the thoughtless crowd and not yield to the multitude's rashness, and the consuls by a joint edict forbade any vote for him (Livy 3.21; the editor's references, checked against the Latin and Roberts's public-domain English). Machiavelli's "convention of accord" may answer to that compromise, but he does not say that the tribunes' re-election broke it, and he gives the plebs a reason of their own (the editor's reading). What the plebs prolonged is the tribunes' office; Machiavelli calls it `Imperio`, the chapter's word, though the tribunes of the plebs held no *imperium* (the editor's note).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not take up Quinctius; his example of a command not prorogued is Sulla's [[FG-CONS.3.24.01]]. Quinctius' poverty is the subject of the next chapter, III.25, which Guicciardini does not answer.

## NM-DISC.3.24.04

**Argument.** The first prorogation of command is named and explained: Publilius Philo, at the end of his consulship, before Palaeopolis, with the victory, as it seemed to the Senate, in his hand, was not sent a successor but made proconsul. The decision was "moved by the Senate for public utility"; the paragraph grants the motive and still names the thing as that which "in time" made Rome servile. The judgment is of consequence, not of intention (the editor's reading).

**History.** Quintus Publilius Philo, consul for the second time with Lucius Cornelius Lentulus, was given the war against Palaeopolis, the Greek city not far from where Neapolis now stands (Livy 8.22); as the elections approached, with the siege near its end, an arrangement was made with the tribunes of the plebs to propose to the people that, his consulship ended, he should act as proconsul until the war with the Greeks was finished (Livy 8.23). Livy notes that two things then befell him first, a prorogation of command never before made, and a triumph after his term of office had ended (Livy 8.26, `acto honore`) (the editor's references, checked against the Latin and Roberts's public-domain English; traditionally 327-326 BC). Machiavelli's `P. Filone` gives the praenomen P., where Livy has Q., Quintus; and in Livy the prorogation passes by a vote of the people, proposed through the tribunes, the senate not named there as its mover, where Machiavelli gives the judgment and the act to the Senate (the editor's reading).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's list of what did not move Caesar's command begins with "the utility of the republic" [[FG-CONS.3.24.01]]; that it answers Machiavelli's "public utility" is the editor's reading.

## NM-DISC.3.24.05

**Argument.** The mechanism. The further the Romans carried their arms, the more necessary prorogation seemed to them, and the more they used it; and it made two inconveniences. The first: fewer men were exercised in commands, so that reputation came to be restricted to few. The second: a citizen who long commanded an army gained it for himself and made it his partisan, because the army in time forgot the Senate and acknowledged that head. The conclusions are drawn twice by `per questo`: through this Sulla and Marius could find soldiers to follow them against the public good; through this Caesar could occupy the fatherland. The seeming necessity grows with distance, and the use grows with it; the chapter does not blame the Romans for extending their arms (the editor's reading).

**History.** Sulla and Marius fought the first civil war of 88-87 BC; Caesar crossed the Rubicon with the army of his Gallic command in 49 BC (the editor's references).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini says that the command had not been prorogued to Sulla when he first came to blows with Marius, the cause being the division between nobility and plebs [[FG-CONS.3.24.01]]; that this contests Machiavelli's first example is the editor's reading, Machiavelli not having said that Sulla's command was prorogued at that clash. He grants that the long command of Gaul increased Caesar's wealth and that Caesar's was an extraordinary prorogation, but gives the prorogation itself another cause, the conspiracy with Pompey and Crassus to occupy the republic, and concludes, as what can be concluded, that without prorogations Caesar would not have lacked the thought or the capacity to trouble it another way (the editor's reading).

## NM-DISC.3.24.06

**Argument.** The chapter ends on a third counterfactual, its terms stacked: had the Romans never prolonged magistracies and commands, had they not come so soon to so great a power, and had their acquisitions been later, they would also have come later into servitude. The conclusion is a delay, not an escape: servitude would have come later, not never. The conditions also widen the cause: power reached so soon and acquisitions made earlier stand beside prolongation as conditions of the timing of servitude, so that the closing sentence makes prolongation one cause among others, not the only one (the editor's reading). The 1554 edition, without the second `se`, makes the coming so soon to power a consequence of prolongation rather than a further condition (NM-DISC-III24-D004; D509).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's close is also counterfactual, and turned the other way: therefore, he says, it can be concluded that, had there not been the prorogations as well, neither Caesar nor the others who occupied the republic would have lacked the thought or the capacity to trouble it by another path [[FG-CONS.3.24.01]]. Where Machiavelli delays servitude by removing prolongation, Guicciardini concludes that its removal would not have taken from such men the thought or the means of troubling a corrupted city (the editor's reading).
