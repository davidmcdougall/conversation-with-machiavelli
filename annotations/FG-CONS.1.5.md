## FG-CONS.1.5.01

**Argument.** He does not understand the question, and says why: it is two questions. One is who governs, the great or the plebs, and Venice serves to answer it — the government is in the nobles and the plebs is shut out. The other is who, when everyone shares the government, holds a particular charge of defending liberty; Rome can serve to answer that, since the tribunes, a plebeian magistracy, seemed to hold it [[NM-DISC.1.5.01]]. Then he corrects even that. At Rome the guard of liberty was no less with the patricians: consuls and dictators had charge of it, as against Maelius, Manlius, the Gracchi and Catiline; and the right to accuse belonged to both orders, so that other magistrates, not only the tribunes, could call a man to judgment. The tribunes were made to defend the plebs against its oppressors, not liberty against whoever would oppress the whole republic; if they accused more often, it was because, as a plebeian magistracy, they had more credit with the plebs, and it seemed in a way their proper office. His own answer: in a mixed government he would have the guard belong to all, and of necessity each order is the guard of the other.

**History.** Spurius Maelius was killed in 439 BC by Servilius Ahala, master of the horse to the dictator Cincinnatus; Marcus Manlius Capitolinus was imprisoned in 385 BC by the dictator Cornelius Cossus. Tiberius Gracchus was killed in 133 BC by senators led by Scipio Nasica, a private citizen; Gaius in 121 BC under the consul Opimius. Catiline's conspiracy was crushed in Rome in 63 BC by the consul Cicero, and Catiline fell at Pistoria early in 62, fighting the army of Cicero's colleague Antonius. Guicciardini's "by the dictators" is loose for Maelius, and his "by the consuls" fits Gaius Gracchus and Catiline, not Tiberius.

**Cross-reference.** "A mixed government as above" points back to his reply to I.2, where he grants the mixed state (.01) and designs its senate (.04). "Fleeing the distinction between nobles and plebeians" takes up I.4, where that distinction was the cause of Rome's disunions (.01).

## FG-CONS.1.5.02

**Argument.** The forced choice. If he had to put a city under either nobles alone or plebs alone, he would believe the nobles the lesser error, because, with more prudence and quality among them, there is more hope they will put themselves into some reasonable form; a plebs, full of ignorance and confusion and ill qualities, can be hoped to do nothing but throw everything down. Then he refuses Machiavelli's distinction [[NM-DISC.1.5.04]]: between a republic that acquires and one that conserves it does not help, since the government of the plebs is for neither, and Rome's government was mixed, not plebeian. He ends by placing himself with "all those who have written of republics", who set the optimates before the multitude.

**Cross-reference.** He restates Machiavelli's verdict in the vocabulary of the rubric's second question, acquiring and keeping [[NM-DISC.1.5.04]] [[NM-DISC.1.5.05]], and does not take up that question — which has the greater cause to make tumult — at all.
