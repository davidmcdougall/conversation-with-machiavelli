## NM-DISC.1.2.01

**Argument.** The chapter sets aside cities born dependent, which I.1 dealt with, and takes only those that governed themselves from the start. The division that matters now is how they got their laws: all at once from one man, like Sparta, or by chance and by instalments, like Rome.

## NM-DISC.1.2.02

**Argument.** A scale of happiness. Happiest is the city given perfect laws by one prudent man; next, a city with a good beginning that accidents can perfect; worst, a city whose orders put it off the right road altogether. Reform needs necessity, and necessity brings danger, so a city can be ruined before it is perfected.

**History.** In 1502 the revolt of Arezzo frightened Florence into making Piero Soderini gonfaloniere for life. Machiavelli, secretary of the Second Chancery since 1498, became Soderini's close agent. In 1512 the sack of Prato by the Spanish army brought the Medici back and ended that republic; Machiavelli lost his post in November.

## NM-DISC.1.2.03

**Argument.** Two accounts of the forms of government: the common one, three forms among which a founder chooses, and a second that, in the opinion of many, is wiser: six forms, three good and three bad, each good form sliding into its bad neighbour. On the second account no simple form lasts, whoever orders it.

**History.** The six forms and the cycle between them follow Polybius, *Histories* 6.3–9, closely. Machiavelli does not name him.

## NM-DISC.1.2.04

**Argument.** A history of how the forms arose. Men lived scattered like beasts, gathered for defence, made the strongest their head, and learned from ingratitude what was just and honourable. After that they chose the most prudent and just prince, not the strongest. Justice, on this account, comes after government and is learned from harm.

## NM-DISC.1.2.05

**Argument.** The first turns of the circle: hereditary princes degenerate into tyrants; men who surpass the rest in spirit, wealth and nobility lead the multitude against them and govern well; their sons turn the government of optimates into a government of few, and are overthrown in their turn. At each step the corruption comes from inheritance, from sons "never having tried ill". Guicciardini names the same cause [[FG-CONS.1.2.03]] and aims his remedy for the optimates at it [[FG-CONS.1.2.04]].

## NM-DISC.1.2.06

**Argument.** The popular state lasts a little, chiefly until the generation that founded it dies; licence follows, and the circle returns to a prince. Machiavelli then limits his own model: few republics live long enough to go round again, and a weakened one is often taken by a better-ordered neighbour first.

## NM-DISC.1.2.07

**Argument.** The conclusion: every simple form is harmful, the good ones for their short life and the bad ones for their malignity, so the prudent choose a form that shares in all three, where each part watches the others. Sparta is the example that lasted and Athens the one that did not. Guicciardini grants this at once, and adds that the mixture is best when it takes each form's good and leaves its bad [[FG-CONS.1.2.01]].

**History.** Polybius (6.10) makes Lycurgus the founder of a mixed constitution of kings, elders and people. Solon's laws date from 594 BC; Pisistratus made himself tyrant in 561, and his son Hippias was driven out in 510. The democracy restored after 510 was overthrown by oligarchs in 411 and again in 404, about a hundred years later; restored in 403, it lasted until Macedon ended it in 322. Machiavelli's forty years falls short of the fifty-one from 561 to 510; his hundred fits the span to 411 or 404.

## NM-DISC.1.2.08

**Argument.** Rome had no Lycurgus, but its accidents did what a founder had not: the first fortune is a perfect founder, the second a good beginning that events improve [[NM-DISC.1.2.02]]. The kings' laws suited liberty, but they made a kingdom, not a republic. Expelling them changed the name, not the royal power, which passed to the consuls. Guicciardini takes up the consuls from the other side: if their authority is to be called royal, it was annual, where his preferred head, like the Venetian doge, is elected for life with closely limited authority [[FG-CONS.1.2.02]].

## NM-DISC.1.2.09

**Argument.** The popular element came last, through the nobility's insolence and the creation of the tribunes, and then Rome was mixed and "perfect". The perfection came through the disunion of plebs and senate, which the next two chapters defend. Guicciardini traces all Rome's seditions to what lay beneath that disunion: its optimates were the patricians from the beginning, and the rest were plebeians [[FG-CONS.1.2.04]].

**History.** The tribunes of the plebs were created after the first secession of the plebs, which Livy (2.32–33) places in 494 BC by the conventional reckoning.
