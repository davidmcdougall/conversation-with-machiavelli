## FG-CONS.2.15.01

**Argument.** The reply is one paragraph and works by division. It begins not with a judgment of the Discourse but with an account of where the ambiguities of deliberations come from, and there are two causes. The first is the weakness of those who are to resolve: not weakness of forces and power, Guicciardini says, but of prudence and mind; it can fall as well in a prince as in a republic; and he believes that when the Discourse said "weak states" it understood weak in prudence. He allows, with `benché`, that weakness of forces can in part increase irresolution, because the courses weak states are to take are commonly fuller of difficulties and dangers. The second cause is proper to republics: when several men are to resolve and their opinions vary; and the variety can proceed either from malignity, because they have diverse ends, or else without malignity, because men's judgments do not conform with one another, as happens often even among the prudent.

Then the judgment, in two steps. "It is true" that these suspensions are commonly pernicious, since while you stand suspended you can provide for neither case; and if they are sometimes useful, as they would have been to the Lavinians had they delayed three or four days more, that utility comes rather by chance than otherwise. Suspension is then to be abhorred, resolution supremely praised, "but" one has to take heed that standing neutral can also proceed from resolution, not from suspension: from suspension it is reprehensible, from resolution it can be both useful and pernicious according to the quality of the cases, a question he says is not now his matter. He says the same of deferring some other action or execution: tardiness from irresolution is always damnable, tardiness done deliberately can be laudable.

In the editor's reading, the reply limits the chapter's conclusion rather than contradicting it: where Machiavelli says slow deliberations are always harmful, Guicciardini says tardiness from irresolution is always damnable, and tardiness done deliberately can be laudable. Whether that contradicts the rubric's second "always" depends on whether a deliberate delay counts as a slow deliberation. The word he chooses for the good case, "deliberately", is the chapter's own word for deciding.

**Cross-reference.** In the editor's reading, the two causes take up Machiavelli's two sources of slow deliberations, "weakness of spirit and of forces" and "the malignity of those who are to deliberate" [[NM-DISC.2.15.03]], applied now to ambiguity: Guicciardini moves the weakness from forces to prudence and mind, keeping a part for forces, and Machiavelli's "spirit" is not his "mind"; and he sets beside malignity a difference of judgments without it. "Standing suspended" is Machiavelli's phrase for what Livy shows the damage of at Syracuse [[NM-DISC.2.15.04]], made by Guicciardini into "stand suspended" and the noun "suspension". The Lavinians are Machiavelli's second example [[NM-DISC.2.15.05]], taken up inside Guicciardini's concession; his "little journey" answers Milionius' "this little of the path". Standing neutral is what the Florentine orators agreed with Louis XII [[NM-DISC.2.15.06]]; in the editor's reading, Guicciardini's distinction falls across that case, the course resolved by the orators and suspended by the delay to ratify it, but he does not mention it. "Tardiness" is Machiavelli's word, beside ambiguity, for what Apollonides held was to be detested [[NM-DISC.2.15.04]], and "damnable" his word for the Florentine course [[NM-DISC.2.15.06]].
