## FG-CONS.1.25.01

**Argument.** A comparative, then a reclassification, then substitute reasons. Guicciardini does not deny the conclusion of the Discourse: he says it is more necessary for whoever reforms a government without changing its species, for example by introducing new orders in a free city, than for whoever changes the species. The reason is men's opinion. If out of a kingdom one introduces a liberty, as the Romans did, men already hold that the old living is not good, and there is no call to conserve the old orders so exactly.

Then Machiavelli's two Roman examples, which he finds not of much moment. He does not call them false; he gives each another reason. In the king of sacrifices, respect was had to the superstition men could have in religion. The lictors were limited because, the royal power having been changed as too great, it would not have been tolerable to arm the consuls with insignia of greater power. Neither is Machiavelli's reason, the keeping of the shadow of the old ways.

**Cross-reference.** The reply moves Machiavelli's own case into the class where the rule matters less. Machiavelli applies the precept to whoever would bring a city from an ancient living to "a new and free living" [[NM-DISC.1.25.03]], and proves it by Rome after the kings [[NM-DISC.1.25.02]]: exactly the change of species from kingdom to liberty. Guicciardini's example, `in una città libera`, is the rubric's own case: a city already free, reformed within its form.

**Cross-reference.** On the lictors the two authors address the same limit from different sides. Machiavelli says the consuls were held to twelve "so as not to pass the number" of the kings' attendants [[NM-DISC.1.25.02]], and reads it as keeping the shadow of the old; Guicciardini says nothing of the number: after a royal power rejected as too great, greater insignia would not have been borne. On the king of sacrifices, Machiavelli's reason is the people's desire for the kings; Guicciardini's is superstition; Machiavelli gives no religious reason.

**History.** Livy's account (2.1.8; 2.2.1-2) has both readings in view: the fasces were not held by both consuls at once, so that the terror should not seem doubled, near Guicciardini's reason for the lictors; the king of sacrifices was created for rites the kings had performed, so that the kings should nowhere be missed, where the occasion is religious and the motive is Machiavelli's [[NM-DISC.1.25.02]]. Neither author cites Livy here.
