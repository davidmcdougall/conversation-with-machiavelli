## FG-CONS.1.26.01

**Argument.** Guicciardini sorts the case by the people, not by the prince. There are cities and kingdoms that take little account of a change of prince and are not used to being governed so legitimately that they cannot bear one who dominates little politically; there remedies so strong are not necessary to found the principate, and a discontented private man can be gained, provided his discontent rests on his own interest, with advantage and honour. The difficulty is where the people's inclination is wholly contrary to the new government, and there he divides again: cities used to being free that come under a tyrant, and kingdoms long under one line that love its name and memory. The kingdoms, he allows, one could hope to gain with good treatment, which in the end could make the old princes forgotten.

For those whose inclination is liberty he grants the strong remedies: no sweetness will root out of them the desire not to acknowledge a superior, and to govern (D287): freedom from subordination, and a share in the government. Three conclusions follow, each drawn with `però`, consecutive. Therefore strong remedies are required in such a case, the prince having before his eyes that the part which can be gained by benefits is to be gained, because violent remedies, if on one side they secure him, on the other make weakness in a thousand ways, above all for a prince not founded on his own arms. Therefore it is required that the prince have the spirit for extraordinary remedies when necessary, and nonetheless be so prudent as to let slip no occasion of establishing his affairs by humanity and benefits. He ends by declining to take what the writer says as an absolute rule, and by a judgment on the man: extraordinary and violent remedies always pleased him beyond measure.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's case is the prince who does not turn to civil life [[NM-DISC.1.26.01]]; Guicciardini's first period describes such a prince from below, one who "dominates little politically", and says where remedies so strong are not needed. His free peoples, who will not acknowledge a superior, refuse what Machiavelli's prince is to exact: that no degree, order or wealth be held except as acknowledged from him [[NM-DISC.1.26.02]].

**Cross-reference.** Both make the extraordinary remedies conditional, on different grounds: Machiavelli on the prince's will, refusing the good path `quando si voglia mantenere`, when he wishes to maintain himself [[NM-DISC.1.26.03]]; Guicciardini on necessity, `quando sia necessario`. Machiavelli has also called these ways most cruel and to be fled by any man. Guicciardini does not refer to that verdict; his charge that such remedies "always pleased" Machiavelli "beyond measure" and Machiavelli's own verdict on them stand side by side in this edition, which does not settle the friction.
