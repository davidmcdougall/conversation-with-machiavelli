## NM-DISC.1.30.01

**Argument.** The chapter answers the question I.29 left: if suspicion is natural to princes, and ingratitude follows from it, what can be done? The rubric asks for four remedies, for the prince and the republic, and for the captain and the citizen they send. The prince's remedy removes the cause rather than the vice. If he goes on his expeditions himself, winning leaves the glory and the acquisition his; there is no captain's glory to suspect. If he does not go, the glory is another's, and he seems to himself unable to use the acquisition without extinguishing that glory; the chapter's next words, "and become ungrateful and unjust", hang loosely on the sentence, and the edition leaves them so. Machiavelli puts the cost in a sentence: the loss is greater than the gain.

**History.** Machiavelli's three examples are classes, not persons: the Roman emperors "in the beginning", the Turk "in our times", and the virtuous of every time. He names no emperor. The Ottoman sultans of his day commanded their own campaigns; presumably Selim I, whom he calls the Grand Turk at I.1, who led the wars of 1514-17 against Persia and the Mamluks in person.

## NM-DISC.1.30.02

**Argument.** The prince who stays at home through negligence or little prudence is given nothing: Machiavelli says he has no precept for him beyond what such princes know for themselves. The advice goes instead to the captain, judged unable to flee the bites of ingratitude; whose judgment that is, Machiavelli's or the captain's own, the text leaves open. He has two ways and, as .03 says, no third. He can give up the army at once after the victory and put himself in his prince's hands, avoiding every insolent or ambitious act, so that the prince, stripped of suspicion, may have cause either to reward him or not to offend him. Or, when this does not seem good to him, he can spiritedly take the contrary part: hold every way by which he may believe the acquisition his own, win the soldiers and the subjects, make friends of the neighbours, occupy the fortresses with his men, corrupt the army's chief men, secure himself of the rest, and by these ways seek to punish his lord for the ingratitude his lord would use towards him. The second course is set out at greater length than the first, and Machiavelli passes no judgment on it beyond calling it punishment.

**Cross-reference.** Two steps of the captain's second course answer what I.29 showed being taken from victorious captains: Ferdinand removed Consalvo's command of the men-at-arms and then took the fortresses from him, and Mucianus, sent by Vespasian, at once took the army's obedience from Antonius. The army and the fortresses are what the captain of I.30 is told to secure.

## NM-DISC.1.30.03

**Argument.** Then nature closes both ways. Men do not know how to be either wholly wicked or wholly good, a claim made at the close of I.26 and in I.27, whose rubric says they very seldom know how. And it always befalls, Machiavelli says, that after the victory captains do not wish to leave the army, cannot carry themselves modestly, and do not know how to use violent bearings that keep their honour: will, power and knowledge fail in turn. Between delay and ambiguity they are oppressed. The two remedies are offered as the only ones, and then shown to be, as men are, almost never taken. The captain's half of the rubric is answered by explaining why the remedy fails.

## NM-DISC.1.30.04

**Argument.** A republic cannot go on its own expeditions; it must send a citizen. Its remedy is Rome's ways, and Machiavelli derives Rome's being less ungrateful than the others from the ways of its government. Because the whole city, nobles and ignoble, served in war, every age produced many virtuous men with victories to their names. No one of them could alarm the people, for there were many and each watched the others. They kept themselves so far uncorrupted, and so careful of seeming ambitious, that whoever laid the dictatorship down soonest earned the most glory from it. Such ways could not generate suspicion, so they did not generate ingratitude: the chain of I.29, suspicion then ingratitude, is cut at its first link. The citizen's remedy follows in one clause: observe the bearings Roman citizens observed.

**Cross-reference.** The republic's remedy takes up a claim of I.29, that among all the peoples that ever had empire Rome was the least ungrateful, and gives a cause for it from the ways of Rome's government. It also anticipates the case of the dictatorship, which Machiavelli argues at I.34. That a city fighting with its own citizens multiplies its men of *virtù* is a thread of the *Discorsi* from I.21 on, where a prince or republic without its own soldiers is blamed.

**History.** The dictatorship was an emergency magistracy, held for six months at most and commonly laid down as soon as its task was done. Livy reports L. Quinctius Cincinnatus resigning on the sixteenth day (3.29); Machiavelli names no dictator here.
