## NM-DISC.1.29.01

**Argument.** The question promised at the end of I.28: which uses ingratitude with greater examples, a people or a prince. Machiavelli disputes it by dividing the cause. The vice arises from one of two things, avarice or suspicion, and the chapter keeps the two apart to the last sentence.

## NM-DISC.1.29.02

**Argument.** The two causes are graded. When a people or a prince has sent a captain on an important expedition and he has won glory, the one who sent him is bound to reward him. To dishonour or offend him instead out of avarice is an error "that has no excuse", indeed an eternal infamy; yet many princes commit it, and Tacitus gives the reason, that repaying an injury is readier than repaying a benefit, since gratitude is a burden and revenge a gain. To fail to reward him, or rather to offend him, out of suspicion earns "some excuse", for people and prince alike. The distinction between no excuse and some excuse carries the rest of the chapter.

**History.** The Tacitus is *Histories* 4.3, written of the Flavian victors in the last days of 69, who punished Capua for its loyalty to Vitellius and gave nothing to Tarracina, which had suffered for their side.

## NM-DISC.1.29.03

**Argument.** Why suspicion follows victory. A captain who has won an empire for his lord gains, of necessity, so much reputation with his own soldiers, the enemy and the prince's subjects that the victory turns sour to the prince. Men's nature is ambitious and suspicious and sets no measure to its fortune: the prince's suspicion arises at once, and the captain's own insolence is bound to feed it. So the prince can think only of securing himself, either by having the captain die or by taking his reputation away and showing that the victory came from fortune, the enemy's cowardice or the other captains' prudence. The chain is offered as necessity, and the excuse granted at .02 rests on it.

## NM-DISC.1.29.04

**Argument.** The first proof, from antiquity. Antonius Primus took Vespasian's part, broke two Vitellian armies and occupied Rome, so that Mucianus, sent by Vespasian, found the work done. His reward: Mucianus took the army from him and left him in Rome without authority, and Vespasian received him so that he died, in Machiavelli's account, almost in despair.

**History.** Tacitus, *Histories* 3-4. Antonius led the Danube legions into Italy in the autumn of 69, won the second battle of Bedriacum near Cremona, and took Rome in December. Mucianus, governor of Syria, arrived after and took control; Antonius went to Vespasian at Alexandria and was received less warmly than he had hoped, though not with hostility (*Histories* 4.80). Tacitus does not record his death; he is known to have lived on into old age. Vespasian was in Egypt, not Asia.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not deny the story; he denies that it shows a prince's ingratitude. Mucianus is a fellow servant, not the prince, and what Vespasian failed to do arose not from suspicion but from his dislike of Antonius' insolent nature and, much more, his respect for Mucianus [[FG-CONS.1.29.04]].

## NM-DISC.1.29.05

**Argument.** The second proof, from "our times". Consalvo won the Kingdom of Naples for Ferdinand of Aragon; Ferdinand came to Naples, took the men-at-arms and then the fortresses from him, and took him back to Spain, where he died unhonoured. Machiavelli's conclusion lands on the prince: the suspicion is so natural that princes cannot defend themselves from it, and cannot use gratitude to those who have won great acquisitions under their ensigns.

**History.** Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, the Great Captain, defeated the French at Cerignola and the Garigliano in 1503 and governed Naples as viceroy. Ferdinand came in the autumn of 1506, left in June 1507 taking Gonzalo with him, and did not employ him again; Gonzalo died at Granada in 1515.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini answers that Ferdinand remunerated Gonzalo with states, had just suspicion of him, and that Consalvo lived on in Spain rich and most honoured [[FG-CONS.1.29.04]].

## NM-DISC.1.29.06

**Argument.** The people's case follows from the prince's: what a prince cannot defend himself from, it is no wonder that a people cannot. A free city has two ends, to acquire and to maintain itself free, and out of too much love it must err in both. Among the errors for liberty are to offend the citizens it ought to reward and to suspect those it ought to trust. Their effects are double. In a corrupted republic they are the cause of great ills, and often bring it to tyranny, as with Caesar, who took by force what ingratitude denied him. In a republic not corrupted they are the cause of great goods: the city lives the more free, and men keep themselves better and less ambitious for fear of punishment. Neither edge cancels the other, and the condition, corrupted or not, decides which holds.

**History.** Caesar's grievance in 50-49 BC was the Senate's demand that he lay down his command before standing for a second consulship; Machiavelli reads his seizure of power as taking by force what ingratitude denied him.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini takes up this second edge, as he reads it, at the close of his reply and calls it very far from the truth [[FG-CONS.1.29.06]].

## NM-DISC.1.29.07

**Argument.** Rome is the least ungrateful of all peoples that held empire, "for the causes discoursed above", I.28's less cause to suspect. Of its ingratitude, it can be said, there is no example but Scipio's. Coriolanus and Camillus are set aside: each was exiled for an injury he had done to the plebs, and Camillus was recalled and adored as a prince for the rest of his life.

**History.** Coriolanus went into exile in 491 BC and led the Volsci against Rome (Livy 2.35-40). Camillus was exiled in 391 over the spoils of Veii and recalled to save Rome from the Gauls (Livy 5.32, 5.46-55).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini puts Camillus back among Rome's examples, with Fabius Maximus, Cicero, Metellus and Rutilius [[FG-CONS.1.29.05]].

## NM-DISC.1.29.08

**Argument.** Scipio's case told as suspicion, with its causes listed: the greatness of the enemy he had vanquished, the reputation of the victory in so long and dangerous a war, its speed, and the favour his youth, prudence and *virtù* won him. The magistrates feared his authority, which displeased wise men as a thing unaccustomed in Rome, and Cato, deemed holy, was the first to act against him, saying that a city could not be called free where a citizen was feared by the magistrates. The excuse is conditional: if the people followed Cato, it merits the excuse of .02.

**History.** The attacks on the Scipios came in 187-184 BC, after the war with Antiochus; Africanus withdrew to Liternum and died there, and his brother Lucius Scipio Asiaticus was condemned and fined (Livy 38.50-60). Livy names Cato among those who pressed them.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini marvels that the case is excused: in his account Scipio's greatness was not fearsome, wise men were never displeased by it, and Cato acted from enmity or his hostility to the nobility, as he showed by pursuing Asiaticus after Scipio's death [[FG-CONS.1.29.05]].

## NM-DISC.1.29.09

**Argument.** The conclusion is a prospect, not a proof: it "will be seen" that peoples never used ingratitude through avarice, and through suspicion much less than princes, "having less cause to suspect", the phrase of I.28; the reason is deferred, "as will be said below". The answer to the rubric's question is given for each cause separately.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's reply keeps the division, adds ignorance and malignity rooted in envy, and does not believe a people less ungrateful than a prince [[FG-CONS.1.29.01]]. Through suspicion he believes a people suspects more lightly, though he confesses that princes bite harder [[FG-CONS.1.29.02]]; through ignorance and envy he believes it beyond comparison more ungrateful [[FG-CONS.1.29.03]]. His "as will be said below", of Rome's errors, repeats Machiavelli's closing words [[FG-CONS.1.29.02]].
