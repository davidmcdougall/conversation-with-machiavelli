## FG-CONS.1.10.01

**Argument.** He grants the title, most true, restating it in his own order (kingdoms first) and in his own word (blame for vituperation). Then the charge: the cases are various and the author confounds the examples. The first case is the man who occupies a tyranny in a free fatherland. Seldom does he have such necessity, or a necessity not of his own making, so that any colour of justification remains to him, and such men, Caesar among them, for all his other *virtù*, are most inhuman and detestable. But inside this case he opens another. Sometimes liberties are so disordered and cities so divided that a citizen who cannot otherwise save himself is driven to seek the tyranny or to join whoever seeks it; he would be laudable to put the fatherland before his safety, but since that love is more desired than found, he merits to be much excused, the more so if the government he goes against is disordered, for many are called liberties that are not. Florence is the example, in the first person: good citizens persecuted since the change of the state, and at the coming of the prince of Orange caught between the Eight's order to stay under pain of rebellion and the danger of staying, so that necessity has led them to desire the change of a state that is tyrannical under the name of liberty, or else to lose fatherland and substance. Justification and excuse stay apart: none for the usurper, much for the citizen. Then the verdict on the first case is restated: whoever makes a tyranny from appetite of dominating merits supreme reproof, Caesar, Phalaris, Pisistratus and the like, each more or less infamous by his cruelty and his other *virtù*.

**History.** The Medici were driven from Florence in May 1527, after the sack of Rome, and the popular government restored. In 1529 Clement VII and Charles V made peace at Barcelona, and from October the imperial army under Philibert of Chalon, prince of Orange, besieged the city; he was killed at Gavinana in August 1530, days before Florence surrendered. The Eight (the Otto di Guardia) were the magistracy of police and security. Guicciardini, who had served the Medici pope, left the city and was declared a rebel; the citizens of his example are, it seems, men of his own party. The present tenses (`è tirannico`, `ha condotti`) suggest the reply was written while the siege government stood. "Of '26" is as both editions print it; the change came in 1527.

**Cross-reference.** `Il titulo` is the rubric, which Machiavelli's first paragraph unfolds [[NM-DISC.1.10.01]]; `sommo biasimo` answers `sommamente vituperati` [[NM-DISC.1.10.03]]. Machiavelli named Caesar against Scipio, with Catiline, and among the emperors; Guicciardini keeps him with the makers of tyranny and adds Pisistratus, whom Machiavelli named in I.2 as the tyranny that arose under Solon's laws. "Excuse" is the word of I.9 and of Machiavelli's last lines here, "some excuse", "no excuse whatever" [[NM-DISC.1.10.08]]; Guicciardini gives it, much of it, to a man Machiavelli does not consider: the citizen who goes towards a tyranny out of necessity.

## FG-CONS.1.10.02

**Argument.** The second case: the heir to a tyranny. He merits less blame for continuing in it than those who founded it, and would merit the more praise for leaving it, since he is less a debtor for a usurpation he did not commit. Very few or perhaps none have left it without necessity, and no marvel. He cannot know the glory of putting the fatherland in liberty, since, nourished in power, he judges power the supreme good; and besides, the danger can hold him back even if he had the will: a tyranny is hard to acquire and keep without many enmities, so to reduce himself to private, or leave his sons private, seems dangerous, peoples being ungrateful and new liberties commonly disordered. Sulla, who laid down a power he had seized himself, not inherited, is the rare example, and he could do it more securely because his faction kept the government, so that he lived secure and his acts and memory were kept after him.

**History.** Sulla, dictator from 82/81 BC, gave up the dictatorship and retired to private life by 79, and died in 78; much of his legislation outlived him.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's argument was that security lay with the good, the tyrants living in danger [[NM-DISC.1.10.03]] [[NM-DISC.1.10.05]]; Guicciardini answers that for the tyrant's heir the danger lies in laying down. "To reduce oneself to private [station]" restates Machiavelli's "private station" [[NM-DISC.1.10.03]], and the heir's sons answer the emperors who succeeded by inheritance [[NM-DISC.1.10.06]].

## FG-CONS.1.10.03

**Argument.** The third case: kings and princes legitimately created, as at Sparta and in early Rome, or made legitimate by time. Some with unbound authority yet govern justly and merit the name of good prince; but he does not know which have brought the kingdom to the perfection that ought to be, succession by choice rather than by inheritance. Where such an institution has existed, he believes some necessity has kept it more than the will of those who reigned, for the love of fathers for their sons is too great, and their wish to leave their house's memory illustrious not small.

**History.** Sparta had two hereditary kings, of the Agiad and Eurypontid houses. Rome's kings after Romulus were chosen, after an interregnum, by the people with the senate's sanction.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's proof was the adoptive emperors from Nerva to Marcus [[NM-DISC.1.10.06]]; Guicciardini answers not with adoption but with election. The acts are distinct: he grants (`pure`), suspends (`non so`), and believes (`credo`). In his reply to I.2 his own design had the head of the government perpetual and `per elezione, non per successione`, and he faulted the Lacedaemonian kings as always of one family and by succession: here he does not know which king will choose what he himself prescribed, and where it has been chosen he credits necessity more than will.

## FG-CONS.1.10.04

**Argument.** The conclusion, by `però`. Tyrants laying down tyrannies and kings ordering their kingdoms against their posterity are thoughts painted more readily in books and imaginations than executed in fact; as often as private men reason about them, so rarely are they done. So those who do not do these things merit lesser reproof. The figure is the rubric's own proportion, turned against the book.

**Cross-reference.** "In books": the *Discorsi*, presumably, among them. Machiavelli granted "some excuse" to the prince who would not order the city if ordering meant laying down the principate, and "no excuse whatever" where it did not [[NM-DISC.1.10.08]]. Guicciardini's lesser reproof falls on the first case, close to Machiavelli's "some excuse"; what he denies is that the second is ever met, since tyrants are held back by danger and kings by love of their sons. Compare his reply to I.9, where the founder who lays down his authority is supremely laudable and perhaps Lycurgus alone.
