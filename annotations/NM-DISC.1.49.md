## NM-DISC.1.49.01

**Argument.** The claim comes first and Rome is its demonstration: how difficult it is, in ordering a republic, to provide for all the laws that may keep it free. Rome had laws from four kings and from the Ten, and still, in handling the city, new necessities kept being discovered and new orders had to be created. The censors are the instance, one of the provisions that aided in holding Rome free for as long as it lived in liberty, and the reason given is that, as arbiters of its customs, they were the principal cause that the Romans deferred longer corrupting themselves.

**History.** Livy places the beginning of the censorship in the year conventionally dated 443 BC (4.8). The kings named are four of the seven; the Ten are the decemvirs of 451-449 BC, Machiavelli's subject at I.40.

## NM-DISC.1.49.02

**Argument.** The new order had a first error, a term of five years, corrected by Mamercus as dictator with a law of eighteen months; the censors then in office took it ill and deprived him of the senate. The blame of plebs and fathers is recorded, and the inference is put as an alternative Machiavelli does not decide: either the historian is defective, since he does not show that Mamercus could defend himself, or the orders of Rome were not good in this part. The ground of the dilemma is general: it is not good that a republic be ordered so that a citizen can be offended without remedy for promulgating a law conformable to free living. The editor's reading: the episode serves the chapter's claim, since the remedy of one error exposed something the orders may not have provided for; Machiavelli does not draw the link himself.

**History.** Livy 4.24: Mamercus, dictator in the year conventionally dated 434 BC, carried a law limiting the censorship to a year and a half; the censors moved him from his tribe and made him an *aerarius* with an eightfold assessment. Livy does not say he was removed from the senate; the phrase is not Livy's. Livy has the leading senators offended at the censors' harshness and the people's indignation great.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not take up the historian's silence. He judges the censors' power itself an error, `Fu anche [e]rrore` [[FG-CONS.1.49.01]], and on the remedy does not believe there was any but a law of the people or restoration by later censors, which he is not certain they could do, though he believes it. His `defettivo`, of the Roman government, echoes Machiavelli's `difettivo` of the historian.

## NM-DISC.1.49.03

**Argument.** The return to the beginning of the discourse turns the Roman case into a comparison. If cities with a free beginning, and which by itself has governed itself, like Rome, have great difficulty in finding laws to keep them free, it is no marvel that cities with a servile beginning have not difficulty but impossibility of ever ordering themselves to live civilly and quietly. The rubric's `quasi una impossibilità` becomes `impossibilità` here. Florence is the instance: subjected to the Roman empire and always under others' government, it began its own orders when it could breathe, mixed them with old wicked ones, and has handled itself for two hundred years of true memory without a state that could truly be called a republic. The difficulties are general to cities with such beginnings, and reform by few citizens given ample authority has always served their party and made greater disorder. The argument runs from the greater to the less, from Rome's difficulty to the servile cities' impossibility, and Machiavelli extends it to all cities with such beginnings: `sono state sempre in tutte`.

**Cross-reference.** The emended `si è r[e]tto` answers Florence `sotto governo d'altri` in the same period and Rome `ordinata da sé medesima` at the close [[NM-DISC.1.49.06]]; the 1824's `rotto` would break both echoes. Florence's beginning is Machiavelli's subject at I.1.

**History.** The grants of ample authority to a few citizens describe what Florence called balìe; Machiavelli uses neither the word nor a date, and does not date the start of the two hundred years.

## NM-DISC.1.49.04

**Argument.** The particular example is one thing an orderer of a republic must examine: in whose hands he sets the authority of blood. Rome ordered it well by an ordinary appeal to the people, and for the case where deferring execution by appeal was dangerous, it had the refuge of the dictator, who executed at once, a remedy used only of necessity. The two Roman provisions answer each other: the ordinary appeal, and, where delay was dangerous, the refuge of the dictator, used only of necessity. The dictator is not named as a danger here.

**Cross-reference.** The appeal to the plebs is mentioned at I.32 and is at issue with the Ten at I.40; the dictator is the matter of I.34, which this edition does not pair.

## NM-DISC.1.49.05

**Argument.** Florence and the cities born like it, being servile, had this authority placed in a stranger sent by the prince; free, they maintained it in a stranger called the Captain, which, since he could readily be corrupted by powerful citizens, was a most pernicious thing; then, through the change of states, eight citizens took the office, and the order went from bad to very bad, because the few were always ministers of the few and of the most powerful. Venice guarded itself against this: its ten citizens may punish without appeal, and because ten would not be enough against the powerful, it added the Quarantie and gave the Pregadi power to chastise them, so that neither accuser nor judge fails. Rome and Venice are set against Florence and the cities born like it. Venice's Ten punish without appeal, and Machiavelli approves the arrangement because the Quarantie and the Pregadi ensure there is a judge for the powerful. The editor's observation: the Roman safeguard was the appeal, the Venetian the number of judges.

**Cross-reference.** The cause said "at other times" is said at I.7, among other places: `pochi sempre fanno a modo de' pochi`, of accusing a powerful man before eight judges. Guicciardini's reply does not take up the Florentine or Venetian examples.

**History.** The eight citizens are presumably the Otto di Guardia; Machiavelli does not name the magistracy. The ten citizens of Venice are its Council of Ten. The Pregadi were the Venetian senate; Machiavelli's `il Consiglio maggiore` may be comparative, larger than the Ten and the Forty, and is not Venice's Great Council (*Maggior Consiglio*).

## NM-DISC.1.49.06

**Argument.** The close draws the comparison again from the Roman side: if Rome, ordered by itself and by so many prudent men, had new causes rising every day that required new orders for free living, it is no marvel that cities with a more disordered beginning meet difficulties such that they can never be reordered. The parenthesis carries the ground, and the conclusion is stated without the rubric's `quasi`.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not answer the comparison of beginnings at all; his reply [[FG-CONS.1.49.01]] uses this chapter, with many others, as evidence that, military discipline set aside, the Roman government was in many parts defective. The consuls he cites are those of I.50, the editor's identification.
