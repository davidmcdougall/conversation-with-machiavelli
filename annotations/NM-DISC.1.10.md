## NM-DISC.1.10.01

**Argument.** A ranking of reputations. Most praised are the heads and orderers of religions; next, the founders of republics and kingdoms; then the captains who have expanded their kingdom or their fatherland; then men of letters, each by his degree; then everyone else, for his art. Against them stand the infamous and detestable: destroyers of religions, dissipaters of kingdoms and republics, enemies of *virtù*, letters and the arts. No one, mad or wise, wicked or good, offered the choice between the two kinds, would fail to praise the first and blame the second. The chapter rests on that agreement: everyone already knows which is to be praised.

**Cross-reference.** The ranking puts founders of religion above founders of states, which is where the next chapter goes: Numa, who gave Rome its religion, is set above Romulus in I.11 (not in this edition's corpus; I.12 follows from it). The founders of republics and kingdoms are I.9's subject.

## NM-DISC.1.10.02

**Argument.** The turn. Nevertheless almost all, deceived by a false good and a false glory, go, voluntarily or ignorantly, among those who merit more blame than praise; able to make a republic or a kingdom with perpetual honour, they turn to tyranny. The case against them is made in the chooser's own currency: fame, glory, honour, security, quiet and satisfaction of spirit, which they flee, against infamy, disgrace, blame, danger and disquiet, which they incur. Security (`sicurtà`) is the thread to the end of the chapter.

## NM-DISC.1.10.03

**Argument.** The proof is conditional. It is impossible that private men in a republic, or men become its princes, who read the histories and set store by the memories of ancient things, should not wish, the private men, to be Scipios rather than Caesars, and the princes Agesilauses, Timoleons and Dions rather than Nabises, Phalarises and Dionysiuses: the histories vituperate the second and praise the first. And the good had no less authority than the tyrants, and far more security. By implication, those who choose tyranny have not read the histories, or have not set store by them.

**History.** Scipio Africanus, victor over Hannibal, who withdrew to Liternum rather, as the tradition has it, than set himself above the laws; Julius Caesar. Agesilaus II, king of Sparta; Timoleon of Corinth, who freed Syracuse from its tyrants and laid down his command; Dion, who drove out Dionysius II. Against them Nabis, tyrant of Sparta; Phalaris, tyrant of Acragas, proverbial for the bronze bull; and Dionysius of Syracuse (the elder and the younger both in view). Plutarch wrote lives of Timoleon, Dion and Agesilaus. The examples do not all bear out the claim of security: Dion was murdered in 354 BC, and Dionysius I died in power after nearly four decades.

**Cross-reference.** The private man who becomes prince "by fortune or *virtù*" is the case Guicciardini takes up first: the man who occupies a tyranny in a free fatherland [[FG-CONS.1.10.01]]. His reply turns Machiavelli's "private station" on the heir, for whom "to reduce oneself to private [station]" is the danger [[FG-CONS.1.10.02]].

## NM-DISC.1.10.04

**Argument.** Caesar's glory is no counter-example. The writers who praise him were corrupted by his fortune and frightened by the length of the rule that bore his name, and could not speak freely of him. What free writers would say of him can be read in what writers say of Catiline, who only attempted what Caesar did; and the writers who could not blame Caesar, because of his power, praised Brutus, his enemy, instead. The one explicit verdict is a proportion: Caesar is the more detestable as the deed is worse than the wish.

**History.** Catiline's conspiracy of 63 BC, known chiefly through Sallust and Cicero; Marcus Junius Brutus, one of Caesar's assassins in 44 BC.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini agrees on Caesar, though he grants him "many other *virtù*": among the most inhuman and detestable, and named first among those who merit supreme reproof [[FG-CONS.1.10.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.10.05

**Argument.** The imperial evidence, offered to the prince who has risen in a republic. The emperors who lived under the laws were defended by their customs, the people's benevolence and the senate's love, and needed no praetorians; the villainous were not saved by the armies of east and west from the enemies their lives made. The count: of twenty-six emperors from Caesar to Maximinus, sixteen killed, ten dead ordinarily. The exceptions are explained inside the same period: the good who were killed (Galba, Pertinax) were killed by the corruption their predecessors left in the soldiery; the villainous one who died ordinarily (Severus) was carried by a fortune and *virtù* few men join.

**History.** Machiavelli's sources for the emperors include the Latin lives of the Caesars and Herodian, whose history runs from the death of Marcus to 238, through the reign of Maximinus Thrax (235–238); the count is his own. Galba was killed in 69, Pertinax in 193 by the praetorians; Septimius Severus died at York in 211. The later part of this set, from Marcus to Maximinus, and the same judgement of Severus' *virtù*, is the matter of *The Prince*, chapter 19.

## NM-DISC.1.10.06

**Argument.** The lesson for ordering a kingdom. All the emperors who succeeded by inheritance were bad, except Titus; all who succeeded by adoption were good, the five from Nerva to Marcus; and when the rule fell again to heirs, it returned into its ruin. Let a prince compare the ages and choose in which he would wish to have been born, or to which he would wish to be appointed.

**History.** Nerva (96–98), himself chosen by the senate, not adopted, adopted Trajan; Trajan Hadrian (an adoption reported at his deathbed and doubted in antiquity); Hadrian Antoninus Pius; Antoninus Marcus Aurelius. Marcus was succeeded by his son Commodus (180–192), with whom, for Machiavelli, the ruin returns.

**Cross-reference.** Inheritance against adoption carries I.9's condition that the founder must not leave his authority hereditary. Guicciardini answers not with adoption but with succession by choice, his own design in I.2; he does not know which kings have made it so, and where the institution has lasted he believes necessity kept it more than the kings' will [[FG-CONS.1.10.03]].

## NM-DISC.1.10.07

**Argument.** The two ages as a vision the prince "will see". Under the good: a secure prince among secure citizens, the senate with its authority, the magistracies with their honours, every licence and ambition extinguished, the golden times in which each may hold and defend his opinion. Under the others: wars, seditions, civil and external wars, Italy afflicted, Rome burned, the Capitol undone by its citizens, exiles, accusers rewarded, servants and freedmen corrupted against their lords and masters, *virtù* imputed as a capital sin. The catalogue ends in irony: then he will know how many debts Rome, Italy and the world have to Caesar.

**History.** Nearly the whole of the bad age, from "atrocious through wars" to "oppressed by their friends", follows Tacitus, *Histories* 1.2 (*atrox proeliis, discors seditionibus, ipsa etiam pace saevum*; four princes killed by the sword; the Capitol burned by its citizens; the sea full of exiles, the rocks stained with blood; nobility, wealth and honours treated as crimes; informers rewarded; slaves and freedmen corrupted against their masters; those without enemies destroyed by friends). Tacitus' subject is the years 69–96; Machiavelli extends it to every bad reign. The opinion freely held echoes *Histories* 1.1.

**Cross-reference.** "Debts" anticipates I.12's irony: the debt Italians owe the Church.

## NM-DISC.1.10.08

**Argument.** The close raises the stakes of I.9. A corrupt city is the greatest occasion of glory the heavens can give, to be reordered as Romulus did, not spoiled wholly as Caesar did. If ordering a city required laying down the principate, the prince who would not do it, so as not to fall from his degree, would merit some excuse; but since the principate can be kept and the city ordered, no excuse whatever is merited. Two paths: one secure in life and glorious after death, the other in continual straits and leaving an everlasting infamy.

**Cross-reference.** "Excuse" is I.9's word for Romulus. Guicciardini's last word falls on the case Machiavelli conceded: those who do not lay down a tyranny, or disinherit their posterity, merit "lesser reproof" [[FG-CONS.1.10.04]], close to Machiavelli's "some excuse". His quarrel is with the condition, that the principate can be kept and the city ordered: his second and third cases say that tyrants are held back by danger and kings by love of their sons [[FG-CONS.1.10.02]] [[FG-CONS.1.10.03]].
