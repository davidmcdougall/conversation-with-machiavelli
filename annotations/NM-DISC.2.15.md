## NM-DISC.2.15.01

**Argument.** The chapter stays with the beginnings of the Latin war that II.13 and II.14 treated, and draws from them a rule for counsel: in every consultation it is good to come to the individual point of what is to be deliberated, and not to stand always in the ambiguous or on the uncertain of the thing. The Latins' consultation shows it. The Romans, sensing the Latins' bad humour and wishing to regain them without arms if they could, asked for eight citizens to consult with; the Latins, conscious of much done against Rome's will, met in council to choose who must go and settle what they were to say, and Annius, their praetor, told them to think more of what was to be done than of what was to be said: once counsels are unfolded, words are easily fitted to things.

**History.** Livy tells of the Roman summons of the leading Latins, and of Annius' speech in the Latin council, at 8.3-8.4 (the editor's references).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not mention the Latin council. His reply opens on the word this paragraph sets against coming to the point, the ambiguity of deliberations [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.15.02

**Argument.** Machiavelli calls Annius' words most true, words that must be relished by every prince and every republic: in ambiguity and incertitude about what one wishes to do, one does not know how to accommodate the words; once the spirit is fixed and the thing to be executed deliberated, it is easy to find them. He has noted this part the more willingly because he has many times known such ambiguity harm public business, with damage and shame to "our republic", Florence. And it will always happen, in doubtful courses and where spirit is required to deliberate them, that this ambiguity arises when they are to be counselled and deliberated by weak men. "Weak", the rubric's word, first appears in the body of the chapter of men; the next paragraph gives weakness of spirit and of forces [[NM-DISC.2.15.03]].

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini takes the weakness to be of prudence and mind, not of forces, and believes that is what the Discourse meant, though weakness of forces, he allows, can in part increase irresolution [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]]. Machiavelli's "spirit" is not Guicciardini's "mind", and Machiavelli keeps forces beside it.

## NM-DISC.2.15.03

**Argument.** Slow and tardy deliberations are no less harmful than ambiguous ones, above all those to be taken in favour of a friend: slowness aids no one and harms oneself. Machiavelli gives them two sources, weakness of spirit and of forces, or the malignity of those who are to deliberate, who, from a passion to ruin the state or to fulfil a desire of their own, impede and cross the deliberation. Good citizens, even when they see a popular rush turning to the pernicious side, will never impede deliberating, above all on things that do not wait for time. The last sentence is the ground of the charge of malignity: obstruction is what good citizens do not do.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini keeps malignity as one ground of varied opinion in republics, beside a difference of judgments without malignity, as happens often even among the prudent [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]]; he does not take up the friend in need, and he answers slowness in the Lavinians' case and directly at the end, by distinguishing tardiness from irresolution from tardiness done deliberately.

## NM-DISC.2.15.04

**Argument.** The first example. After the death of Hieronymus, with the war great between the Carthaginians and the Romans, the Syracusans were divided whether to follow Rome or Carthage, and the ardour of the parties left the thing ambiguous and no course taken, until Apollonides showed that neither party was to be blamed, but the ambiguity and tardiness in taking a course was to be detested, since he saw in such ambiguity altogether the ruin of the republic, while any course taken offered some hope of good. Machiavelli judges that Livy could not show more than he does here the damage that "standing suspended" draws behind it.

**History.** Hieronymus was killed in 214 BC; the debate in the Syracusan assembly and Apollonides' speech are in Livy 24.28 (the editor's references). Machiavelli gives the gist of the speech, not its words.

**Cross-reference.** "Standing suspended" is the phrase Guicciardini turns into "stand suspended" and the noun "suspension": "these suspensions are commonly pernicious, because while you stand suspended you cannot provide for either the one case or the other" [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.15.05

**Argument.** The second example, from the same Latin war. The Lavinians, asked by the Latins for aid against Rome, deferred so long that their troops were just out of the gate when news came of the Latin rout; their praetor Milionius said that this little of the path would cost them much with the Roman people. Had they deliberated earlier, either way, they would have been better placed: not aiding, they would not have irritated Rome; aiding in time, they could have helped the Latins win; deferring, they lost in any case.

**History.** Livy 8.11.3-4 (the editor's reference). In modern texts of Livy the people are the Lanuvini, of Lanuvium (the editor's identification); Machiavelli and Guicciardini both write Lavinians.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini grants that suspensions are commonly pernicious and takes up the Lavinians inside that concession: if suspensions are sometimes useful, as they would have been to the Lavinians had they delayed three or four days more, that utility comes rather by chance than otherwise [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.15.06

**Argument.** Machiavelli turns to Florence. Had the Florentines noted this text, they would have been spared many damages and vexations at the passage of Louis XII against Lodovico of Milan. Louis sought an accord; the Florentine orators agreed with him that Florence stand neutral and that he was to maintain the city in its state and take it into protection, with a month to ratify. Whoever, through little prudence, favoured Lodovico deferred the ratification until Louis was on the point of victory, and then he refused it, as one who knew the Florentines had come to his friendship forced, not of their will. It cost Florence much money, and it was about to lose its state, as later, another time, befell it for a similar cause. The course was the more damnable because it did not even serve Lodovico (`ancora`, "even"; the editor's reading, "yet" being possible), who, had he won, would have shown many more signs of enmity to the Florentines than the King did. In the editor's reading, the case shows the rubric's second half, harmful slowness, which .07 assigns to "this weakness"; the delay is laid on whoever favoured Lodovico, not on the city as a whole.

**History.** Louis XII's descent on Milan was in 1499; Florence's agreement with him and its delayed ratification belong to the same year, when Machiavelli was secretary of the Second Chancery (the editor's account). The "other time" is very probably 1512 (the editor's identification): the republic, which had not joined the Holy League, fell to the League's Spanish army, and the Medici returned. Machiavelli does not name it.

**Cross-reference.** In the editor's reading, this neutrality is a case for Guicciardini's distinction: standing neutral can also proceed from resolution, not from suspension; from suspension it is reprehensible, from resolution it can be both useful and pernicious, according to the quality of the cases, which he says is not now his matter [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]]. He does not name the Florentine case.

## NM-DISC.2.15.07

**Argument.** Machiavelli acknowledges that it has been discoursed in another chapter of the ill that arises to republics from this weakness, and repeats it because a new accident gave him occasion, and because it seems to him above all a matter to be noted by republics similar to ours. "This weakness" brings the last paragraph back to the rubric's weak states.

**History.** The other chapter is not named; the editor takes it to be Discourses I.38, on weak republics that are badly resolved and cannot take a course.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's reply ends where Machiavelli's chapter does not go: on deferral done deliberately, which can be laudable [[FG-CONS.2.15.01]].
