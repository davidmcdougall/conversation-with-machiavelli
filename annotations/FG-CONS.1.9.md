## FG-CONS.1.9.01

**Argument.** He grants the thesis without reserve — there is no doubt that one alone can set better order than many — and goes beyond Machiavelli's excuse to praise: the man who reorders a disordered city by violence, fraud and extraordinary ways, when he cannot otherwise, merits it. Then the turn. Pray that republics never need it. Men's spirits are fallacious, and the honest colour can cover a tyranny; and even a good will is in danger, because the founder cannot lay down his authority as soon as the laws are constituted, since laws brought in by violence would forthwith be annihilated; he must hold it until time and experience establish them, and in that space the sweetness of power and the licence of the principate can turn his intention to ill. So it is a way of medicine desirable when there is no other hope of health, but dangerous and of ill example. Two degrees of founder follow. Supremely laudable is the one who keeps the authority no longer than the ordering needs, as Lycurgus did, and any other, if one can be adduced; of the one who keeps it for life, although he governs rightly and leaves a good form of government, he does not know how far he is to be praised, since his motive cannot be interpreted except as ambition, and though what he has done is useful and he is not detestable, as is he who uses ill the authority occupied, he is not free of all reproof. On Romulus and the senate he says nothing yet: the life must first be read, and, if he remembers well, it was suspected that the senate killed Romulus for arrogating too much authority. He ends without a stop.

**History.** Lycurgus, in Plutarch's *Life*, had the Spartans swear to keep his laws until he returned, and then left Sparta for good, so that the oath would bind them. The tradition that the senators killed Romulus is in Livy (1.16), who reports it as a whisper against the official account of his apotheosis, and in Plutarch (*Romulus* 26–27) and Dionysius of Halicarnassus (2.56), who give the motive Guicciardini gives: Romulus had grown too absolute. Canestrini's note, which is his and is not carried, says that the observation on Romulus' life alludes to Machiavelli's verdict that Romulus merits excuse and not blame.

**Cross-reference.** `Uno solo` is Machiavelli's `un solo` [[NM-DISC.1.9.02]], though the grant is comparative where Machiavelli's rule is necessary; `merita laude` answers `meritare scusa, e non biasimo` [[NM-DISC.1.9.05]]; `racconcie` is Machiavelli's `racconciare`, the violence that mends [[NM-DISC.1.9.02]]; `di malo esemplo` answers the `cattivo esempio` the many judge [[NM-DISC.1.9.01]]; `occupare la tirannide` is what the ephors feared of Agis [[NM-DISC.1.9.04]]. Machiavelli forbade bequeathing the authority and wanted the thing left to many; Guicciardini praises the founder who lays it down, adds that he cannot do so at once, and makes Lycurgus, one of Machiavelli's "infinite examples" [[NM-DISC.1.9.04]], the model. Machiavelli's proof is the senate [[NM-DISC.1.9.03]]; Guicciardini's hedged memory makes the senate the suspect. The medicine is the figure of his reply to I.4, where the tribunes were a good remedy for a sickness that need not have been.
