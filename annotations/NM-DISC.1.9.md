## NM-DISC.1.9.01

**Argument.** A turn in the *Discorsi*. Answering the reader who may think he has run on into Roman history without a word about Rome's orderers or the orders of its religion and soldiery, Machiavelli comes back to the founder. He states the objection first: many, it may be, will judge it of bad example that Romulus, the founder of a civil living, killed his brother and consented to the death of his companion in the kingdom, judging that his citizens could then, with their prince's authority, out of ambition and desire to command, offend whoever opposed them. The opinion would be true if one did not consider the end. The chapter is the consideration of that end.

**History.** Remus was killed at the founding (Livy 1.7, which gives two versions); Titus Tatius, king of the Sabines, shared the kingdom with Romulus by treaty after the war over the Sabine women (1.13), so that Machiavelli's "chosen by him" is his own, and was killed at Lavinium; Romulus, Livy says, took it less hard than was right (1.14).

**Cross-reference.** Religion follows from I.11 (I.12 and I.14 in this edition); the soldiery comes much later in the work. Guicciardini takes up the end and turns it to the man: who can read the founder's intention, and what power will do to it [[FG-CONS.1.9.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.9.02

**Argument.** The rule: a republic or kingdom is never, or seldom, ordered well from the beginning, or reformed wholly anew, unless by one, on whose mind the whole ordering depends. So a prudent orderer whose aim is the common good and the common fatherland, not himself or his succession, must contrive to have the authority alone, and no wise mind will reprove anyone for an extraordinary action used to order a kingdom or constitute a republic. It is fitting that, the deed accusing him, the effect excuse him, and a good effect always will: he who is violent to spoil, not he who is violent to mend, is to be reproved. Then the limits. He must not leave the authority hereditary, because men are readier for ill than good and a successor may use ambitiously what he used virtuously; and the thing ordered lasts only when it remains in the care of many, who cannot order a thing together, since their opinions divide them, but will not agree to give it up once they know its good.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini grants the thesis, in a comparative form: one alone can set better order than many. Machiavelli forbade bequeathing the authority and wanted the thing left to many; Guicciardini praises the founder who lays the authority down, and says he cannot do so at once [[FG-CONS.1.9.01]]. On the many as keepers of what they could not have ordered, compare I.58, still to come.

## NM-DISC.1.9.03

**Argument.** The proof that Romulus met the conditions. He at once ordered a senate, with which he might take counsel and according to whose opinion he might deliberate, and kept for himself only the command of the armies in a war already deliberated and the right to assemble the senate. The proof of the proof is that when the Tarquins were driven out nothing of the old order was innovated, except that two annual consuls took the place of a perpetual king: Rome's first orders were more conformable to a civil and free living than to an absolute and tyrannical one.

**History.** The senate of a hundred *patres* is Livy 1.8; the expulsion of the Tarquins and the first consuls, traditionally 509 BC, Livy 1.60 and 2.1, where Livy says the change was in the length of the office rather than in its power.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini will not take this proof yet: the life of Romulus must first be read, and, if he remembers well, the senate was suspected of killing him for arrogating too much authority [[FG-CONS.1.9.01]]. In his reply to I.2 he had counted the annual consuls among Rome's defects against Venice.

## NM-DISC.1.9.04

**Argument.** Infinite examples, Moses, Lycurgus, Solon, are left aside as known; one less celebrated is given for those who would order good laws. Agis, wishing to bring Sparta back within Lycurgus' bounds, was killed by the ephors as a man who wished to occupy the tyranny. Cleomenes, finding Agis' records and writings and the same desire in himself, came to know that he could do this good to his fatherland only if he became alone in authority, it seeming to him that, because of men's ambition, he could not do what was useful to many against the longing of few; he had the ephors and whoever else could oppose him killed, and renewed Lycurgus' laws. The deliberation was apt to revive Sparta and give Cleomenes the reputation Lycurgus had, but for the power of Macedon and the weakness of the other Greek republics; attacked, inferior in forces, without refuge, he was conquered, and the design, however just and laudable, remained imperfect. Read as a pair, the two kings are the demonstration: the reformer who did not make himself alone was killed, the one who did, by violence, nearly succeeded.

**History.** Agis IV (king c. 244–241 BC) proposed a cancellation of debts and a redistribution of land and was executed on the ephors' order. Cleomenes III (king c. 235–222) killed four of the five ephors (c. 227; Machiavelli's "all" is his), abolished the ephorate, cancelled debts and redistributed land, and was defeated by Antigonus Doson of Macedon at Sellasia (c. 222), dying in exile in Egypt. Machiavelli's source is probably Plutarch's *Agis and Cleomenes*, perhaps in a Latin version. The 1554 edition has Cleomenes summon the ephors rather than kill them (NM-DISC-I9-D007).

**Cross-reference.** Lycurgus is the founder of I.2 and I.6; Moses returns from I.1, Solon from I.2. Guicciardini makes Lycurgus the founder who laid the authority down, and leaves open whether there is another [[FG-CONS.1.9.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.9.05

**Argument.** The conclusion, in the rubric's words: to order a republic one must be alone, and Romulus, for Remus and Tatius, merits excuse, and not blame. Excuse, and no more than excuse: the verdict claims what the argument proved.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini answers `merita laude`, praise, for the man who reorders a disordered city by violence when there is no other way, and then grades the founders and suspends judgment on the one who keeps power for life [[FG-CONS.1.9.01]]. I.10 turns from the founders of republics and kingdoms to the founders of tyrannies.
