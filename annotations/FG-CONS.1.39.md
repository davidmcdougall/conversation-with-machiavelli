## FG-CONS.1.39.01

**Argument.** The reply opens on a negated belief: he does not believe the Florentines' complaint against the Ten was wholly without reason. It is not a concession to Machiavelli, and not a defence of the people's decision. The reason is the office itself: under the ancient orders of the city, made in another species of government, the Ten had more authority than a well-ordered liberty bears, to make peace, war, truces and leagues without others, to hire captains at will, to spend all the money without appropriation or bridle, and generally, in the things of war, as much authority as the Florentine people. From that excess, in good part, arose the popular opinion against creating the office again. Then experience showed both sides, under one "although": too much authority was pernicious, and lacking a magistracy of prudent men in difficult times was most damaging. Taught by the cudgellings what reason had not taught them, the Florentines created the Ten anew, with their authority limited, in the things judged dangerous, by the participation of the Eighty; after that the office was created even in peacetime without difficulty, under a new name, Ten of Liberty and Peace, not, as before, of Balìa, the name given for the absolute authority they had.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's generality blamed the Ten for the war and its cost, and the people, seeing its error, recognised the fever and not the physician [[NM-DISC.1.39.02]]. Guicciardini gives the popular opinions against the office a cause of his own, in good part its too absolute authority; on the cause of the war, and the figure of the fever, he says nothing. He takes up Machiavelli's words, the complaint, the magistracy, the deliberation, pernicious, and gives "pernicious" to the excess of authority rather than to the lapse. Machiavelli said the people remade the Ten; Guicciardini says how, with limits, and the verb of the limit is the one Machiavelli gave Terentilius' five men [[NM-DISC.1.39.04]].

**History.** Canestrini's notes, not carried, describe the Ten as an old magistracy that often changed its name and number, and the Eighty as a council drawn from the Great Council. Guicciardini gives no dates.

## FG-CONS.1.39.02

**Argument.** The Roman example is not similar. The consuls' authority, when they were not on campaign, was in no part absolute: it was subject to appeal to the people, impeded by the tribunes' intercession, and in grave things rather the executrix of the senate's views than mistress. Therefore there was less cause to moderate it; on the contrary, it was a movement wholly seditious, aimed at a government entirely popular and licentious; the subject is unstated, most naturally Terentilius' attempt. Whence, even though the plebs was then strong and much given to striking at the patrician magistrates, the consular authority, being not too great but moderate and fitting, defended them more readily. The reply ends on the measure the Ten had lacked.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's three limits are all limits at home, and he leaves the consuls' authority in the field unjudged; it was outside the city that Machiavelli's people thought the nobles would oppress it under the consuls, and it thought it necessary either to remove the consuls or so to regulate their power that they would have authority over the people neither outside nor at home [[NM-DISC.1.39.03]]. He does not take up Machiavelli's premise that the same humours recur in all peoples [[NM-DISC.1.39.01]]; he denies the likeness of the two examples on which the chapter rests its case. Machiavelli's Romans hated the consular name more than the authority [[NM-DISC.1.39.04]]; Guicciardini speaks only of the authority.

**History.** The appeal to the people and the tribunes' intercession are the Roman *provocatio* and *intercessio*. The edition's English for `provocazione` is the "appeal" of Machiavelli's `appellazione` at I.32.
