## NM-DISC.1.14.01

**Argument.** The chapter continues Machiavelli's run on Roman religion. The rubric states the thesis before the example, in three parts: the Romans read the auspices by necessity, kept up the show of observing them when they did not, and punished anyone who despised them rashly. The chapter opens by restating how much the auguries mattered, so that what follows reads as the management of something important, not contempt for something trivial.

**History.** The *pullarii* kept the sacred chickens that went with a Roman army. Before battle the birds were offered food; if they ate so greedily that grain fell from their beaks, the sign was favourable (Cicero, *De divinatione* 2.72).

## NM-DISC.1.14.02

**Argument.** The thesis in small: when reason said a thing should be done and the auspices were against it, the Romans did it anyway, but dressed the doing in "terms and modes" that kept it from looking like contempt of religion. Papirius is introduced as the man who used this "term" before his story is told.

## NM-DISC.1.14.03

**History.** The story is Livy's (10.40), from the Third Samnite War: the battle of Aquilonia, 293 BC, under the consul Lucius Papirius Cursor. In Livy the consul tells his nephew that if the keeper has lied, the omen falls on the keeper's own head; the keeper, posted in the front line, is struck down by a stray javelin.

**Argument.** Machiavelli tells it as a demonstration. The "prince of the *pullarii*" who reports a favourable sign is the liar the dart later kills: the consul turns the lie into a true sign by putting the liar where the gods can punish him, and fights with his army's piety intact. Every step is a management of appearances. The reply reads the same steps as ordinary procedure [[FG-CONS.1.14.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.14.04

**History.** "Appius Pulcher" is Publius Claudius Pulcher, consul in 249 BC, who had the chickens thrown into the sea before the sea-fight off Drepana and lost his fleet (Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.7). Machiavelli gives him his father's name, Appius.

**Argument.** The counter-example, and the third clause of the rubric: rash contempt, punished. Machiavelli says the two men were judged not so much for losing and winning as for going against the auspices "prudently" and "rashly". Both went against them. Read strictly, the second "the one ... the other" would make Appius the prudent one, since he was "the one" who lost; the sense requires the reverse, and the English keeps the Italian's looseness.

## NM-DISC.1.14.05

**Argument.** The conclusion is functional: this way of taking the auspices existed to send soldiers confidently into battle, and confidence almost always wins. Guicciardini does not dispute that; what he doubts is the rubric's picture of captains working the auspices knowingly [[FG-CONS.1.14.01]].
