## NM-DISC.1.25.01

**Argument.** The precept, its condition and its reason, in one period. Whoever would reform the state of a city, if he wants the reform accepted and maintained with the satisfaction of each, is necessitated to retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways, so that it may not seem to the peoples that the order has changed, even though the new orders are in fact altogether alien from the old. The reason is a claim about the generality of men: they feed on what seems as much as on what is, and many times are moved more by what seems. The precept is conditional: it binds whoever wants the reform accepted and maintained.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini answers this period by ranking the cases it fits [[FG-CONS.1.25.01]]: reform within a species of government, where it is more necessary, against a change of species, where it is less.

## NM-DISC.1.25.02

**Argument.** The Roman proof, in two cases, and each keeps something different. When the kings were expelled and two consuls created in place of one king, the consuls were given no more than the twelve lictors the king had had, so as not to pass the old number: the number kept. And since a yearly sacrifice could be made only by the king, the Romans, so that the people should not come to desire, through the absence of the kings, anything of the ancient things, created a head of that sacrifice, called him king, and subjected him to the supreme priest: the name kept, and the man set under the supreme priest. The people were satisfied, and never had the want of it as a cause to desire the kings' return.

**History.** Livy tells of both. Livy's first consuls keep all the kings' rights and insignia, and the only caution is that both should not have the fasces at once, so that the terror should not seem doubled (2.1.8); the kings' twelve lictors go back to Romulus (1.8). Of the *rex sacrificulus*, that because some public rites had been performed by the kings in person, a king of sacrifices was created so that the kings should nowhere be missed, and set under the *pontifex* lest the name, joined to an honour, should stand in the way of liberty (2.2.1-2). Machiavelli's reason for the second case, the people's desire, is Livy's; the first he turns into the keeping of a number.

## NM-DISC.1.25.03

**Argument.** The general precept, its terms, and its limit. Whoever would cancel an ancient living in a city and bring it to a new and free one must observe this: since new things alter men's minds, the alterations should retain as much of the old as possible, and if the magistracies vary in number, authority and time, they should at least keep the name. The Roman cases return in this list: the lictors were a matter of number, the king of sacrifices a matter of name. The last period, "as I have said", fixes the precept's scope: it holds for whoever would order an absolute power by the path of a republic or a kingdom; whoever would make the absolute power the authors call tyranny must renew every thing, as the following chapter will say.

**Cross-reference.** I.26, whose rubric is that a new prince must make every thing new in a city or province taken by him, is the next chapter in this edition, and Guicciardini answers it too.

**Text.** The 1554 edition drops the words between the first `assoluta` and the second, so that the sentence runs on without the contrast between republic or kingdom and tyranny.
