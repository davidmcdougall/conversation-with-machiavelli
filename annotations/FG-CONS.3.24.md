## FG-CONS.3.24.01

**Argument.** The reply has the shape of a grant followed by a relocation of the principal ground. It opens on assent, "there is no doubt": the prorogation of commands was a great occasion to whoever wished to occupy the republic, because it was an instrument for making friends of the soldiers and a following with kings and in foreign nations or provinces, and it increased the captains' wealth, with which they could corrupt men, as the long command of Gaul did for Caesar. Guicciardini's word is `prorogazione`, the second of Machiavelli's two words, and an occasion is not a cause (the editor's reading).

The principal foundation follows at once, introduced by `Ma`: the principal foundation of the ills was the corruption of the city, which, given to avarice and delights, had degenerated from its ancient customs, so that the sanguinary divisions arose from which free peoples always come to tyranny. Four consequences are drawn, from the corruption or from the divisions it bred, by the anaphora "from there ... from here": the facility of corrupting citizens and soldiers; one Catiline's hope of occupying the republic without command or armies; the conspiracy of several powerful men to divide the commands and armies among themselves; and the extraordinary prorogations, Caesar's among them, whose cause was not the utility of the republic, the necessity of war or the admiration of his *virtù*, but the conspiracy with Pompey and Crassus (the editor's reading). Prorogation thus appears twice, first as an occasion to those who wished to occupy the republic, then as an effect of corruption (the editor's reading).

Of Sulla, Guicciardini says that the command had not been prorogued to him when he first came to blows with Marius; the cause was the division between nobility and plebs, the plebs having Marius for head, so that the nobility was forced to seek one too. That this turns Machiavelli's example against him is the editor's reading. The conclusion is introduced by `Però`, therefore: while Rome was not corrupted, the prorogations of commands and the continuation of the consulship, used many times in difficult times, were "a useful and holy thing"; once the city was corrupted, civil battles and the seeds of tyranny rose even without them. A second conclusion, introduced by `E però` and impersonal, closes the reply: one can conclude that, had there not been the prorogations as well, neither Caesar nor the others who occupied the republic would have lacked the thought or the capacity to trouble it by another path. The reply does not deny that prorogation did harm; it denies that it was the principal foundation of the ills, and concludes that without it such men would not have lacked the thought or the means to trouble the republic another way (the editor's reading).

**History.** Caesar held his Gallic command from 58 BC; it was extended for five years by the agency of Pompey and Crassus, consuls in 55 BC (Suetonius, *Divus Iulius* 24; the editor's reference). Pompey, Crassus and Caesar made their compact in 60-59 BC (Suetonius 19). Catiline's conspiracy is of 63 BC, Catiline holding no magistracy; Sulla's first march on Rome, against Marius and the tribune Sulpicius, is of 88 BC, Sulla then consul (the editor's references, against standard chronology). Canestrini prints `decemvirale`, decemviral, where the 1933 edition prints `decennale`, decennial; the ten years of Caesar's command support the 1933's reading (the editor's reading), and the edition keeps Canestrini's provisionally (O50).

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's two causes, the Agrarian contentions and prolongation, and his diagnosis "if one considers well" [[NM-DISC.3.24.01]]; the harm without tumult, which Guicciardini's opening may be read as taking up (the editor's reading) [[NM-DISC.3.24.02]]; the "public utility" of the first prorogation, which "not the utility of the republic" may be read as answering (the editor's reading) [[NM-DISC.3.24.04]]; Sulla and Marius, and Caesar, "through this" [[NM-DISC.3.24.05]]; and the closing counterfactual, which Guicciardini turns: Machiavelli's servitude would have come later without prolongation; Guicciardini's republic, as one can conclude, would not have lacked men with the thought and the capacity to trouble it by another path [[NM-DISC.3.24.06]]. The reply names corruption, a word that does not occur in Machiavelli's chapter. This is the last of the *Considerazioni*; nothing in the reply marks it as an end (the editor's note).
