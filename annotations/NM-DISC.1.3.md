## NM-DISC.1.3.01

**Argument.** The founder's premise: whoever orders a republic must presuppose all men bad, ready to use their malignity whenever they are free to. Malignity that does not show is not absent; it waits on a cause not yet seen, and time uncovers it. The claim is about what a lawgiver must assume, and the rest of the chapter proves it on a case.

## NM-DISC.1.3.02

**Argument.** The case. After the kings were driven out, plebs and senate seemed united and the nobles seemed tamed. It was a deceit, kept up only while the Tarquins lived and the nobles feared the plebs would go over to them. Once the Tarquins were dead the nobles turned on the plebs. That proves the premise, and also the claim from I.1: men do good only by necessity, and where choice abounds everything fills with disorder (I.1).

**History.** In the tradition Tarquinius Superbus was expelled in 509 BC and died in exile at Cumae in 495 BC. Livy (2.21) puts the nobles' turn on the plebs in the year of his death.

## NM-DISC.1.3.03

**Argument.** From the case to the rule: hunger and poverty make men industrious, the laws make them good; where good usage holds a law is not needed, where it fails a law is. The Tarquins had done a law's work by making the nobles afraid. With them gone, Rome needed an order that would do the same, and after years of strife it found the tribunes. Machiavelli puts them between plebs and senate; Guicciardini's reply denies them that place [[FG-CONS.1.3.02]].

**History.** The first tribunes of the plebs were created after the plebs' secession to the Sacred Mount, in the tradition 494 BC (Livy 2.32–33). The chapter's title promises the accidents that made them; the next chapter argues that the disunion they institutionalised made Rome free and powerful.
