## FG-CONS.2.proem.01

**Argument.** Guicciardini grants the conclusion and refuses one claim. The conclusion, that the ancient times are often praised more than is due, is most true, and the Writer's reasons for it are well considered; he could add others and passes over them. He does not accord with the claim that there was always as much good in one age as in another, the places varying; he leaves out Machiavelli's wicked and does not deny that the good moves among places, only that its sum is constant across ages. By the influence of the heavens or by some other occult disposition, there run at times certain ages in which, not only in one province but universally in all the world, there is more *virtù* or more vice than in another age, or at least an art or a discipline flourishes more than it has in any part of the world at another time. The evidence is put as questions nobody could answer otherwise: painting and sculpture excellent under the Greeks and Romans, then obscure in all the world and buried for many centuries, and returned to light within the last one hundred and fifty or two hundred years; military discipline flourishing in antiquity, of which his own and his fathers' times have seen in any part of the world only small and obscure vestiges. The same, it can be said, of letters and of religion. Ages of war and of peace, and the variations of arts, religion and the movements of human things, make it no marvel that the customs of men vary too, which often take their motion from institution, occasions and necessity. His close weighs both sides: the ancient times are not always to be preferred, but it is not true to deny that one age is sometimes more corrupt or more virtuous than the others.

**History.** The editor puts the *Considerazioni* about 1530; counted from then, `da cento cinquanta o dugento anni in qua` sets the revival of painting and sculpture in the fourteenth century, and the longer figure reaches the age the Florentine writers associated with Giotto. Both the date and the identification are the editor's. The two causes he gives for the ages, the heavens' influence and an occult disposition, are kept as alternatives; he does not choose between them.

**Cross-reference.** The conclusion he grants is Machiavelli's opening, `ma non sempre ragionevolmente` [[NM-DISC.2.proem.01]], with the reasons of [[NM-DISC.2.proem.02]] and [[NM-DISC.2.proem.06]]. The claim he answers is [[NM-DISC.2.proem.04]], in his own restatement. The arts that Machiavelli set aside as clear in themselves [[NM-DISC.2.proem.02]] are Guicciardini's first proof that ages differ; `le cose umane` and `moto` are Machiavelli's words from [[NM-DISC.2.proem.03]], and the last clause, `ma non è già vero`, answers his `ma non essere già sempre vero` there. He does not take up Machiavelli's use of the argument for Italy and Greece [[NM-DISC.2.proem.05]], though military discipline and religion, two of his proofs, are two of the three things Machiavelli says Italy lacks; nor does he take up Machiavelli's own justification for praising Rome [[NM-DISC.2.proem.07]]. On the editor's reading his conclusion supports that justification on another ground.
