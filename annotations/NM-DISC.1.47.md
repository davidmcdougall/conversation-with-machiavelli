## NM-DISC.1.47.01

**Argument.** The case comes before the claim. The people, having taken the consular name in dislike, wanted plebeian men to be able to be made consuls, or the consular authority limited; the nobility, to debase that authority by neither, took a middle path, tribunes with consular power who could be plebeians as well as nobles, and the plebs was content, it seeming to it that it had extinguished the consulate and held its part in the supreme degree. Then the notable case: when it came to the creation of these tribunes, and all could have been created plebeians, the people created all nobles. Livy's sentence is given as the verdict, that spirits are one thing in the contest for liberty and honour and another in uncorrupted judgment once the contests are laid down.

**History.** The Latin Library text of Livy 4.6 (consulted 2026-10-22) has the sentence Machiavelli quotes, with `dignitatis` where he has `honoris`, and without the `in` that the 1824 prints before `incorrupto` (the 1554 omits it too). It continues that the people created all the tribunes patricians, content that account had been taken of the plebeians; Guicciardini quotes that clause [[FG-CONS.1.47.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.47.02

**Argument.** The claim is a belief (`credo proceda`) and a comparison: men deceive themselves much in general things, not so much in particular ones. The rubric's absolute is not repeated here. It seemed to the plebs, judging generally, that it merited the consulate, for its larger part in the city, its greater danger in the wars, its arms that kept Rome free and powerful; and this desire seemed to it reasonable. When it was to judge its own men particularly, it knew their weakness, judged that none of them merited what all together it seemed to merit, and, ashamed of them, had recourse to those who did. Livy's marvel at the modesty of the whole people, which one would not now find in one man, closes the case. No one in this telling makes the plebs judge particularly; the Roman case is credited to the senate only in .06.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini restates the claim as `più facilmente ... che`, a comparison, and offers, as something one can also consider, a further reading of the Roman case: the plebs had fought only to remove the infamy of general exclusion, and, partly vented, abstained from choosing those not fit [[FG-CONS.1.47.01]]. Machiavelli's reason, the knowledge of particular men, is not denied there; another is set beside it.

## NM-DISC.1.47.03

**Argument.** Capua is offered as corroboration, and here the descent to particulars is arranged. After Cannae, with Italy stirred up, Capua was near tumult from the hatred between people and senate. Pacuvius, in the supreme magistracy, designed to reconcile them (`con suo grado`, of his own will or by his office's standing; the translation takes the first, D372): he told the senators of their danger, of being killed and of the city given to Hannibal, and proposed to shut them in the palace and put them in the people's power in order to save them.

**History.** The Latin Library text of Livy 23.2-3 (consulted 2026-10-22) tells the story of Pacuvius Calavius: the senate shut in the curia, the people called on to name a replacement for each senator condemned, and the names shouted down.

## NM-DISC.1.47.04

**Argument.** The people is called to assembly and told the time has come to avenge itself; but since Pacuvius believed it would not want the city without government, new senators would have to be created if it wished to kill the old. The names are drawn; the first meets an uproar, and at Pacuvius' request for a replacement the assembly falls silent; the plebeians named are hissed, laughed at and abused, and all are judged unworthy. Pacuvius takes the occasion: since they judge the city fares ill without a senate and do not agree on replacements, let them be reconciled, for fear will have humbled the senators, and the humanity sought elsewhere will be found in them. The deceit is discovered when the people is constrained to come to particulars. `scoprire` returns in .05, where the popular men threaten to discover the citizens' deceit and it is their own that is discovered; the translation keeps "discover" for both.

## NM-DISC.1.47.05

**Argument.** A second kind of deceit: peoples judge things and their accidents generally, and become aware of the deceit when they know them particularly. Popular men, seeing the ruin of the city and understanding no other cause, blamed the ambition of some powerful man and threatened, if they ever became Signori, to discover and punish the citizens' deceit. Often, when one of them rose to the supreme magistracy and saw things from nearer, he knew the disorders, their sources, the dangers and the difficulty of the remedy; and seeing that the times and not the men caused the disorder, he became of another spirit, because the knowledge of particular things took away the deceit he had presupposed in considering generally. That change is Machiavelli's own statement. What belongs to those who had heard him before is the cause they gave it, that he had been got round and corrupted by the great; the proverb of one spirit in the piazza and another in the palace arose among them. Their belief is itself a judgment of a particular man, and a false one, an editorial observation: the case supports the first claim, about things, not the conclusion about distributions.

**History.** The 1824 dates the case `Dopo il 1514`, the 1554 `MCCCCXCIIII`, 1494 (D364). Machiavelli names no one: his `Principi` driven from Florence are not identified in the text. A reference account (English Wikipedia, "Piero di Lorenzo de' Medici", consulted 2026-10-22) gives 1494 for the Medici's flight from Florence and 1512 for their return. The edition keeps its governing witness and records the disagreement.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini keeps the Florentine example and restates its point: the Florentines lacked in the piazzas the notice and the advices they later saw in the palace [[FG-CONS.1.47.01]]. Where Machiavelli has the knowledge of particular things, Guicciardini has the notice a magistracy holds; he too speaks of knowing the particulars.

## NM-DISC.1.47.06

**Argument.** Two conclusions. The first is a means: peoples' eyes can be opened quickly by finding a way to make them descend to particulars when a general thing deceives them, as Pacuvius did at Capua and the senate at Rome. The second is a belief that one can conclude: a prudent man must never flee popular judgment in particular things, concerning the distributions of degrees and dignities, because only in this the people is not deceived, and if it is at some time, it will be so rare that the few who were to make such distributions will be deceived more times. The conclusion is limited twice, to particular things and to distributions; the few are hypothetical (`avessero a fare`), and the comparison is a prediction (`s'inganneranno`). The chapter then promises the order the senate held to undeceive the people in its distributions, the one matter in which it has just been said not to be deceived; the tension is the editor's observation, and the next chapter is not part of this pair.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini believes the people deceives itself less in distributions than in other things, because it judges a citizen by long universal opinion, and does not accept that in this it is not deceived, or is deceived at rarer times than the few; it governs itself by universal opinions, and as to ignorance it is much more dangerous than the judgment of few [[FG-CONS.1.47.02]].
