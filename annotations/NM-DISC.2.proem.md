## NM-DISC.2.proem.01

**Argument.** The proem to Book II opens on a habit and a doubt about it. Men always praise the ancient times and accuse the present, not always reasonably, and they are partisans of the past both where they know it from writers and where they remember it from their own youth. Machiavelli does not say the opinion is always false: when it is false, as most times it is, he persuades himself that the causes of the deceit are various. The first is given in .02, and further causes follow there and in .06; the qualification `non sempre` governs everything until he turns the case in .03.

## NM-DISC.2.proem.02

**Argument.** The causes of the deceit about ancient things. First, the truth of the past is not wholly understood: most times what would shame those times is hidden and what brings them glory is magnified, because most writers obey the fortune of the victors and make even the enemies' deeds illustrious, so that whoever is born afterwards on either side has cause to marvel at those men and is forced to praise and love them. Further, men hate from fear or envy, and past things can neither offend nor be envied, so two most powerful causes of hatred are extinguished in them; present things, known whole, with their faults, are judged much inferior even where they might merit more glory. The argument is limited at once: it concerns the life and customs of men, not the arts, whose clearness is such that the times can give or take little glory from them.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini calls these reasons well considered and passes over the others he could add [[FG-CONS.2.proem.01]]; he then takes the arts that Machiavelli sets aside here as his first evidence that ages differ.

## NM-DISC.2.proem.03

**Argument.** The habit is real, but erring in it is not always so. It is sometimes necessary that men judge the truth, because human things are always in motion, going up or going down. A city or province ordered to public living by an excellent man may go for a time in augmentation under his *virtù*: whoever is born there at that time and praises the past deceives himself, for the causes just given. Whoever is born in that city when its time has come to go down towards the worse does not, at that time, deceive himself. The judgment of present against past now depends on where in its course a city stands.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's last words take up this paragraph's `non essere già sempre vero` and turn them against the next one [[FG-CONS.2.proem.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.proem.04

**Argument.** The general claim behind the turn, given as Machiavelli's judgment (`giudico`). The world has always been in one same way, with as much good as wicked in it; what varies is where the good and the wicked are. *Virtù* was placed in Assyria, then Media, then Persia, and came into Italy and to Rome; after the Roman empire no lasting empire held it together, but it is scattered among many nations where men lived virtuously, the Franks, the Turks, the Sultan, the peoples of Germany, and earlier the Saracen sect. In those provinces and sects, since Rome fell, there has been *virtù*, and some of it remains. The claim is about the world as a whole; it is the one Guicciardini answers.

**History.** The succession of Assyria, Media and Persia before Rome recalls the old scheme of the world empires, carried from antiquity through the medieval chroniclers; Machiavelli names no source, and the comparison is the editor's. On the editor's identifications, the kingdom of the Franks is presumably Charlemagne's; the Turks are the Ottomans; the Sultan is presumably the Mamluk sultan of Egypt and Syria, whose state the Ottomans ended in 1517; the Saracen sect is the religion of the Arabs and those who carried it, from the conquests of the seventh century.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini restates this paragraph as `tanto del buono in una età quanto in una altra`, leaving out the wicked, and does not accord with it [[FG-CONS.2.proem.01]]. On the editor's reading, Machiavelli's world keeps its good and wicked while their seat moves; Guicciardini's answer is that certain ages at times have more *virtù* or more vice universally, in all the world, or at least flourish more in an art or a discipline.

## NM-DISC.2.proem.05

**Argument.** The consequence for the reader. One born in the provinces where *virtù* has been, and still is in some part, might deceive himself in praising the past; one born in Italy or in Greece, who has not become ultramontane in Italy or Turk in Greece, has reason to blame his own times, because nothing redeems them: no observance of religion, not of laws, not of soldiery, and every kind of filth, the more detestable in those who sit in judgment, command each one and wish to be adored. On the editor's reading, Machiavelli's own praise of Rome is here placed among the cases where the habit is justified, as .07 then claims.

**History.** The editor takes `coloro che seggono pro tribunali, comandano a ciascuno, e vogliono essere adorati` to point at the rulers of Italy, perhaps including the prelates; the text names no one.

## NM-DISC.2.proem.06

**Argument.** Further causes, from men themselves; the count is the editor's. If judgment errs about antiquity it should not err in old men judging their own youth and age, which they have known and seen alike; but men vary. Their force fails and their judgment and prudence grow, so that what seemed supportable and good in youth turns out insupportable and bad in age, and they accuse the times instead of their judgment. And human appetites are insatiable: nature gives the power and the wish to desire everything, fortune the means to attain few, so a continual ill contentment makes men blame the present, praise the past and desire the future, even though no reasonable cause moves them to do it.

## NM-DISC.2.proem.07

**Argument.** Machiavelli applies the doubt to himself and resolves it on a condition. He does not know whether he will be numbered among the deceived if he praises the times of the Romans too much and blames his own; but if the *virtù* of then and the vice of now were not clearer than the sun he would speak more held back, and since they are manifest he will speak openly, that the young may flee the one and prepare themselves to imitate the other whenever fortune gives them occasion. It is the office of a good man to teach the good he could not work, through the malignity of the times and of fortune, so that someone more loved by Heaven may work it. Book I spoke of Rome's deliberations pertaining to the inside of the city; Book II will speak of those pertaining to the augmentation of its empire.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini concludes that the ancient times are not always to be preferred, which Machiavelli has said in .01 and .03, and that it is not true to deny that an age is sometimes more corrupt or more virtuous, which, on the editor's reading, is the ground Machiavelli's praise of Rome needs [[FG-CONS.2.proem.01]]. On that reading the two agree on Machiavelli's conclusion for Italy and differ on its ground: for Machiavelli the good has moved elsewhere in a world that keeps it; for Guicciardini an age can be worse everywhere.
