## FG-CONS.1.58.01

**Argument.** Guicciardini says without doubt that it is a difficult enterprise, and very alien from the opinion of men, to attribute constancy and prudence to the people and to set it before princes in them. No writer on political things, he says, ever doubted that the government of one, when princes are regulated by the laws, is better than a multitude's even regulated, to which the government of the optimates is also preferred. The reasons are his own: in a lesser number *virtù* is more united, with more order, thought and resolution; where there is a multitude there is confusion, and in so much dissonance there can be no reasonable discourse. Men are lightly moved by every vain suspicion and uproar and return to what they condemned; therefore the multitude is likened to the waves of the sea. A people by itself is an ark of ignorance and confusion; therefore merely popular governments have been little durable and have brought forth tyranny or ruin. The two `però` are consecutive, and `per sé medesimo`, a people by itself, prepares the distinction of the next paragraph.

**Cross-reference.** The editor's reading: the reply's first words answer Machiavelli's doubt about a `provincia dura` [[NM-DISC.1.58.01]] with `sanza dubio`, and `molto aliena dalla opinione degli uomini` takes up Machiavelli's `contro alla comune opinione` [[NM-DISC.1.58.05]]; the two passages share the sense, not the words. `non sanza cagione è assomigliata la moltitudine alle onde del mare` answers Machiavelli's `non senza cagione si assomiglia la voce d'un Popolo a quella di Dio` [[NM-DISC.1.58.07]] in nearly his words: the same ground, the same verb, a different likeness.

## FG-CONS.1.58.02

**Argument.** The examples are so many that there is no occasion to repeat them, and they have deservedly brought forth the writers' opinion. Guicciardini denies only that Machiavelli's reasons and examples are repugnant to it, for one who considers well. His instrument is a distinction: it is one thing to consider a multitude that deliberates by itself, another a popular government ordered so that the grave and important deliberations are to be made by the more prudent. In the first case there will often be variety, ignorance and confusion, however much the multitude is regulated by the laws; in the second, if things are deliberated prudently and stably, it is not because the defects are absent from the multitude but because they are absent from the more prudent. Rome is the second case: the senate, consuls and principal magistrates deliberated the most important things; had the multitude had to deliberate, even under good laws and with customs most careful of liberty, there would have appeared many times, with most grave damage to the republic, the imprudence and variety the writers reprove in other multitudes. The counterfactual stays contrary to fact.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's claim was that a people that commands and is well ordered is stable, prudent and grateful no otherwise than a prince, or better [[NM-DISC.1.58.05]]. Machiavelli names magistrates in the people's choices of them and in Rome's orders [[NM-DISC.1.58.08]], but does not ask who deliberated; Guicciardini's reply turns on that question.

## FG-CONS.1.58.03

**Argument.** Three comparisons follow, each under its own act. First, on even terms: even if the Romans' deliberations are called the multitude's, take a prince who stands among princes in the degree of *virtù* in which the Roman people stood among peoples; he believes without doubt that such a prince would proceed with greater prudence and constancy, because where the terms are even there is more order, distinction, resolution and firmness in one than in many. Second, unbound against unbound, as almost all princes are, the French kings among them: he grants that in a prince there may perhaps be found more other vices and more promptness to execute them, but says that in discoursing of these the Author departs from the terms of his question; commonly a prince would have more prudence and constancy, which is precisely the Author's title. Although some princes are found most imprudent, and their imprudence, when it is of that last species, is perhaps more pernicious than the multitude's, two hundred years of a kingdom would give prudent and imprudent kings, and two hundred years of a multitude a continuation of imprudence and variety. Third, augmentation: the argument is impertinent, since it arises from other causes, and he does not doubt that fifty years of a prince equally good would bring more than fifty of a good popular government. He then concedes that popular augmentation goes on more continuously, because successors are not always alike, and closes on a compatibility, not a refutation: it can very well stand together that popular government is a city's better fortune, a consideration outside the dispute, and that there is ordinarily more imprudence and inconstancy in a people than in a prince.

**Cross-reference.** `piglisi al rincontro uno principe` reverses Machiavelli's `all'incontro loro si debbe porre una moltitudine` [[NM-DISC.1.58.02]]: Machiavelli set a regulated multitude against the kings held by laws, Guicciardini sets a prince against the Roman people. The French kings, whom Machiavelli placed among the princes held by laws (`moderato più dalle leggi`), Guicciardini places among the unbound, and gives Machiavelli's claim as `legati`, bound; Machiavelli's words are `moderato` and, at .10, `obbligato alle leggi` [[NM-DISC.1.58.10]]. `perché nasce da altre cagioni` contradicts Machiavelli's `Il che non può nascere da altro` [[NM-DISC.1.58.08]] in the same verb. `il titulo dello Autore` is Machiavelli's rubric, which calls the multitude wiser and more constant; Guicciardini reads its wisdom as prudence and holds the dispute to prudence and constancy, setting the other vices and augmentation outside it.
