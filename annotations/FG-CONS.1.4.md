## FG-CONS.1.4.01

**Argument.** He moves the cause back a step. Machiavelli found liberty in the disunion [[NM-DISC.1.4.02]]; Guicciardini asks where the disunion came from, and finds it in a founding choice: the city was divided into patricians and plebeians and the plebs shut out of every magistracy. Had there been no such distinction, or had half the honours been given to the plebs from the start, as was done later, the divisions would not have arisen. They were damaging — though in a less virtuous republic they would have done more harm — and without them the plebs would not have wanted tribunes, and liberty would have run no more danger from one order than the other. Once the honours were shared, the tribunate was perhaps more harmful than useful, and at least in the latter times the instrument and pretext of those who would disturb the republic; its two great powers, proposing laws and the veto, he cannot praise.

**History.** Half the honours "as was done afterwards": the Licinian-Sextian laws of 367 BC opened the consulship to plebeians, and other magistracies followed. The fuller treatment he says he wrote "at another time" is his earlier political writing; the translator's note names the *Dialogo del reggimento di Firenze*.

**Cross-reference.** In I.3 he had already denied the tribunes the place Machiavelli gave them as intermediaries between plebs and senate.

## FG-CONS.1.4.02

**Argument.** The denial is explicit, and it turns the chapter's title back on it: it was not the disunion that made Rome free and powerful [[NM-DISC.1.4.02]]. He concedes what can be conceded — Rome's seditions did less damage than other cities', and the patricians did well to yield rather than find a way to do without the plebs — and then gives the reply its image: to praise the disunions is to praise a sick man's sickness for the goodness of the remedy. The disorder was there from Rome's beginning, but the kings held it harmless; it broke out when they were expelled, and no one noticed it then: men were thinking of the present ill, and those without great skill in public things learn them only by experience. So a republic has seldom, or perhaps never, had its ordering perfect from the beginning. The remedy was useful; failing to remove the causes was not.

**History.** Servius Tullius, sixth king, was remembered as the plebs' friend and the author of the census classes. Under the kings, plebeian families were at times enrolled among the patricians. After the expulsion, in the tradition 504 BC, the Sabine Attus Clausus — Appius Claudius — was received into the patriciate with his clients (Livy 2.16): Guicciardini's instance of a stranger preferred over the plebs' own leaders.

## FG-CONS.1.4.03

**Argument.** He will not discourse now on the rest of Rome's orders, using Machiavelli's verb from the chapter's first line [[NM-DISC.1.4.01]], and says only that he does not believe they were fit for a founder to copy. Then the concession that closes the reply: Rome's military discipline was excellent, and its *virtù* sustained all the government's other defects. That is the military half of the opinion Machiavelli set out to answer; of fortune Guicciardini says nothing. He adds the condition under which it holds: defects of government matter less in a city that governs itself by arms than in cities that live by industry, whirligigs and the arts of peace — which, from a Florentine, may be read as a glance at Florence; the text does not name it.
