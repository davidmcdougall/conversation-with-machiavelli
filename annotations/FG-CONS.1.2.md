## FG-CONS.1.2.01

**Argument.** He grants at once what Machiavelli argued at length, that the mixed government is better and more stable than any simple one [[NM-DISC.1.2.07]]. His interest lies in the qualification: it is better still when each species gives its good and leaves its bad behind, and that is where a founder can go wrong. The rest of the reply is his account of how to do it.

**Argument.** The good of one man's rule is practical: order, speed, secrecy, decision. Its ill is that power given for good works ill in bad hands, and in weak hands breeds disorder; election does not remove the danger entirely, because electors misjudge, power changes men, and a king with sons will want them to succeed him. Machiavelli had traced the same corruption through inheritance [[NM-DISC.1.2.05]].

## FG-CONS.1.2.02

**Argument.** A head for the mixed government, with its authority cut down to what one man does well: watching, receiving business, putting it forward. He gives up one man's being able to deliberate and execute alone, as the price of safety from tyranny. The head is to be elected, better for life than for a term, and if for a term, a long one.

**Argument.** Venice is his model, against Sparta and Rome. Sparta's kings were hereditary; Rome's kings had so much authority they could become tyrants. Machiavelli had said the consuls kept the kings' power under a new name [[NM-DISC.1.2.08]]; Guicciardini grants the name only conditionally, and counts their annual term against Rome: the doge is elected for life and tightly limited.

**History.** The doge of Venice was elected for life. Canestrini's note: he could undertake nothing without the Great Council, the Senate (*Pregadi*) and the *Collegio*. Servius Tullius was the sixth king of Rome, Tarquinius Superbus the seventh and last, expelled in the tradition in 509 BC.

## FG-CONS.1.2.03

**Argument.** The optimates, being several, cannot make a tyranny as easily as one man; being the most qualified, they govern with more prudence than a multitude; being honoured, they have less cause to trouble the city. Their ill is to favour themselves against the people and to fall out among themselves; and if their rank is hereditary, the good are soon succeeded by the bad. The last ill is the one Machiavelli gave for the optimates' turn into a government of few: inheritance [[NM-DISC.1.2.05]].

## FG-CONS.1.2.04

**Argument.** The senate gets the longest treatment. Its members are to be drawn from everyone eligible for office, not from set families, many in number and for life, so that others hope to enter it and bear it. It is to deliberate war, peace and foreign affairs, but not to create magistrates, above all those with criminal jurisdiction or charge of the revenues, nor to make law without the people. Machiavelli credits Rome's perfection to the disunion of plebs and senate [[NM-DISC.1.2.09]]; Guicciardini traces all its seditions to optimates drawn from the patricians alone.

**History.** Guicciardini had proposed a similar frame for Florence in the *Discorso di Logrogno*, written in Spain in August 1512: a gonfaloniere for life, as Florence already had in Soderini; the Great Council to elect the magistrates; and a senate of the ablest citizens to prepare laws and handle the weightiest business.

**History.** Sparta's council of elders, the gerousia, was twenty-eight men over sixty, elected for life from the full citizens; Aristotle (*Politics* 2.9, 5.6) thought its election narrow. Guicciardini's "whole body of the city" is generous to it.

## FG-CONS.1.2.05

**Argument.** The people's government keeps out tyranny and puts the law above men; its ill is ignorance, instability, and readiness to strike at the qualified citizens. So the people keeps only what liberty needs: the choice of magistrates and the final confirmation of laws that the magistrates and senate have first digested. Free harangues are forbidden; in the council only those commissioned may speak. With that, he says, one will have the mixture Machiavelli spoke of.
