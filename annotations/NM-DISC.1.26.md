## NM-DISC.1.26.01

**Argument.** The case before the precept. The subject is any new prince of a city or a state who does not turn his rule, by kingdom or by republic, to civil life; weak foundations make the precept the more pressing, they do not define the case. That is the prince I.25 set aside at its close: whoever would order an absolute power by the path of a republic or a kingdom must keep the shadow of the ancient ways, whoever would make the absolute power the authors call tyranny must renew every thing. For this prince the best remedy for holding the principate is to make every thing anew in the state. The instances follow in a run of infinitives: new governments with new names, new authority, new men; making the poor rich.

**History.** David, the shepherd made king (1 Samuel 16 to 2 Samuel 5), is joined to a verse of the Magnificat, Mary's song in Luke 1.53, which Machiavelli makes a relative clause on David by adding `qui`. The verse's reversal of hungry and rich is the programme in small: those raised by the new prince owe him everything.

## NM-DISC.1.26.02

**Argument.** The list goes on from offices to the land and the people: build new cities, unmake some of the old, move the inhabitants, leave nothing intact, and, coordinated with these, that there be no degree, order, standing or wealth which its holder does not acknowledge as from the prince. Here the sentence turns to "you", as if the precept were spoken to the prince, and back to the third person for the model, Philip of Macedon, who with these ways rose from a small king to prince of Greece; of whom, Machiavelli reports, one who writes of him says that he used to move men from province to province as herdsmen move their herds. `questi modi`, "these ways", enters here, and the verdict will be pronounced on it.

**History.** Philip II (r. 359-336 BC) won the hegemony of Greece at Chaeronea in 338 BC. Machiavelli does not name the writer; Justin's epitome of Pompeius Trogus (8.5-6) is the likeliest source, where Philip transfers peoples and cities as shepherds drive their flocks now to winter, now to summer pastures. The 1554 edition has the perfect, "transferred", where the 1824 has the imperfect of habit.

## NM-DISC.1.26.03

**Argument.** The verdict falls on the same `modi` named with Philip: these ways are most cruel, enemies of every way of living, not only Christian but human, and any man (the chapter's first word again) must flee them and wish rather to live a private man than be king with such ruin of men. That condemnation is unreserved. Then `Nondimeno`: whoever will not take that first path of the good, when he wishes to maintain himself, it is fitting that he enter this ill. The necessity is conditional on two acts of will, refusing the good and wishing to maintain oneself, and the verdict is not withdrawn by it. The middle paths that men take are called most damaging, because men do not know how to be wholly good or wholly bad.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini divides the case by the people the prince takes over, not by the prince [[FG-CONS.1.26.01]]. For those inclined to liberty he grants strong remedies but would have the prince gain by benefits whatever part can be gained; whether that mixture is one of the middle paths condemned here is the reader's question, not one either author poses. Guicciardini closes on Machiavelli's taste for extraordinary remedies; set beside this paragraph's verdict on the same ways, the edition leaves the two to stand together.

**Text.** The last clause promises an example in "the present chapter". The example, Giovampagolo Baglioni, who had Julius II in his power at Perugia and did not dare to use it, is the matter of I.27, whose rubric takes up the clause (`Sanno rarissime volte gli uomini essere al tutto tristi, o al tutto buoni`); I.27 dates the pope's expedition 1505, where the histories give 1506. The 1554 edition reads `seguente`, "following", which fits; the 1824 `presente` is kept as printed (D282).
