## FG-CONS.2.24.01

**Argument.** The reply opens on method before it reaches fortresses: antiquity must not be praised so much that every modern order the Romans did not use is blamed, because experience has uncovered things the ancients did not consider, and, the foundations being different, what suits one condition did not suit the other. Therefore the Romans' not building fortresses in subject cities does not make whoever builds them now in error. Many cases make them very useful: to a prince or tyrant with his own citizens, to a lord with his subjects, to a potentate with strangers.

Guicciardini marvels that the opinion has contradictors, and gives his reasons. If subjects always answered to the rule over them, loving a prince who treats them well, he confesses that, as to them, fortresses would be useless to a prince who governed well, since the love of the peoples would be enough. That condition he does not grant: peoples, even well treated, are often little reasonable and eager for new things; the memory of an old prince, or the appetite for a liberty they are used to, works in them; and a prince or tyrant is often forced to govern with some injury. So, whether or not he can have the peoples as friends, the ruler must make some foundation upon force, on holding his peoples in some terror, or be too often a prey to their levity, malignity and just hatred.

He then calls very frivolous the reason adduced in the Discourse, that fortresses give princes spirit to be insolent and to make ill carriages. By that reasoning a prince should keep no guard, no arms, no armies, so as to have to seek the peoples' favour the more, the more exposed he was to their discretion; and things useful in themselves must not be fled though the security they give may make a man bad, as medicine is not blamed because men, trusting it, guard less against what makes them ill. Nor is this a reason to refuse the good, when the ill that may follow is in the ruler's power to let follow or not.

**Cross-reference.** The opening answers the chapter's first paragraph and its Roman authority [[NM-DISC.2.24.01]] [[NM-DISC.2.24.10]]. The three uses answer Machiavelli's three cases, enemies and, once .04 divides it, subjects in one's own city and in a city occupied (the editor's reading) [[NM-DISC.2.24.02]] [[NM-DISC.2.24.04]]; the third, strangers, he names and does not argue. "Well treated" answers the "ill treatments", and the "ill carriages" and the spirit to do ill are Machiavelli's phrases taken up to be refused [[NM-DISC.2.24.03]]. The "some foundation upon force" stands, in the editor's reading, against the prince who founds himself on the benevolence of men [[NM-DISC.2.24.04]]; the "just hatred" grants the hatred Machiavelli derives from ill government, and he grants that the fortress's security may give spirit to be bad, while denying that this is a reason to refuse it (the editor's reading).

## FG-CONS.2.24.02

**Argument.** "According to the order of the Discourse": to a tyrant of a city, or to any prince, fortresses in that city are most useful. Neither people nor private enemies can rise on every light occasion while the prince sits secure in his fortress: it is hard to kill him with all his line, and hard to have forces ready to shut in or take the fortress before he can retake the town with men brought in through it. The same holds of a subject city, which cannot think of rebellion unless it sees a foreign army hostile to the prince in the province.

The examples of Milan and the rest are not good, because those states were lost not by the rebellion of peoples alone but through powerful war; and it could be said that without the fortresses they would have been lost, perhaps much earlier, even in peace. If a fortress does not always recover a lost town, it sometimes has, as at Brescia, where Foix, with a powerful army, could not have recovered the town had he not been let in through the fortress; and where it does not, the fear of it keeps the enemy engaged until he takes it, an interval that may greatly benefit the one assailed.

**History.** Brescia, February 1512: Gaston de Foix entered through the castle, which the French still held, and retook the town (the editor's summary; Canestrini's note on the passage is not carried).

**Cross-reference.** The order followed is Machiavelli's own city and city occupied [[NM-DISC.2.24.04]] [[NM-DISC.2.24.09]]; the "rein of the fortress" is his figure [[NM-DISC.2.24.01]]. Milan and "the others" are the examples of .05-.07 [[NM-DISC.2.24.05]] [[NM-DISC.2.24.07]]; the counterfactual reverses Machiavelli's, in which without the fortress the Sforza would have seen the danger sooner and resisted better (the editor's reading). Brescia is the objector's case, which Machiavelli answers as rare and dependent on a near army [[NM-DISC.2.24.11]] [[NM-DISC.2.24.12]]: there the fortress needed the army, here the army needed the fortress (the editor's reading); Guicciardini's "sometimes" accepts that it is not always, without granting that it is negligible (the editor's reading). The fortress holding the enemy for an interval meets, in the editor's reading, Machiavelli's coastal fortress that sustains the enemy some days [[NM-DISC.2.24.14]], which he called sometimes useful.

## FG-CONS.2.24.03

**Argument.** The Roman example. Setting aside Guidobaldo, Ottaviano and the others, whose authority does not outweigh that of the many who have built fortresses, Guicciardini gives two possible causes for the Romans' practice: in the beginnings of their empire they did not reduce cities to open servitude but held them under a shadow of liberty and equal confederation, which did not bear fortresses; and, always having ordered and very powerful armies, and colonies in many places, they judged they had less need of them, being accustomed rather to destroy cities they deemed most hostile. Yet had they judged fortresses useless, they would have destroyed that of Tarentum and the others they found built, since one built by others would be as useless as one's own.

The close is a confession with its limits. In many cases and times fortresses do not help, and other remedies are perhaps sometimes more useful and harder; but fortresses are often useful against conspiracies and rebellions and for recovering lost towns. Therefore our times employ them not without cause; the ancients used them, and the Romans did not unwall those they found.

**History.** Guidobaldo da Montefeltro razed his fortresses on returning to Urbino; Ottaviano Fregoso took and razed the Briglia at Genoa in 1514 (the editor's identifications; Canestrini's note is not carried).

**Cross-reference.** Guidobaldo and Ottaviano are Machiavelli's examples [[NM-DISC.2.24.07]] [[NM-DISC.2.24.08]]. "As the Author has said elsewhere" points to the Roman way with companions, in the editor's reading Discourses II.4 and II.13. The colonies and ordered armies answer Machiavelli's own reasons for Roman success; the destroying of cities recalls the alternative Machiavelli himself gives for Pisa, "make it a companion or unmake it" [[NM-DISC.2.24.09]]. Tarentum is turned against its use in the chapter [[NM-DISC.2.24.11]], and "did not unwall them" answers "unwalled and did not wall" [[NM-DISC.2.24.10]]. The confession grants Machiavelli's "do not help" only "in many cases and in many times" [[NM-DISC.2.24.05]], and the other remedies only as "perhaps sometimes" [[NM-DISC.2.24.14]].
