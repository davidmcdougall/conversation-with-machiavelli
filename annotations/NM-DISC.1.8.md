## NM-DISC.1.8.01

**Argument.** The story first. Camillus' *virtù* had made the citizens yield to him without feeling diminished; Manlius, who had saved the Capitol, could not bear it. Unable to sow discord among the Fathers, he turned to the plebs and spread the charge that the gold gathered to ransom Rome from the French had been taken by private citizens, and could be recovered to relieve the plebs of tributes and debts. He gathered a following and made tumults; the senate made a dictator; summoned and asked in public who held the treasure, Manlius would only say that they knew already, and the dictator put him in prison. He would not bring the charge to proof. That is what makes it, in .05, a calumny.

**History.** Livy 6.11–16, under 385 BC. The dictator was Aulus Cornelius Cossus, named ostensibly for a war with the Volsci, which he won, and turned on Manlius on his return; the gold was the ransom being weighed for the Gauls when Camillus arrived (5.48–49). Manlius was released (6.17), and in 384 was accused of aiming at kingship, condemned, and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (6.18–20).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini has named Manlius twice already: as one whom the consuls and dictators put down in defence of liberty (his reply to I.5), and as a favourite the people would not condemn until his design was out (his reply to I.7). Here he takes Machiavelli's example and turns it: the scandalous calumny is put down without the order Machiavelli asks for [[FG-CONS.1.8.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.8.02

**Argument.** The rule. Calumnies are detestable in any form of living, and no order that serves to repress them should be spared. The best is to open many places to accusation: an accusation needs witnesses and corroboration, a calumny needs nothing, so anyone can calumniate anyone, and calumny is used most where accusation is used least. The orderer of a republic should let any citizen be accused without fear, and then punish calumniators sharply; they have no grievance, since the courts were open to them. Where this part is not ordered, calumny irritates without chastising, and the irritated look to their own remedies, hating rather than fearing what is said against them.

**Cross-reference.** The courts are I.7's — "to the people, or to any magistracy or council" — now in the plural; the irritated who "avail themselves" are I.7's citizens who, without an ordinary outlet, turn to extraordinary ways. Guicciardini grants the conclusion in Machiavelli's own word, `detestabili` [[FG-CONS.1.8.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.8.03

**Argument.** The part was well ordered in Rome and has always been ill ordered in Florence, whose histories are full of calumnies against citizens in public charge: one robbed the commune, another lost an enterprise for a bribe, a third did this or that from ambition. From them came hatred, division, sects and ruin, the chain of I.7 shortened. An order of accusation would have prevented it, since every citizen, condemned or acquitted, would have been unable to harm the city, and far fewer would have been accused than were calumniated. And among the other means some citizen used to come to greatness were calumnies against the powerful who stood in his way, taking the people's side and confirming its ill opinion of them.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli does not name the citizen. Guicciardini does, and calls the example a dream [[FG-CONS.1.8.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.8.04

**Argument.** One example. The Florentine army failed before Lucca under the commissary Giovanni Guicciardini; whether by his bad management or bad fortune, he was blamed and said to have been bribed by the Lucchese, and his enemies kept the charge alive. He offered to put himself in the hands of the Captain, and could not clear himself, because the republic had no way of doing it. Indignation arose among his friends, most of the great men, and among those who wanted change, and that, with other such causes, grew until it ruined the republic.

**History.** The war against Lucca of 1429–1433, in which Florence failed to take the city, with Milanese help for Lucca. The accusations against Giovanni Guicciardini, commissary in 1430, are told in Machiavelli's *Istorie fiorentine* (book 4), among the quarrels of the years before the change of regime of 1433–1434. Giovanni was of Francesco Guicciardini's own family.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini grants the want of a way to clear himself, which is Machiavelli's diagnosis, and affirms the injustice, which Machiavelli left open ("however the case stood"); and he reverses the cause: the city's discords made the case, not the case the discords [[FG-CONS.1.8.01]].

## NM-DISC.1.8.05

**Argument.** The verdict on Manlius: a calumniator, not an accuser, and the Romans showed how calumniators are to be punished. The remedy turns them into accusers. If the charge is corroborated, reward or at least do not punish them; if not, punish them as Manlius was.

**Cross-reference.** The text shows only the prison of .01; in Livy the later death (6.20) was for aiming at kingship, and Machiavelli does not say which punishment he means. The chapter closes I.7's pairing of accusation and calumny (I.7 .07), and I.9 turns to the founder alone.
