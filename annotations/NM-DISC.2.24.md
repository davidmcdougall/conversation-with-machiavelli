## NM-DISC.2.24.01

**Argument.** The chapter opens on the objection it will answer: to the wise men of the present it will seem ill considered that the Romans, securing themselves of the Latins and of Privernum, built no fortress there as a rein to hold them in faith, the more so since a Florentine saying, which those wise men cite, holds that Pisa and like cities must be held with fortresses. Machiavelli's reply is that the Romans were of another *virtù*, judgment and power: while Rome lived free and kept its orders and virtuous constitutions it never built one to hold cities or provinces, though it saved some already built.

**History.** The settlement of the Latins (338 BC) and of Privernum (329 BC) is Livy 8.13-14 and 8.19-21; the chapter before, II.23, discusses both judgments (the editor's references). The Florentine saying appears in *The Prince*, chapter 20, with Pistoia held by parties beside Pisa held by fortresses (the editor's reference).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini opens on the same comparison, refusing to praise antiquity so much that every modern order the Romans did not use is blamed; the Romans' not building fortresses in the subject cities is not, for him, a reason to blame whoever builds them now [[FG-CONS.2.24.01]]. The Romans' saving "some of those built" is the ground of his last word (Tarentum and the other places where they found fortresses made and did not unwall them), in the editor's reading [[FG-CONS.2.24.03]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.02

**Argument.** The question is put: whether it is good to build fortresses, and whether they do damage or advantage to the builder. They are made either to defend oneself from enemies or from subjects; in the first case they are not necessary, in the second they are damaging. The rest of the chapter follows this division: the second case first (.03-.12), the first at the end (.13-.14).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's three uses of the fortress, to a prince or tyrant with his citizens, to a lord with his subjects, to a potentate with strangers, answer Machiavelli's three cases once .04 divides the second (the editor's reading) [[FG-CONS.2.24.01]]; his "order of the Discourse" follows Machiavelli's own city and city occupied, and he names the case of strangers without arguing it [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.03

**Argument.** Why fortresses against subjects are damaging. A prince or republic that fears its subjects must first be hated by them; the hatred comes from its ill carriages; these come either from believing it can hold the subjects by force or from little prudence in governing; and one of the things that makes it believe it can force them is having fortresses on their backs. So the ill treatment that causes the hatred arises in good part from the fortresses, which, when this is true, are far more harmful than useful. The condition, "when this is true", is Machiavelli's own.

Two reasons follow. First, the fortresses make the ruler more audacious and violent toward the subjects. Second, there is not the security within that he persuades himself of: of all the forces used to hold a people only two avail, a good army always ready for the field, as Rome had, or scattering and disjoining the people so that it cannot combine; for impoverished men keep arms (Juvenal), disarmed men find them in fury (Virgil), and heads cut off grow again like the Hydra's. Fortresses are useful in peace, because they give more spirit to do the subjects ill (in the editor's reading a use that is itself the harm), and most useless in war, when enemy and subjects assail them together; and in our times artillery makes small places, and places where one cannot withdraw with ramparts, impossible to defend.

**History.** The two Latin tags are Juvenal, *Satires* 8.124, and Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.150 (the editor's references). The earlier discussion of artillery is II.17 (the editor's reading of "as we discoursed above").

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini takes up two of these phrases: the "ill carriages", in the reason he calls very frivolous, and the spirit the fortress gives, which he answers with medicine, whose security may make men careless and is not on that account refused [[FG-CONS.2.24.01]]. Against the hatred that ill treatment causes he sets peoples who, "even well treated", are often unreasonable [[FG-CONS.2.24.01]]. The 1554 omits the negative in "there is not that security" and reads "useless" for "useful" in peace (variant notes).

## NM-DISC.2.24.04

**Argument.** Machiavelli disputes the matter more minutely, dividing the second case again: a prince who would rein in the people of his own city, or a prince or republic that would rein in a city occupied by war. To the prince first: a fortress to hold his citizens cannot be more useless, for the causes given; it makes him prompt and unrespectful in oppressing them, and the oppression exposes them to his ruin and kindles them, so that the fortress which caused it cannot later defend him. A wise and good prince, to keep himself good and give his sons no cause or daring to become wicked, will never build one, so that they found themselves not on a fortress but on the benevolence of men.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's "some foundation upon force", upon holding the peoples in some terror, answers this founding on benevolence word for word in the root, in the editor's reading [[FG-CONS.2.24.01]]. His "particulars according to the order of the Discourse" take up this first sub-case [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.05

**Argument.** Francesco Sforza, deemed wise, built a fortress in Milan; in this, Machiavelli says, he was not wise, and the effect showed it was to the damage of his heirs. Judging they could live secure by it and offend their citizens and subjects, they spared no kind of violence, became odious beyond measure, and lost the state as soon as the enemy attacked; the fortress neither defended them nor helped them in war, and in peace had done them much damage. Without it, had they through little prudence handled their citizens sourly, they would have seen the danger sooner, drawn back, and resisted the French more spiritedly with friendly subjects and no fortress than with hostile subjects and a fortress. Fortresses do not help in any part: they are lost through the fraud of those who guard them, the violence of those who assail them, or hunger.

**History.** Francesco Sforza, duke from 1450, rebuilt the castle of Porta Giovia; his son Lodovico lost Milan to Louis XII in 1499, and the castle was surrendered by its castellan (the editor's identifications and dates).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini denies that Milan's example is good: the states were lost not by rebellion of the peoples alone but by powerful war, and it could be said that without the fortresses they would have been lost, perhaps much earlier, even in peace [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]]. His counterfactual turns Machiavelli's (without the fortress, the danger seen sooner) the other way, in the editor's reading.

## NM-DISC.2.24.06

**Argument.** If a fortress is to help recover a lost state, where the fortress alone remains, an army is needed to assail the one who drove you out; with that army you would recover the state in any case, fortress or no, and the more readily as men would be more your friends, not having been ill-treated through the fortress's haughtiness. Experience shows that the fortress of Milan brought neither the Sforza nor the French any advantage in their adverse times, but to all much damage and ruin, since by it they thought of no more honest way of holding the state.

**History.** The castle passed to the French in 1499, surrendered by its castellan, back to the Sforza in 1512-13, and to Francis I in 1515 (the editor's summary).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini counts recovery among the fortress's uses and answers Machiavelli's point with a "not always" that his Brescia example turns against him: by *virtù* of the fortress the lost town is not always recovered, but it has sometimes been [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]]; and where it is not, the fear of the fortress holds the enemy until it is taken, an interval that can benefit the one assailed (the editor's reading of the answer).

## NM-DISC.2.24.07

**Argument.** Three modern examples: two rulers who unmade fortresses on their return, and a pope who built one and lost it. Guidobaldo of Urbino, returning to a state from which Cesare Borgia had driven him, had all the fortresses of the province ruined: loved by men, he did not want them in respect of them, and against enemies he saw he could not defend them without an army in the field. Julius II built a fortress in Bologna after driving out the Bentivoglio, and afterwards had the people despoiled, it being ill-treated by a governor of his; so it rebelled and he lost the fortress at once; carrying himself otherwise, it would have helped him. Niccolò Vitelli, returning from exile to his fatherland, at once unmade two fortresses Sixtus IV had built there, judging that the benevolence of the people, not the fortress, was to keep him in his state.

**History.** Guidobaldo da Montefeltro was driven out by Cesare Borgia in 1502; the "accident" that let him return is, in the editor's reading, Alexander VI's death in 1503, though he had come back briefly in October 1502. Julius II took Bologna in 1506 and built a fortress at the Porta Galliera; it was lost and razed when the Bentivoglio returned in 1511; the governor is usually taken to be the legate, Cardinal Alidosi (the editor's identification). Niccolò Vitelli returned to Città di Castello in 1482 (the editor's date). *The Prince*, chapter 20, gives the examples of Vitelli and Guidobaldo (the editor's reference).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini sets aside "the example of Duke Guido, of Ottaviano and of the others": their authority is not enough to confound that of so many who have built fortresses [[FG-CONS.2.24.03]]. "Assassinare" is taken in its old sense of despoiling, and the 1554 makes the governor the agent (variant note).

## NM-DISC.2.24.08

**Argument.** The freshest and most notable example, apt to show the inutility of building fortresses and the utility of unmaking them, is Genoa. After its rebellion of 1507 Louis XII came in person, recovered it, and built a fortress stronger than any known, set on a point of hill over the sea, the Codefa, commanding the port and much of the town. In 1512, the French driven from Italy, Genoa rebelled notwithstanding the fortress; Ottaviano Fregoso took the state, reduced the fortress by hunger in sixteen months, and, against the counsel of many that he keep it as a refuge, ruined it, knowing that the will of men, not fortresses, keeps princes in their states. He has held Genoa on his *virtù* and prudence: where a thousand foot used to change its state, ten thousand have not shaken him. So unmaking the fortress has not harmed Ottaviano, and making it did not defend the king, who could recover Genoa without a fortress when he could come into Italy with an army, and could not hold it with one when he could not. The paragraph closes on parallel antitheses: expense and shame for the king, glory and use for Ottaviano.

**History.** The fortress is the Briglia, begun by Louis XII on the Capo di Faro in 1507 and surrendered to Ottaviano Fregoso in 1514 (the editor's identification and dates). Ottaviano was doge of Genoa from 1513 and then governor for Francis I until 1522; "he has held it and holds it" places the writing within those years, and .14's "nearest times" of 1517 agrees (the editor's reading). Machiavelli's account passes over Giano Fregoso, doge in 1512-13 before Ottaviano (the editor's note).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's "Ottaviano" in the reply is this example, set aside with Guidobaldo's [[FG-CONS.2.24.03]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.09

**Argument.** The second sub-case: republics that build fortresses not in the fatherland but in the towns they acquire. If France and Genoa were not enough, Florence and Pisa suffice: the Florentines built fortresses to hold Pisa and did not see that a city always hostile to them, that had lived free and has liberty for refuge in rebellion, had to be held the Roman way, made a companion or unmade. The fortresses' *virtù* was seen when Charles VIII came: they were given up to him through the little faith of their guards or fear of worse; without them the Florentines would not have rested their hold on Pisa upon them, the king could not have taken it that way, and the ways that had kept Pisa until then would perhaps have sufficed.

**History.** Charles VIII entered Pisa in November 1494; the Florentine fortresses there had been handed to him, and Pisa's revolt followed (the editor's summary). Florence recovered Pisa only in 1509.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's "subject city", which by the rein of the fortress cannot think of rebellion unless it sees a foreign army hostile to the prince in the province, answers this case; Charles VIII's coming is such an army, in the editor's reading [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.10

**Argument.** The conclusion for the second case: to hold one's own fatherland the fortress is damaging; to hold acquired towns fortresses are useless; and the authority of the Romans suffices, who in towns they meant to hold by violence unwalled and did not wall.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini answers the authority of the Romans with two causes for their practice, and the unwalling with its contrary: at Tarentum and wherever they found fortresses made they did not unwall them [[FG-CONS.2.24.03]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.11

**Argument.** The objection from Tarentum and Brescia, places recovered from rebellion by means of their fortresses. For Tarentum, Fabius Maximus was sent with a whole army, which could have recovered it without the fortress, and would have found another path; and a fortress that needs a consular army and a Fabius to give you back the town is of no utility Machiavelli can see. Capua, which had no fortress, was retaken all the same by the army's *virtù*.

**History.** Tarentum fell to Hannibal in 212 BC while its citadel held out under Livius, and was retaken by Fabius in 209 (Livy 25.7-11, 27.15-16); Capua was retaken in 211 (Livy 26). "At the end of a year" is Machiavelli's figure (the editor's references).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini turns Tarentum the other way: the Romans did not destroy that fortress, or the others they found built, and would have if they had judged them useless [[FG-CONS.2.24.03]].

## NM-DISC.2.24.12

**Argument.** Brescia. It seldom happens that a fortress left in your hands when the town rebels has a large army near, as the French had: Gaston de Foix, at Bologna, understanding the loss, went without delay, arrived in three days and recovered the town through the fortress. So the fortress of Brescia also needed a Foix and a French army to succour it in three days; the example does not outweigh the contrary ones, since in the wars of these times many fortresses have been taken and retaken with the same fortune as the open country, in Lombardy, Romagna, the Kingdom of Naples and all Italy.

**History.** Brescia rose for Venice in early February 1512; Gaston de Foix marched from Bologna, entered by the castle, and retook and sacked the town on 19 February; he fell at Ravenna in April (the editor's dates).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini cites the same case for the other side: Foix, though with a powerful army, could not have recovered Brescia had he not been let in through the fortress [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]]. The case is the objector's, which Machiavelli answers; Guicciardini turns it back: there the fortress needed the army, here the army needed the fortress. Machiavelli's "seldom" and Guicciardini's "sometimes" meet on one event (the editor's reading).

## NM-DISC.2.24.13

**Argument.** The first case, fortresses against outside enemies: they are not necessary to peoples or kingdoms with good armies, and useless to those without, since good armies defend themselves without fortresses and fortresses cannot defend you without good armies. Experience of those held excellent shows it: the Romans did not build fortresses, and the Spartans not only abstained from them but would not have walls to their city, wanting the *virtù* of the particular man, and no other defence, to defend them; so a Spartan, asked whether the walls of Athens were beautiful, answered yes, if women lived there.

**History.** Sparta was unwalled until the Hellenistic period; the answer is of the kind collected among the sayings of the Spartans (the editor's notes).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not take up external enemies separately; his "potentate with strangers" and the uses of the fortress in war (holding the enemy, the interval gained) bear on it, in the editor's reading [[FG-CONS.2.24.01]] [[FG-CONS.2.24.02]]. The 1554 omits the clause on those without good armies (variant note).

## NM-DISC.2.24.14

**Argument.** A prince with good armies may sometimes find a fortress on the coasts, at the front of his state, useful for holding the enemy some days until he is ready; but it is not necessary. A prince without a good army finds fortresses in his state or at the frontiers damaging or useless: damaging, because he readily loses them and then they make war on him; useless, if they are too strong to be taken, because good armies, unless strongly opposed, enter enemy country and leave cities and fortresses behind, as the ancient histories show and as Francesco Maria did in assailing Urbino, leaving ten hostile cities behind. So a prince who can make a good army can do without fortresses; one who cannot must not build them, but should fortify the city where he lives, keep it furnished and its citizens well disposed, to sustain an onset until accord or outside aid frees him. All other designs are expense in peace and useless in war. The chapter ends where it began: the Romans, wise in every other order, were prudent in their judgment of the Latins and the Privernates, securing themselves of them by more virtuous and wiser ways than fortresses.

**History.** Francesco Maria della Rovere, deprived of Urbino by Leo X in 1516, recovered it with a mercenary army in 1517, then lost it again (the editor's dates).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's closing confession grants part of this: in many cases and times fortresses do not help, and there are other remedies, perhaps sometimes more useful and harder; but they are often useful against conspiracies and rebellions and for recovering lost towns [[FG-CONS.2.24.03]].
