## FG-CONS.3.17.01

**Argument.** The reply sets the precept against the prince before it answers the example. A prince is to abstain from committing himself to a man he has injured much more than a republic, for three reasons. The man injured by a prince acknowledges the injury as wholly the prince's; the man injured by a republic acknowledges the injury more as from some particular man who persecuted him or held the magistracy than from the name of the city, and therefore, offending the city, it does not seem to him that he avenges himself. Whoever seeks the ruin of the fatherland does ill to his kinsmen, his friends, all his own things and himself, with infamy, which does not befall whoever acts against a prince. And a prince is easier to extinguish than a republic, and for this one who is injured can be more prompt to enter into this thought.

Therefore Guicciardini would not be quick to shun (`non sarei facile a fuggire`) a citizen injured by his republic, above all when the injury has not been very atrocious, "in which case one could have respect to it". As printed the case follows the not-atrocious clause; the sense seems to require the atrocious injury the negation implies, where the injury would be taken into account (the editor's reading; the translation leaves it unresolved). On that reading the conclusion, conditional and personal, leaves the atrocious injury where Machiavelli put every notable injury.

Nero's case he calls a thing ridiculous to believe: that through having been calumniated for the time he was in Spain, "and also with some reason" (the calumny not baseless, in the editor's reading; it could be the indignation that had reason), he should have had so much indignation that he could desire to be routed. The words, he says, were not Nero's but Salinator's, who after his consulship had been condemned by the people, and, having received such an ignominy, it is no marvel if he resented it more. Of Salinator himself, although he spoke so from indignation or from certain natures or fantasies that men have, it is to be believed that in fact he meant otherwise, and his proceedings show it: first, he refused the consulship obstinately until he was almost forced by the principal citizens, which he would have desired had he had cupidity to avenge himself; afterwards, chosen consul, he did what was possible to win, and went very reluctant to give battle with Hasdrubal, even though he had said earlier that he wished to hasten it.

**History.** Marcus Livius Salinator, Nero's colleague in the consulship of 207 BC. Livy tells of his condemnation by the people after his consulship and retirement to the country, his recall to the city by the consuls and his restoration to the Senate by the censors (27.34), where he alone refuses the consulship offered, accusing the city's levity and complaining that the white gown is forced on an unwilling man, until the senators rebuke him and all together make him consul with Nero; his answer to Fabius that he would fight as soon as he saw the enemy, and, asked why the haste, that he would take either outstanding glory from the enemy or a joy, deserved if not honourable, from his defeated fellow citizens, with Livy's note that he set out still full of anger against the citizens (27.40); and, before the Metaurus, a council in which many favoured delay so that Nero's men could rest, without naming Livius among them (27.46) (the editor's references). Canestrini's note identifying Salinator is not carried.

**Cross-reference.** The prince is Guicciardini's: Machiavelli's precept is for a republic [[NM-DISC.3.17.01]], and the comparison leaves it in force there, less strongly. "Avenge himself", "calumniated" and "indignation" take up Machiavelli's account of the charge at Rome, the talk in the city and Nero's answer [[NM-DISC.3.17.02]]; "ridiculous to believe" refuses the answer as evidence, and the giving of the words to Salinator follows Livy, who puts words of like sense in Salinator's mouth (27.40, the editor's reference). That Salinator went very reluctant to give battle is Guicciardini's account, not Livy's in so many words. Machiavelli's conclusion, that because a sure remedy cannot be given to such disorders it is impossible to order a perpetual republic [[NM-DISC.3.17.03]], is not answered.
