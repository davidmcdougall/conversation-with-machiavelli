## FG-CONS.2.10.01

**Argument.** Guicciardini passes over the chapter's examples and takes up its reading of the sentence. Whoever first said that money is the sinew of war, and whoever has followed it since, did not mean that money alone makes war, nor that it is more necessary than soldiers: that would have been an opinion false and ridiculous. They meant that whoever made war had very great need of money, and that without it war could not be sustained. The reasons that follow are his own, in the present tense: money is needed to pay soldiers and to provide arms, victuals, spies and munitions, in a quantity no one who has not tried it can imagine. An army in dearth of money has sometimes provided for it by its *virtù* and its victories, but these are most rare examples, above all in his own time, and in every case and at every time money follows armies only after they have won. He confesses that soldiers of one's own make war with less money than mercenaries; but even they need money, not everyone has them, and it is much easier to find soldiers with money than money with soldiers. The close is a prediction under a condition: whoever interprets the sentence in its author's sense, and as it is commonly understood, will neither marvel at it nor condemn it. He does not call the sentence true in so many words, and he says nothing to deny that soldiers are needed.

**Cross-reference.** The sentence and its followers are Machiavelli's report of Curtius and of the princes who follow him (`La quale sentenza`, `seguitata`) [[NM-DISC.2.10.02]]; Guicciardini's `falsa` answers Machiavelli's `Né può essere più falsa` there. The concession is made on the arms of one's own of [[NM-DISC.2.10.01]]; the chiasmus reverses [[NM-DISC.2.10.04]]; and `non corrono e' danari dietro agli eserciti` answers the gold carried into the Roman camps there. He passes over the Spartan king, the necessities of [[NM-DISC.2.10.05]], and every example, and he does not refer to Machiavelli's money necessary in the second place [[NM-DISC.2.10.06]].
