## NM-DISC.1.1.01

**Argument.** The book opens on Rome's beginning and on what came of it: the *virtù* that lasted and the empire the republic won. The claim underneath the chapter is that the beginning explains the rest.

## NM-DISC.1.1.02

**Argument.** The first distinction: cities are built by natives or by strangers. Guicciardini accepts it, and works on both halves: where Rome falls [[FG-CONS.1.1.01]], and what colonies are [[FG-CONS.1.1.02]].

## NM-DISC.1.1.03

**History.** Theseus was credited with the synoecism of Athens, drawing the villages of Attica into one city. Venice was held to have begun with refugees from the mainland settling the lagoon islands during the barbarian invasions of the fifth and sixth centuries.

## NM-DISC.1.1.04

**Argument.** Cities built by strangers who are not free, colonies and princes' foundations, rarely grow great. Florence is the example Machiavelli's readers would feel: a city born dependent, under Rome. Guicciardini replies that dependence at the start decides less than this [[FG-CONS.1.1.02]].

## NM-DISC.1.1.05

**Argument.** Free builders: peoples driven out by disease, famine or war, like Moses and Aeneas. Here the builder's *virtù* shows, in two things, the choice of site and the ordering of laws. This chapter takes the site; the laws are deferred to later chapters ("as will be said below").

## NM-DISC.1.1.06

**Argument.** The central turn. Men act by necessity or by choice, and *virtù* is greater where choice counts for less; so a sterile site, which forces men to work, would seem best. But men want to command others, and security needs power, so a fertile site is necessary, and the laws must supply the necessity the site does not. Guicciardini also prefers a fertile site, but because it draws people; and where Machiavelli lets the laws supply necessity, Guicciardini ranks necessity above the laws [[FG-CONS.1.1.03]].

**History.** The Mamluks were a soldiery of purchased slaves who ruled Egypt from 1250, and Syria from 1260, until the Ottoman conquest of 1516–17 under Selim I.

## NM-DISC.1.1.07

**History.** Vitruvius (*De architectura* 2, preface) tells of Dinocrates proposing to carve Mount Athos into the figure of a man holding a city in one hand; there Alexander objects that the city could only be fed from overseas. The architect who "had not thought of it", and Alexander's laugh, are Machiavelli's.

**Cross-reference.** Alexandria appears twice: in .04 as a prince's foundation, not free and so unlikely to grow great, and here as the model of a well-chosen site.

## NM-DISC.1.1.08

**Argument.** Rome fits either case, built by strangers if Aeneas is its progenitor, by natives if Romulus, and on either account its beginning was free. Guicciardini refuses the Aeneas option and keeps Rome among cities built by natives, as on Machiavelli's Romulus reading, but for a different reason: it was built without necessity, as a colony of Alba [[FG-CONS.1.1.01]]. The last sentence announces the plan of Book I: things done inside the city by public counsel.
