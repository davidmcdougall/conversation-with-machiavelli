## FG-CONS.1.28.01

**Argument.** Guicciardini begins with a counterfactual. If Rome had never lost its liberty after the kings, the reason considered in the Discourse could perhaps be approved; but whoever considers that the Ten occupied the tyranny, and held it until necessity constrained them to lay it down, will say that this discord, the difference between the cities, has arisen from another foundation. Above all because of how Rome dealt with them: in the time of the recovery, when the memory of injuries is fresher and one is wont to proceed more atrociously, Rome proceeded against the Ten and their adherents most humanely and with supreme circumspection.

He offers two causes and believes one. Either it arose from the nature of the Romans, who lacked the levity of the Athenians, a levity the Athenians shared with the other Greeks; or, `come io credo`, the diversity of government was the cause. Athens was merely popular, and wars, peaces and the other important deliberations were treated in the harangues of the people. In Rome the people had its part, but the senate's authority was great, the plebs had the counterweight of the nobility's power, and commonly, apart from the creation of magistrates and the constitution of new laws, grave things were treated in the senate; the tribunes' authority to bring them to the people was used only where there was great temerity or urgent cause. Hence at Athens the citizens could much more readily than at Rome make themselves great by the popular arts, and in the merely popular government could more readily come into suspicion and be oppressed with more levity and less consideration. At Rome the greatness of the citizens was more moderate, since to continue in it they needed the senate's consent as well as popular favour. Where there is less greatness, there is less cause to suspect; where the government is mixed, there is neither so much inclination nor so much facility to beat the powerful, since one tribune could accuse them and another could oppose the accusation, and would perhaps have done so, seeing it calumnious. The close draws three consequences from the quality of the Roman government, graver, more tempered and more prudent than the Athenian: the path to tyranny less open to the citizens, in consequence less reason to suspect them, and less facility to beat the powerful.

He does not choose between the causes by refuting the first; he states a belief in the second. Whether the Athenian levity of the first returns in the second, where the popular government oppresses "with more levity", the reader may judge.

**History.** The first board of decemvirs was appointed in 451 BC to write the laws; the second, under Appius Claudius, kept office beyond its year and ruled as tyrants until the death of Verginia brought it down in 449 (Livy 3.33-58). Machiavelli discusses the decemvirate at I.35 and I.40-45. Of the ten, Appius Claudius and Spurius Oppius killed themselves in prison before judgment, the rest went into exile and their goods were confiscated; the tribune Marcus Duillius then declared an end to prosecutions (Livy 3.56-59). Guicciardini gives no instances of the humanity he praises.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's claim rests on a span, from the expulsion of the kings to Sulla and Marius, in which no citizen took Rome's liberty away [[NM-DISC.1.28.02]], and on a counterfactual: had Rome lost its liberty as Athens did, it would have been no more pious [[NM-DISC.1.28.04]]. Guicciardini's opening reverses the counterfactual and names the Ten, who fall within the span. He does not say whether their tyranny is the case Machiavelli's counterfactual imagined, with its Athenian conditions; he says only that Rome, having recovered its liberty from them, proceeded humanely.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's rule of the recovery, `per essere più fresca la memoria delle ingiurie si suole procedere più atrocemente`, is close to two things in Machiavelli: Athens `ricordandosi delle ingiurie ricevute` and the maxim of the writers of civility, that peoples bite more fiercely after recovering their liberty [[NM-DISC.1.28.03]]. Guicciardini cites neither; he grants the rule as what is wont to happen, and denies it of Rome.

**Cross-reference.** The mechanism is Machiavelli's: less cause to suspect [[NM-DISC.1.28.01]]. Guicciardini keeps it, in his own words (`minore causa di sospettare di loro`), and moves its ground from an accident, liberty never taken away, to an order, the mixed government. Machiavelli's `diversità degli accidenti` [[NM-DISC.1.28.04]] becomes `la diversità del governo`; his `inconsideratamente` [[NM-DISC.1.28.02]] returns as the Athenians' `manco considerazione`; and where Machiavelli has Rome without cause to offend its citizens, Guicciardini has `battere`, beat, three times, twice of the facility a mixed government lacks. The word `ingratitudine` does not occur in the reply. The calumnious accusation that a second tribune could stop is the distinction between accusation and calumny drawn in I.7 and I.8.
