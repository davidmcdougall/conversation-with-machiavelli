## NM-DISC.1.5.01

**Argument.** The question I.4 left behind. The tumults gave Rome its tribunes, and the tribunes were the guard of its liberty (I.4 .05); now Machiavelli asks where such a guard is better placed. Every republic has great men and popular men — the two humours of I.4 (I.4 .02) — and founders have chosen both ways: Sparta and Venice gave the guard to the nobles, Rome to the plebs. Guicciardini's reply begins by saying he does not understand the question [[FG-CONS.1.5.01]].

**History.** Sparta and Venice, whose Great Council became hereditary with the closing (*Serrata*) of 1297, were the standing examples of the aristocratic republic; Rome's tribunate was the example of the other kind. Machiavelli names no Spartan institution.

## NM-DISC.1.5.02

**Argument.** Two ways of judging. By the reasons there is something to say on every side; by the end, one would take the nobles' part, since Sparta's and Venice's liberty lasted longer than Rome's. Machiavelli then gives Rome's reasons first: put on guard over a thing those with the least appetite to usurp it. The great desire to dominate; the people desire only not to be dominated, so it is reasonable that they should care more for liberty and, unable to occupy it themselves, not let others occupy it.

## NM-DISC.1.5.03

**Argument.** The other side, in its defenders' voice: giving the guard to the powerful does two good works. It satisfies the ambition of the great, and it takes from the restless plebs an authority that breeds dissension and is apt to drive the nobility to a desperation that in time does harm. Their example is Rome itself: armed with the tribunes, the plebs wanted one consulship, then both, then every degree of command, and in time began to adore whoever could beat the nobility — whence the power of Marius and the ruin of Rome.

**History.** The Licinian-Sextian laws of 367 BC required, as Livy reports, that one consul be plebeian; the first, Lucius Sextius, held office in 366. Plebeians reached the censorship in 351 and the praetorship in 337. Both consulships were open by law from 342, but two plebeians first held them together in 172 BC. The defenders' order — one consul, both, then the censorship and praetorship — is rhetorical, not chronological. Marius, consul seven times between 107 and 86 BC, was the popular leader whose rivalry with Sulla ended in civil war.

## NM-DISC.1.5.04

**Argument.** The verdict on the first question is conditional. Weighed well, the reasons might leave one in doubt, since it is unclear which quality of men is more harmful, those who desire what they lack or those who desire to keep what they have. So the answer depends on the republic: one that means to make an empire must do everything like Rome; one content to maintain itself can imitate Venice and Sparta. The next chapter takes this up. Guicciardini refuses the distinction [[FG-CONS.1.5.02]].

## NM-DISC.1.5.05

**Argument.** The second question, through a Roman case. A plebeian dictator, given power to investigate those who sought office by ambition and extraordinary ways, was charged by the nobles with being such a man himself; he resigned, submitted to the people's judgment, and was acquitted. The dispute it raised was who is more ambitious, the man who would keep or the man who would acquire. Machiavelli grants that either appetite readily causes very great tumults, and then holds that most often they are caused by those who possess: the fear of losing breeds the same longings as wanting; possessing much, they can make alteration with greater power; and their unruly, ambitious conduct kindles in those who do not possess a longing to possess, either to avenge themselves by despoiling them or to enter into the wealth and honours they see ill used. In the Roman case, those who possess are the nobles.

**History.** Livy 9.26: in 314 BC the dictator Gaius Maenius and his master of the horse Marcus Folius, both plebeians, were appointed to investigate conspiracies at Capua, and the inquiry turned on the Roman nobility, who charged the dictator himself. Maenius resigned and was acquitted. Machiavelli's names are Marco Menennio and Marco Fulvio; the 1554 edition prints `Follio`.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not take up this second question; his reply ends with the first [[FG-CONS.1.5.02]].
