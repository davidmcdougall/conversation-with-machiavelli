## NM-DISC.1.58.01

**Argument.** The chapter opens by stating the case against its thesis: Livy and all the other historians affirm that nothing is more vain and inconstant than the multitude, and Livy supplies two texts, the Roman people desiring Manlius after it had condemned him, and the nature of the multitude at Syracuse, either to serve humbly or to dominate proudly. Machiavelli's undertaking is announced as a doubt: he does not know whether he is taking upon himself a hard province, one he may have to abandon with shame or follow with charge. What he stakes is method: he does not judge it a defect to defend an opinion with reasons, without using authority or force.

**History.** Manlius Capitolinus, who had saved the Capitol from the Gauls, was accused of aiming at kingship and condemned in the year conventionally dated 384 BC (Livy 6.20). Hieronymus, grandson of Hiero II, reigned at Syracuse in 215-214 BC; after his murder the Syracusan crowd killed the women of the royal house, and Livy's remark on the nature of the multitude comes in that account (24.25). Livy goes on to say that liberty, the mean between the two extremes, the multitude knows neither how to build with measure nor how to hold; Machiavelli quotes only the extremes.

## NM-DISC.1.58.02

**Argument.** The first move extends the defect from the multitude to men taken one by one: each one not regulated by the laws would make the errors of the unbound multitude, and princes above all. The princes in question are those who have been able to break the rein that can correct them, of whom many have been and few good and wise. The kings of Egypt, of Sparta and of France were held by laws, and so they are not to be counted among the princes whose nature is to be weighed against the multitude; against them one must set a multitude likewise regulated. Such a multitude will show the same goodness, and Livy's two verbs come back denied: it will neither dominate proudly nor serve humbly. Rome is the instance, while the republic lasted uncorrupted.

**Cross-reference.** France as a kingdom moderated by laws is also Machiavelli's example in I.16, where its kings had bound themselves to infinite laws. Guicciardini sets the French kings among the unbound and says the Author calls them `legati` [[FG-CONS.1.58.03]]; Machiavelli's word here is `moderato`, and at .10 `obbligato alle leggi`.

## NM-DISC.1.58.03

**Argument.** The Roman people rose against the powerful and obeyed dictators and consuls when the public safety required it. The Manlius case is turned, not denied: if the people desired Manlius after his death, it desired his *virtù*, which the writers agree is praised even in enemies, and had he risen again it would have judged him as it did. Princes held wise did the same with those they had put to death: Alexander with Clitus, Herod with Mariamne.

**History.** In Livy the senate released Manlius from prison under pressure from the plebs (6.17) and the people later condemned him (6.20); Machiavelli gives both acts to the people. Alexander killed Clitus at Maracanda in 328 BC (Arrian, *Anabasis* 4.8-9; Plutarch, *Alexander* 50-52); Herod had Mariamne put to death about 29 BC (Josephus, *Antiquities* 15.7).

## NM-DISC.1.58.04

**Argument.** Machiavelli limits Livy's saying to its case: it was said of an unbound multitude, the Syracusan, not of one regulated by the laws, like the Roman. The unbound multitude made the errors of men infuriated and unbound, and Alexander and Herod are again the comparison. So the conclusion is drawn on even terms: the nature of the multitude is not more to be inculpated than that of princes, because all err equally when all can err without respect. The Roman emperors and other tyrants supply examples of inconstancy and variation of life greater than any multitude's.

## NM-DISC.1.58.05

**Argument.** The conclusion is stated against the common opinion, and graded. A people that commands and is well ordered will be stable, prudent and grateful no otherwise than a prince, or better than a prince even esteemed wise; an unbound prince will be more ungrateful, various and imprudent than a people. The variation arises not from a different nature, which is of one way in all, but from more or less respect to the laws. The clause that if there is advantage of good it is in the people is a conditional aside; the thesis is the sameness of nature and the difference of laws.

## NM-DISC.1.58.06

**Argument.** Two proofs from Rome: four hundred years of enmity to the royal name and love of the common good, and, against the charge of ingratitude to Scipio, the answer already discoursed.

**Cross-reference.** The answer on Scipio is I.29, where Machiavelli judges peoples less ungrateful than princes. In this edition I.29 is a pair; Guicciardini's reply there adds envy and ignorance to the causes of ingratitude.

## NM-DISC.1.58.07

**Argument.** From equality the chapter turns to superiority in prudence and stability. The likening of the people's voice to God's is given a ground, `non senza cagione`: a universal opinion makes marvellous effects in its prognostications, so that it seems (`pare che`) that by an occult *virtù* it foresees its ill and its good. In judging, it very seldom fails to take the better of two opinions, and the claim holds `quando e' sono di egual virtù`, when the speakers are of equal *virtù*. Its error in hard things, or those that seem useful, granted earlier in the work, is set beside the prince's errors in his own passions, which are more.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini answers `non senza cagione si assomiglia la voce d'un Popolo a quella di Dio` with `non sanza cagione è assomigliata la moltitudine alle onde del mare` [[FG-CONS.1.58.01]]. The place where the people errs in `cose gagliarde` is presumably I.53, which this edition does not translate.

## NM-DISC.1.58.08

**Argument.** Three further proofs: choices of magistrates, where a people will not be persuaded to raise an infamous man, as a prince readily is; constancy in an opinion held for centuries, Rome's hatred of the royal name, which no debt to a citizen could overcome; and the excessive augmentations of cities where peoples are princes, Rome after the kings and Athens after Pisistratus. The last is put as a cause: it can arise from nothing other than that the governments of peoples are better.

**History.** Pisistratus died in power at Athens in 528/7 BC; the city was freed from his son Hippias in 510 BC. Machiavelli names the house by its founder, as at I.2 and I.28.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini calls the argument from augmentation impertinent, because it `nasce da altre cagioni`, in Machiavelli's own verb [[FG-CONS.1.58.03]].

## NM-DISC.1.58.09

**Argument.** Livy is not to be set against this opinion, because if all the disorders and glories of both are discoursed, the people will be seen to be by far superior. The princes' part is put conditionally: if princes are superior in ordering laws, civil lives, statutes and new orders, peoples are so much superior in maintaining what is ordered that they add, without doubt, to the glory of those who ordered it.

## NM-DISC.1.58.10

**Argument.** The summary puts both forms under the laws: states of princes and of republics have both lasted much, and both have needed to be regulated, since a prince who can do what he wishes is mad and a people that can is not wise; the asymmetry of the two predicates is Machiavelli's. A prince obliged to the laws against a people chained by them: more *virtù* in the people. Unbound against unbound: fewer and lesser errors in the people, and greater remedies, since a licentious people can be spoken to by a good man and brought into the good path, while against a bad prince there is no remedy but the steel. The physician's figure follows: where greater cure is required there are greater errors.

## NM-DISC.1.58.11

**Argument.** The fears differ by tense. The madnesses of an unbound people are not feared for themselves but for the tyrant that can arise from them; with a wicked prince the present ill is feared and hope placed in the future. The cruelties differ by object: the multitude's are against those it fears may occupy the common good, the prince's against those feared to occupy his own good.

## NM-DISC.1.58.12

**Argument.** The last paragraph gives the cause of the common opinion the chapter began by contradicting: of peoples men speak ill without fear, even while peoples reign; of princes only with a thousand fears and respects. The question of which confederations are more to be trusted is handed to the next chapter.

**Cross-reference.** Machiavelli's account of where the opinion against peoples arises (`nasce`) has its counterpart in Guicciardini's claim that the examples `meritamente hanno partorito` the writers' opinion [[FG-CONS.1.58.02]]: one says the opinion comes from fear of speaking of princes, the other that it was deserved. Guicciardini does not answer I.59.
