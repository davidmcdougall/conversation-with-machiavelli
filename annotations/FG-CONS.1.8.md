## FG-CONS.1.8.01

**Argument.** He grants the conclusion in Machiavelli's word — calumnies are detestable — and doubts they can be removed: difficult, and perhaps impossible. They are natural in a free city: a false charge, born of malice or error, spreads in a multitude readier to believe ill than good, and there are always men to foment it; and therefore even Rome, where accusation was easy and open, was full of false charges, as Fabius Maximus found. The calumniator cannot always be punished, and no way of republic, except on paper, provides so promptly against every disorder. Therefore free peoples will always have calumniators. Then he lowers the stakes. False calumnies often die of themselves; a grave citizen calumniated does not turn on the republic, whatever his anger at the author and, more, at whoever accused him falsely; private resentments make no important disorder in a city otherwise well regulated; the scandalous calumnies, like Manlius', are put down, and the rest fall of themselves. Then the two examples. The unnamed citizen is Cosimo de' Medici, and the example is a dream: prudence and, above all, excessive wealth opened his path, with which, the government of Florence being most disordered and by nature full of sedition, he corrupted citizens and, fomenting division, walked to tyranny as head of a party. And Giovanni Guicciardini's case was begged for want of examples: true, he was calumniated unjustly and had no way to clear himself, but the division of the city did not arise from his calumny nor grow by it; the city's discords made his case of more moment than it would have been by the ordinary. He ends without a stop.

**History.** Fabius Maximus, dictator in 217 BC, was decried at Rome for cowardice, and made suspect of collusion when Hannibal spared his estate; his master of the horse was given equal command (Livy 22.12–26). Cosimo de' Medici, exiled in 1433, returned in 1434 and governed Florence until his death in 1464 through his wealth, his bank and his party; Guicciardini's charge here, corruption and a walk to tyranny, is the conventional one against a rule kept under republican forms. Canestrini's note on Cosimo, which is his and is not carried, glosses Machiavelli's unnamed citizen the same way. Giovanni Guicciardini was commissary at Lucca in 1430; Canestrini's other note says the calumny was favoured by `nobili venduti a Cosimo`, "nobles sold to Cosimo".

**Cross-reference.** `Detestabili` is Machiavelli's [[NM-DISC.1.8.02]]; his rule was to open many places to accusation, and Guicciardini's answer is that Rome, which had them, had calumnies anyway. Manlius is the case Machiavelli told [[NM-DISC.1.8.01]], now the type of the scandalous calumny that is put down. The citizen who used calumnies among other means [[NM-DISC.1.8.03]] becomes Cosimo, whose path was opened not by calumnies but by prudence and money. Machiavelli said many examples could be adduced and gave one [[NM-DISC.1.8.04]]; Guicciardini says the matter is narrow of them, and that one was begged. He grants Giovanni Guicciardini the want of a way to clear himself, which is Machiavelli's diagnosis, affirms the injustice, which Machiavelli left open, and refuses the cause; `di più momento` turns Machiavelli's phrase for Manlius' tumults ([[NM-DISC.1.8.01]]) on the case itself. His point that Rome's easy accusation did not stop false charges keeps the caution of his reply to I.7, where the ways of accusing were to be ordered so that the innocent are not readily vexed.
