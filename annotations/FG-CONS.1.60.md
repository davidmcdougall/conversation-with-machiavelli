## FG-CONS.1.60.01

**Argument.** He answers Machiavelli's fact, not his reasons. The Discourse does not remember that the younger Scipio Africanus could be consul only once a particular law had lifted the ban of age; nor that Cicero, in a work the text leaves blank, says that a man of thirty-three lacks ten years of the time to be consul. Corvinus is taken under `se`, neither granted nor disputed: if it was otherwise with him, it must be said, and so with truth, that the orders of the early republic were other than those that arose later. The interval between consulships is a like case: at first nothing forbade continuing in the consulship, and later a law required ten years between one consulship and the next. Whether the two laws, of age and of time, are useful to republics he leaves to another place, since the Discourse does not treat them. He does not take up the argument from blood, or Machiavelli's claim, on the editor's reading of `quella del tempo`, that disregard of age is necessary.

**History.** Scipio Aemilianus, adopted son of the elder Africanus's son, was named consul by the people for the year conventionally dated 147 BC while standing for the aedileship, and released from the laws, since by his years he could not lawfully be consul (Livy, *Periochae* 50, the Latin Library text, consulted 2026-11-19); he triumphed in 146, at about thirty-eight. The ten-year interval is the plebiscite Livy records in the year conventionally dated 342 BC, that no one should hold the same magistracy again within ten years (7.42). Canestrini's note guesses Cicero's *De Legibus* III. The editor's identification is *Philippics* 5.47-48 (Latin Library text, consulted 2026-11-19), from the debate of January 43 BC on honours for the young Octavian. At 5.47 Cicero says that the early Romans had no laws fixing the ages for office, which came many years later, Guicciardini's distinction of early and later orders; at 5.48 that Alexander died in his thirty-third year, ten years short of the Roman consular age, which does not match Guicciardini's thirty-three exactly, and he names the Corvini and the elder Africanus as men made consul young, which tells for Machiavelli's examples. Whether Guicciardini had the passage in mind cannot be shown.

**Cross-reference.** The `mai` that Guicciardini's two examples answer, on the editor's reading, is Machiavelli's `il rispetto della età mai non fusse in Roma` [[NM-DISC.1.60.01]]. His Corvinus is the age witness of [[NM-DISC.1.60.01]], named again at [[NM-DISC.1.60.03]]; his Scipio is the younger, where Machiavelli's, among those who triumphed most young, is presumably the elder.
