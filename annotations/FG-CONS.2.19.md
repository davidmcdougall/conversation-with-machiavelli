## FG-CONS.2.19.01

**Argument.** The reply is one paragraph and does not dispute the chapter's long argument about Roman orders and the German republics. It takes the rubric's claim, that acquisitions in republics not well ordered, and not proceeding by Roman *virtù*, are to their ruin, at the point where Machiavelli applies it to Florence and Venice, and answers with a question: who doubts that the two would be weaker and of less power, had they shut their territory within small borders, than they are? Then the reasons, as a list without connectives: having subdued their neighbours and enlarged their jurisdiction, it is not easy for every neighbour to assail them; they are not troubled by every weak accident; if a great motion does not come, they hold the enemy outside the yolk of their state; war does not readily come to their walls; having many subjects makes the public revenues greater in many ways, and the dominant city richer in private. With these means, though not armed with soldiers of their own, they conduct strangers, and to be defended by them is better than to be defended by no one.

Then the concession and its limit. "I confess" that a republic with arms of its own is more powerful and makes more capital of acquisitions; "but I will not confess" that a disarmed republic becomes weaker the more it acquires, nor that Venice, which now fears neither kings nor emperors, would be more secure without dominion on land and at sea. The last sentence turns the claim against its own restriction: if it were true, Guicciardini does not know why the Discourse restricts it to republics, since for the same causes a prince without arms of his own would derive weakness and not power from acquisitions and the expansion of dominion; and that, both the reasons and the experience show at large to be most false.

The concession is narrow. In the editor's reading it is the only point at which the reply meets Machiavelli's condition, empire without forces (.08) and mercenary or auxiliary soldiers (.10); Guicciardini puts that condition as arms of one's own and a "disarmed" republic, words not in the chapter. Guicciardini does not deny that acquisition can harm. His opening question holds that dominion has made Florence and Venice stronger than small borders would have; what he will not grant is the general claim that a disarmed republic grows weaker the more it acquires. His "I do not know why" stands under the condition "if it were true": it opens a reductio, not a suspension. If disarmed republics grew weaker by acquiring, so would unarmed princes, and that consequence he calls most false. In the editor's reading, the restriction to republics is the rubric's and .04's; the chapter's general statements of the damage are put to "whoever" (.08) and "those who" avail themselves of mercenaries (.10), so the charge holds against the rubric more than against the whole chapter.

**History.** Venice's "now" follows the war of the League of Cambrai (1509-1517), after which it recovered most of its mainland dominion; the kings and emperors are the powers of that league, and the *Considerazioni* are usually dated about 1530 (all the editor's reading).

**Cross-reference.** The opening question answers Machiavelli's Venetians and Florentines, "much weaker" with Lombardy and Tuscany than with the sea and six miles of borders [[NM-DISC.2.19.08]]. Guicciardini's "small borders" recall both those six miles and the "few borders" of the quiet republic, which Machiavelli himself judges impossible [[NM-DISC.2.19.04]]; and his Venice "without dominion on land and at sea" answers the Venice "content with the sea" and goes beyond it, since Machiavelli takes away the land, not the sea (the editor's reading). "Subdued" is the verb of Machiavelli's Roman list ("subdue the enemy"), and "makes more capital of acquisitions" turns "make capital of the spoils" to the acquisitions themselves, in the editor's reading [[NM-DISC.2.19.03]]; so do his public revenues greater and dominant city "richer in private" stand against "the public rich, the private poor" there, and his "many subjects" against "companions and not subjects". "Soldiers of one's own" and the strangers conducted in their place answer the close, in the editor's reading, where Machiavelli speaks of those who avail themselves of soldiers either mercenary or auxiliary [[NM-DISC.2.19.10]]. "Weakness and not power", in the editor's reading, answers "empire and not forces" [[NM-DISC.2.19.08]]. Guicciardini does not take up the German republics, the leagues [[NM-DISC.2.19.07]], or the corruption by delights at Capua [[NM-DISC.2.19.09]], which Machiavelli gives as a damage that sometimes falls even on a well-ordered republic.
