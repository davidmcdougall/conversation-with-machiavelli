## NM-DISC.1.6.01

**Argument.** The question follows from I.4 and I.5. Machiavelli has argued that the controversies between people and senate kept Rome free; now he grants that they ran on to the Gracchi and ruined free living, and asks whether Rome could have had its greatness without them. The method is comparative: find the republics that were long free without such enmities, see what their state was, and ask whether it could have been brought to Rome.

**Cross-reference.** "Discoursed above" points to I.4 (I.4 .01), where the tumults are defended, and to I.5, where the guard of liberty is placed in the plebs (I.5 .04).

## NM-DISC.1.6.02

**Argument.** Venice first. Its form was given by chance more than by prudence: the first settlers, once numerous enough to need laws, ordered a government and closed it to later comers, and the names gentlemen and commoners came afterwards. It stayed quiet because no one already there was excluded, and those who came later had neither cause (nothing was taken from them) nor convenience (they were kept from any office that carried authority), and were never many enough to outnumber the governors.

**History.** The closing Machiavelli describes is the *Serrata* of the Great Council, begun in 1297 and completed by stages into the 1320s, which made membership hereditary in the families admitted; the "gentlemen" are the patricians inscribed in it. It did not admit everyone then living in Venice, as Machiavelli's account has it.

**Cross-reference.** Venice settled on its rocks "for the causes stated above": the flight from barbarian invasion in I.1 (I.1 .03).

## NM-DISC.1.6.03

**Argument.** Sparta second. A king and a narrow senate held it united because there were few inhabitants, strangers were kept out, and Lycurgus' laws made Sparta more equal in wealth and less equal in rank, so that the plebs, poor among the poor, did not covet the offices. The kings, set between the nobility and the people, protected the plebs from injury to secure their own dignity, and a plebs that did not fear did not desire rule. Two things, then: few citizens, and no strangers.

**History.** The "narrow senate" is the *gerousia*, twenty-eight elders with the two kings. Sparta granted citizenship to outsiders almost never; its citizen body shrank over the classical period, which bears on the "weak foundation" Machiavelli finds in it (.05).

## NM-DISC.1.6.04

**Argument.** The conclusion is reached by consequence. To be quiet like these republics Rome had either to keep the plebs out of war, as Venice did, or keep strangers out, as Sparta did; it did neither, and the plebs grew in force and in occasions of tumult. Had Rome been quieter, it would have been weaker, its path to greatness cut. The cause of the tumults and the cause of expansion were the same cause. Then the general rule: no inconvenience is removed without another arising, so a people armed for empire cannot be handled as you please, and a people kept small or unarmed cannot hold what it takes, or grows so base that you are anyone's prey. Choose the course with fewer inconveniences; nothing is wholly clean.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini grants the premise that Rome needed its plebs in arms, and reframes it in his opening clause: having armed them, Rome had to keep them content [[FG-CONS.1.6.01]], and denies the conclusion: the tumults came from the patricians' refusal to share, not from the arms.

## NM-DISC.1.6.05

**Argument.** Rome could have copied Sparta's king for life and small senate, but not its small citizen body, and so the copy would have done little for union. Whoever founds a republic must first choose: expansion, and then order it like Rome and accept the tumults; or narrow bounds, and then order it like Sparta or Venice and forbid it to acquire, because acquisitions on a weak foundation ruin it. Sparta and Venice both expanded and both fell at a touch.

**History.** Sparta's hegemony after the Peloponnesian War broke when Thebes, freed by Pelopidas in 379 BC, defeated it at Leuctra in 371. Venice's mainland empire, built through the fifteenth century, collapsed after Agnadello (14 May 1509), when the French army of the League of Cambrai routed its own in a single day; Venice recovered most of its mainland by 1517; Machiavelli calls it "one battle", as in *Principe* 12.

**Cross-reference.** The choice between a republic that would "make an empire" and one content to maintain itself restates I.5's verdict (I.5 .04), which promised that "the following chapter" would give the causes.

## NM-DISC.1.6.06

**Argument.** The ideal, granted and withdrawn. He would believe the best lasting republic one ordered within like Sparta or Venice, set in a strong place, strong enough to deter attack and not so great as to frighten its neighbours, war is made on a republic to master it or for fear of it, and such a republic almost wholly removes both motives; the more so if a law forbade it to expand. That would be "the true political living". But human things move; necessity drives where reason does not; a republic built not to expand, if forced to, loses its foundations, and one left in peace goes soft or divides. Since, as he believes, the balance cannot be held, the founder must choose the more honourable part, and order the republic so that it can keep what necessity makes it take.

**Cross-reference.** Idleness that makes a city effeminate or divided is the danger I.1 assigns to fertile sites (I.1 .06).

## NM-DISC.1.6.07

**Argument.** Back to the question: he believes the Roman order must be followed, since he does not believe a middle way can be found, and tolerate the enmities as the inconvenience necessary to Roman greatness. He adds the tribunes once more: besides guarding liberty, the authority of accusing, which the tribunes among others held, benefits republics; that is the subject of the next chapter.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini answers this sentence with his last: the tribunes, once the city was pacified, "rather damaging than useful" [[FG-CONS.1.6.01]].
