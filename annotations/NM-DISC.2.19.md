## NM-DISC.2.19.01

**Argument.** The chapter opens on "these opinions contrary to the truth", which in the editor's reading are the opinions of the chapter before, II.18, that set cavalry above infantry. They rest on the ill examples of corrupted centuries, and they make men not think of deviating from the accustomed ways: thirty years back no Italian could have been persuaded that ten thousand foot could fight and vanquish ten thousand horse and as many foot on a plain, as the example of Novara, cited more times, shows.

**History.** Novara is the battle of June 1513, where Swiss infantry routed a French army with its men-at-arms (the editor's identification). "Thirty years back" sets the persuasion before the French descent of 1494, if the chapter was written about 1517, as is usually supposed (the editor's dating).

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini does not take up infantry or cavalry; his reply begins from Machiavelli's Florence and Venice (.08) [[FG-CONS.2.19.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.19.02

**Argument.** Though the histories are full of it, the moderns would not have believed them; and had they believed them, they would have said that men now arm better, and that a squadron of men-at-arms would be apt to dash against a rock, let alone an infantry; so with false excuses they would have corrupted their judgment, and would not have considered that Lucullus with few foot routed a hundred and fifty thousand of Tigranes' horse, among them a cavalry like our men-at-arms. The fallacy has been uncovered by the example of the ultramontane troops. In Machiavelli's account ancient testimony would have been disbelieved, and a modern example uncovered the fallacy (the editor's summary).

**History.** Lucullus' victory over Tigranes is the battle of Tigranocerta, 69 BC (the editor's identification). The figure is Machiavelli's and is not corrected.

## NM-DISC.2.19.03

**Argument.** From infantry the argument widens: as the histories' report of infantry has been seen to be true, so all the other ancient orders should be believed true and useful. Were this believed, republics and princes would err less, resist an onset better, not hope in flight, and those who hold a civil living would direct it better, whether by expanding or by maintaining. They would believe that these paths (Rome's, in the editor's reading; see II.3-II.6) make a republic great and acquire empire: increase the city with inhabitants, make companions for themselves and not subjects, send colonies to guard acquired countries, make capital of the spoils, subdue the enemy by raids and battles and not by sieges, keep the public rich and the private poor, and maintain military exercises with supreme study.

**Cross-reference.** In Guicciardini's own account Florence and Venice depart from two of these orders: they have subjects, not companions, and conduct strangers in place of soldiers of their own [[FG-CONS.2.19.01]]. His public revenues greater and dominant city "richer in private" stand against "the public rich, the private poor", and his "makes more capital of acquisitions" turns "make capital of the spoils" to the acquisitions themselves (both the editor's reading).

## NM-DISC.2.19.04

**Argument.** The second path, maintaining: whoever did not like this way of expanding would think that acquisitions by any other path are the ruin of republics, rein in all ambition, regulate the city within by laws and customs, prohibit it from acquiring, think only of defence, and keep defences well ordered, as the republics of Germany do, which live so and have lived free for a time. But, as Machiavelli said when he discoursed of ordering to acquire and ordering to maintain, it is impossible for a republic to stand quiet and enjoy its liberty and the few borders: if it does not molest others it will be molested, and from being molested arises the longing and the necessity to acquire; without an enemy outside it would find one at home, as it seems necessary should befall all great cities. The German republics can live so, and have lasted a time, only from conditions found in that country and not elsewhere.

**Cross-reference.** The discourse on ordering to acquire and to maintain is Discourses I.6, in the editor's identification. Guicciardini does not take up the German republics. His "small borders" recall the "few borders" here as well as the six miles of .08 (the editor's reading). Machiavelli has himself judged a quiet republic within few borders impossible, so the two do not differ on staying small; they differ on what acquisition does to a republic without arms of its own [[FG-CONS.2.19.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.19.05

**Argument.** The German conditions begin in history. That part of Germany was subject to the Roman empire, as France and Spain were; when the empire declined and its title came to rest in that province, the more powerful cities, according to the baseness or necessity of the emperors, made themselves free, buying themselves back from the empire and reserving to it a small annual tax, until all the cities held immediately of the emperor, and subject to no prince, had bought themselves back.

**History.** The cities are the free and imperial cities of the Holy Roman Empire; "immediately of the emperor" is the status of immediacy, holding of the emperor with no lord between (the editor's gloss).

## NM-DISC.2.19.06

**Argument.** In the same times certain communities subject to the Duke of Austria rebelled, Filiburgo and the Swiss among them, and grew until, far from returning under Austria's yoke, they are held in fear by all their neighbours. The province is partitioned into Swiss, republics which they call Free Lands, princes and emperor. What keeps wars from arising among so many diversities of living, or from lasting, is the emperor's sign: without forces, he has such reputation that, as mediator, he extinguishes at once every scandal. The greatest and longest wars have been between the Swiss and the Duke of Austria; though emperor and duke have for many years been one, he has never overcome the Swiss, with whom there has been no accord but by force; nor has the rest of Germany given him many aids, the communities because they do not know how to offend those who wish to live free as they do, the princes because some are poor and some envy his power.

**History.** The emperors were Habsburg dukes of Austria from 1438 (the editor's note); Maximilian's war with the Swiss, ended at Basel in 1499, is the editor's example. "Filiburgo" is taken here for Fribourg (the editor's identification; the 1554 prints `Filiborg`).

## NM-DISC.2.19.07

**Argument.** Hence the German communities can live content with small dominion: the imperial authority gives them no cause to want more, and a near enemy, ready to seize them if they fell into discord, keeps them united within their walls. Were the province otherwise conditioned they would have to expand. Since such conditions are found nowhere else, this way of living cannot be taken elsewhere, and it is required either to expand by leagues or to expand as the Romans did.

**Cross-reference.** Discourses II.4 sets out three ways of expanding: leagues, as the Tuscans and the Swiss; companions, as Rome; and subjects, as Sparta and Athens, the last judged useless there. Two remain here (the editor's link). Guicciardini's reply does not consider leagues, and his "many subjects" defend the third way, in the editor's reading [[FG-CONS.2.19.01]].

## NM-DISC.2.19.08

**Argument.** Whoever governs himself otherwise seeks his death and ruin, for acquisitions are damaging in a thousand ways and for many causes, because empire and not forces go very well together; whoever acquires empire and not forces, it is fitting that he go to ruin. He cannot acquire forces who grows poor in wars, even victorious ones, putting in more than he draws out. So the Venetians and Florentines have been much weaker holding Lombardy and Tuscany than when the one was content with the sea and the other with six miles of borders: all has arisen from wishing to acquire without knowing the way, and they merit the more blame because they had the Romans' example, where the Romans, through their own prudence, found the way without one.

**History.** Venice's dominion on the mainland was checked at Agnadello in 1509; Florence's wars for Pisa ran from 1494 to 1509 (the editor's examples, not Machiavelli's).

**Cross-reference.** This is the paragraph Guicciardini answers most directly. He asks who doubts that Florence and Venice would be weaker within small borders than they are, and sets out what dominion gives them; he grants that a republic with arms of its own makes more capital of acquisitions, but will not grant that a disarmed one grows weaker the more it acquires [[FG-CONS.2.19.01]]. In the editor's reading, his "weakness and not power" answers "empire and not forces" here, and his Venice "without dominion on land and at sea" answers the Venice "content with the sea" and goes beyond it: Machiavelli takes away the land, not the sea.

## NM-DISC.2.19.09

**Argument.** A further damage: acquisitions sometimes harm even a well-ordered republic, when it acquires a city or province full of delights, where one can take some of its customs through the conversation one has with it. So it befell Rome first at Capua, and afterwards Hannibal. Had Capua been farther from the city, the soldiers' error not had its remedy near, or Rome been anywhere corrupted, that acquisition would without doubt have been Rome's ruin; Livy bears witness. Such cities avenge themselves on the victor without engagement and without blood, filling him with their wicked customs and exposing him to be vanquished by whoever assails him; and Juvenal says the same, that with the acquisitions of peregrine lands peregrine customs entered Roman breasts, and gluttony and luxury took the place of parsimony and other most excellent *virtù*.

**History.** Rome received Capua's surrender in 343 BC; the next year part of the Roman garrison there conspired to seize the city, and the mutiny that followed was ended by the dictator Valerius Corvus (Livy 7.38-41; the quotation is 7.38.5). Hannibal wintered in Capua after Cannae, 216-215 BC (the editor's references). The Juvenal is Satires 6.292-293; Machiavelli's line replaces the poet's `saevior armis` with `gula` (the editor's comparison).

## NM-DISC.2.19.10

**Argument.** The close draws the conclusion under its condition: if acquiring was about to be pernicious to the Romans, when they proceeded with so much prudence and *virtù*, what will it be to those who proceed at a distance from their ways, and who, besides their other errors, avail themselves of mercenary or auxiliary soldiers? The damages that often follow for them are the matter of the next chapter.

**Cross-reference.** Guicciardini's republics are, in the editor's reading, of just this kind: not armed with soldiers of their own, they conduct strangers, by whom to be defended is better than to be defended by no one [[FG-CONS.2.19.01]]. He grants that a republic with arms of its own is more powerful and makes more capital of acquisitions, and will not grant the rest. The next chapter, II.20, treats auxiliary and mercenary arms; Canestrini prints no reply to it.
