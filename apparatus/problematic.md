# Problematic — what the translation must keep visible

Each unit has a structure that smoothing would bury. These are checked on every
chunk.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.12

**The argument is a single chain, and the conclusion is an indictment reached by
it, not asserted.** Religion must be kept uncorrupted → because every religion
rests on some principal order of its own → which the prudent maintain and increase
*however false they judge it* → the Roman Church has not done this → therefore two
charges. Any rendering that lets the Church attack float free of the instrumental
argument about religion has lost the chapter.

**The instrumental irony must survive.** Machiavelli advises princes to favour and
increase what they privately judge false, and then condemns the Church for
corruption. He does not flag the tension. Neither does the translation.

**The two charges are numbered and must stay numbered.** `due potentissime` — the
first moral (example destroys devotion), the second political (division). This is
also where the 1554 witness transmits a different argument entirely (D006), so the
structure is doing evidentiary work as well as rhetorical.

**`favorirle ed accrescerle` → `favorita e accresciuta`.** Prescribed of princes in
.02, then reported of Camillus in .03. The echo is the demonstration: Rome did what
the chapter recommends. Both must land in the same English words.

**`propinqui` → `propinquo`.** Those nearest the Roman Church have least religion;
ruin or scourging is near. The repetition turns proximity from a blessing into a
threat within two sentences.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.12

**He concedes the premise and refuses the inference.** Not disagreement. The
movement is: intensify the condemnation of the Roman court beyond Machiavelli's →
accept as credible that the Church prevented an Italian monarchy → suspend
judgment on whether that was good or bad → distinguish republic from kingdom →
add counterweights → withhold easy concurrence. A translation that makes him
sound simply opposed destroys the unit, and with it the edition's argument.

**The acts of judgment are distinct and must not merge into one hedge.** `credo`,
`non so già`, `reputo`, `non concorro facilmente` are assent, suspension, judgment
and non-concurrence. Four different things.

**The hinge is `io non concorro facilmente`.** Ruled in SCOPE §3: *I do not readily
concur*. It answers Machiavelli's `discorrere` / `occorrono` directly, and the
whole terminology policy exists to protect that answer.

**He never resolves it.** The unit ends mid-thought, with no full stop in the
witness. The English does not supply a conclusion the Italian withholds.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.2

**The cycle is an argument, not a catalogue.** Six forms, each sliding into its
neighbour, and at every step the cause is inheritance; the mixed state is the
conclusion drawn from it, and Rome reaches it by accident, not by a founder.
Splitting the long periods of .04–.06 into a list of stages loses the causes
that join them.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.2

**He grants the conclusion and changes the subject.** Not disagreement: the
mixture is conceded in the first sentence, and the reply becomes a design. The
design runs through chains of `bisogna ... che` + subjunctive (.04, .05), and
every link must stay attached to its governor. Venice, not Rome, is his model;
the Romans' annual consuls and patrician optimates count against them.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.3

**The premise is a founder's working assumption, and the chapter proves it by
consequence.** `presupporre` is to take as given for the purpose of ordering.
Then the proof: the nobles hid their venom while the Tarquins lived, and
spat it once they were dead. Then the maxim, and then the tribunes, reached by
three consecutive `però`. The tribunes follow from the premise; an English
that lets the maxim or the Roman story stand alone has lost the chain.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.3

**He limits the premise and keeps the rule.** `troppo assolutamente`: the
claim is too absolute, not false. He grants that one must order against those
who would do ill, and denies only that all men are to be presupposed so. Then
he turns to the tribunes and takes away the middle place Machiavelli gives
them. A translation that makes him an optimist answering a pessimist, or makes
the second paragraph a separate topic, has lost the reply.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.4

**A defence against an opinion, built as a chain.** The tumults kept Rome
free → because laws for liberty arise from the disunion of the two humours →
and cost almost no blood → and good examples come from education, education
from laws, laws from the tumults. The chain `nascono ... dalla ... dalle ...
da quelli tumulti` must stay a chain, each link's verb carried by the first.
The objection (`E se alcuno dicesse`) is quoted and answered inside one
period; it must not be broken off as a separate voice.

**The concession is to fortune, not to *virtù*.** He grants that fortune and
soldiery made the rule, and turns it: good soldiery presupposes good order.
Guicciardini's last sentence gives the opinion back its point.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.4

**He moves the cause back one step.** Machiavelli says the disunion made Rome
free; Guicciardini says the disunion had a cause, the shutting of the plebs
out of the honours, which a better founding would not have had. The
counterfactual period of .01 (`se ... non nascevano ... non arebbe ... né
sarebbe`) states what would have been, not what was, and must stay
counterfactual. The simile of the sick man is its landing: the remedy was
good, the sickness was not.

**He ends by conceding what Machiavelli refused.** Military discipline and its
*virtù* sustained the other defects — the "many" of NM .01, whose opinion
Machiavelli set out to answer. The echo is `virtù` and `difetti` in both.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.5

**Two questions, answered in order, and the first is left open.** Where the
guard of liberty is better set: the case for Rome (the people want only not to
be dominated), the case for Sparta and Venice (the great are content, the plebs
quietened), and the verdict that it depends on whether a republic means to make
a rule or to maintain itself. Then the second question, through the case of
Menennius: those who possess cause more tumult than those who would acquire,
because the fear of losing breeds the same appetite. The English must not turn
the first verdict into a preference for Rome; Machiavelli says only that a
republic that would expand must do as Rome did.

**The opponents speak in their own period.** The Spartan and Venetian case
(.03) is reported speech — `dice, che` — down to the ruin of Rome. It is not
Machiavelli's judgment, and an English that lets the "two good works" or the
fury of the plebs float free of `dice` has made it his.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.5

**He does not understand the question, and the reply is a distinction.** Who
governs is one question, who holds a particular charge of liberty another. Rome
answers the second, and only partly: consuls and dictators guarded liberty as
much as the tribunes did. His own answer is that in a mixed government the guard
belongs to all, each order the guard of the other. The English must keep
`altro è a dire ..., altro è dire` as one opposition.

**The forced choice is conditional and future.** `Ma quando fussi necessitato
... crederò`: he does not prefer the nobles; he would believe them the lesser
error if he had to choose. Then he refuses Machiavelli's distinction outright:
the government of the plebs serves neither for acquiring nor for conserving,
and Rome was mixed, not plebeian.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.6

**A counterfactual answered by a trade-off.** Could Rome have been ordered
without the enmities? The chapter asks what made Sparta and Venice quiet
(chance and smallness at Venice, Lycurgus and the exclusion of strangers at
Sparta), finds that Rome did the opposite of both (armed the plebs, opened the
city to strangers), and concludes that the cause of the tumults and the cause
of the expansion were the same: no inconvenience can be cancelled without
another springing up. The English must keep the chain `per queste cagioni`
→ `Considerando adunque` → `In modo che` → `Pertanto`; the conclusion is
reached, not announced.

**The ideal is granted and then withdrawn.** `Crederei bene` and `credo` mark
his judgment that a small, well-defended, non-expanding republic would be
"the true political living"; `Ma sendo tutte le cose degli uomini in moto`
takes it away, and `come io credo` says it cannot be balanced. An English that
lets the ideal stand as his recommendation, or the withdrawal stand as a
maxim detached from it, has lost the movement. The enmities are tolerated as
`uno inconveniente necessario`, not praised.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.6

**He grants the premise and moves the cause.** `Io credo essere vero`: arming
the plebs required keeping it content. What made the tumults was not the arms
but the patricians' refusal to share the government and their injuries. Two
counterfactuals follow, both stated as what would have been, not what was:
no distinction of orders, or honours shared from the start; and, more
modestly (`forse`), a good order against injuries and a way for the principal
plebeians into the patriciate. The Sextian laws are his evidence that the
plebs wanted relief, not honours.

**His sharpest non-concurrence so far.** `Non veggo adunque ... anzi lo
giudico molto facile`: not "I do not readily concur" but "I do not see",
and then the opposite judgment. And the tribunes, Machiavelli's guard of
liberty, end as "rather damaging than useful". He does not soften it, and the
unit ends without a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.7

**An order, two effects, and a demonstration by its absence.** The authority
to accuse deters (the first effect) and vents the humours by an ordinary way
(the second). The proof runs through what happens without it: Coriolanus,
whom only the tribunes' summons saved from a riot; Valori, killed because
Florence had no ordinary way; Soderini, brought down with a foreign army;
Arruns, who fetched the Gauls. The chain `offesa ... paura ... difesa ...
partigiani ... parti ... rovina` (.04) must stay one chain, each link
generating the next. The chapter's verb is `sfogare`, "vent": the humours
will find an outlet, ordinary or extraordinary, and the English keeps the one
verb.

**The conclusion names the judges.** `bisogna che i giudici siano assai`,
"it is needful that the judges be many": Machiavelli's answer to Soderini's
eight. Guicciardini's reply picks it up and puts a number on it.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.7

**He grants the principle and moves the question to the judge.** `[È]
verissimo` is the strongest assent he has given, and `ma bisogna anche
avvertire` is where the reply begins: the innocent must not be readily
vexed. Then the candidates, each refused for its own reason: the people
(moved by uproars, led into servitude by those who court it, unable to put
down its favourites), and a select body of citizens (too powerful if chosen
narrowly, too respectful if broadly), when the judges must in fact be more
than fifty. His answer is a design, not a denial,
as in I.2: courts fitted to the humours, or judges mixed and nearer the mean.
The English must keep the concession and the turn in one period, and must not
make him reject accusations.

**He turns Machiavelli's own example.** Coriolanus, for Machiavelli the case
of an ordinary way that saved Rome, is for Guicciardini a citizen whose
condemnation did the city damage. The tribunes are not named, but the people
as judge is the tribunes' court, and the line of his I.5 and I.6 replies
continues. The unit ends without a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.8

**A story, a rule drawn from it, and a city that lacked it.** Manlius, envious
of Camillus, calumniates the senate to the plebs; the senate makes a
dictator, Manlius is summoned, cannot name the holders of the treasure, and
is imprisoned. The rule: calumnies are detestable, the remedy is many places
open to accusation and sharp punishment for calumniators, because a
calumny needs no proof and an accusation does. Florence lacked the order,
and its histories are full of calumnies: hatred, division, sects, ruin. The
chain `odio ... divisione ... Sette ... rovina` (.03) is I.7's chain again and
must stay one chain. The last sentence turns the calumniator into an accuser,
and the English keeps the condition (`quando l'accusa si riscontri vera`)
that decides between reward and penalty.

**The unnamed citizen and the named commissary.** "Some citizen" rose by
calumnies against the powerful (.03); Giovanni Guicciardini was ruined by
one (.04). Machiavelli names the second and not the first. The English must
not name him either.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.8

**He grants the conclusion and denies the remedy.** `[È] vera conclusione`:
calumnies are detestable. But they are natural in a free city, Rome with its
easy accusations had them in plenty, and no order on paper provides against
every disorder: `però` twice, each a consequence, not a contrast. Then he
lowers the stakes: false calumnies die of themselves, a grave citizen
does not turn on the republic, the scandalous are put down. The English must
keep the concession and the denial in one movement and must not make him
defend calumny.

**He names Machiavelli's citizen and turns both examples.** The example of
Cosimo "is a dream": prudence and wealth, not calumnies, made him. Giovanni
Guicciardini's case was "begged": true, and unjust, but it did not divide
the city; the city's discords made the case. The causation is reversed, and
the English must keep it reversed. The unit ends without a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.9

**A rule, the case it excuses, and the conditions that limit it.** The
founder's crime is stated first, as the opinion of "many"; the rule follows
(a republic or kingdom is ordered well, or reformed anew, only by one); then
its conditions (the end must be the common good, the effect must excuse the
deed, the authority must not be left hereditary, and the thing ordered must
pass into the care of many); then the proof that Romulus met them (the
senate, the narrow authority he kept, Rome's orders surviving the kings);
then Agis and Cleomenes. The verdict, "excuse, and not blame", is reached,
and is no more than excuse. The English must keep the conditions attached to
the rule, and must not let "it is necessary to be alone" float free as a
maxim.

**The deed and the effect.** `accusandolo il fatto, l'effetto lo scusi`: the
chapter's two terms, in I.7's and I.8's verb of accusation. Excuse (`scusa`,
`scusare`) returns three times, the last in the chapter's last line; blame
(`biasimo`) and reproof (`riprendere`) are what it is set against.

**Cleomenes is the demonstration, not an ornament.** Agis tried the
reformation without being alone and was killed; Cleomenes made himself alone
by killing the ephors and nearly succeeded. The violence is the point of the
example, and `imperfetto` lands it.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.9

**He grants the thesis outright and then hedges the founder.** `Non è dubio`
— the strongest assent yet, in Machiavelli's own `uno solo` — and praise,
not merely excuse, for the man who reorders a disordered city by violence and
fraud when there is no other way. Then `Ma`: pray it is never needed, because
the founder cannot lay down his authority until time establishes the laws,
and in that time power corrupts. The remedy is a medicine for the hopeless
case. The English must keep the grant whole before the turn, and must not
make him an opponent of the thesis.

**Two degrees of founder, and a suspended judgment.** Supremely laudable is
the one who keeps the authority no longer than the ordering needs (Lycurgus,
and perhaps no other); of the one who keeps it for life, however good his
government, `non so quanto sia da essere laudato` — suspension, not denial —
and yet he is not free of all reproof. The four acts again: assent (`Non è
dubio`), suspension (`non so`), and a judgment deferred.

**On Romulus he refuses to answer yet.** `non dico ora altro`: he will not
take Machiavelli's proof (the senate) until the life is considered, and the
memory he gives — that the senate was suspected of killing Romulus for
arrogating too much authority — turns Machiavelli's proof against him without
asserting it. The English must keep `se bene mi ricordo` and `si dubitò` as
the hedges they are. The unit ends without a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.10

**Judgment and choice come apart, and the case is made in the chooser's own
currency.** Everyone, mad or wise, wicked or good, praises the founders and
blames the destroyers (.01). `Nientedimeno` almost all choose tyranny,
deceived by a false good, `volontariamente o ignorantemente` (.02). The case
against the tyrant is then made by his own measure: fame, glory, honour and
above all security (`sicurtà`, `sicuro`), which he flees and the founder
keeps. The security vocabulary runs from .02 to the last line and lands in
the same English words every time.

**The proof is conditional, and the condition is reading.** `è impossibile
che ... se leggessero l'Istorie ... che non volessero`: men would choose
otherwise if they read the histories and set store by them. The double `che`
keeps the impossibility attached to its condition; the English must not let
it become a flat assertion that men want to be Scipios.

**Caesar's glory is a corrupted witness.** The writers who praise him were
corrupted by his fortune and frightened by the rule that bore his name; what
free writers would say shows only sideways, in Catiline and in the praise of
Brutus. The `tanto ... quanto` proportion is the chapter's one explicit
verdict on Caesar, and it turns the rubric's `Quanto ... tanto`.

**The imperial evidence is a count with its exceptions explained.** Twenty-six
emperors, sixteen killed, ten dead ordinarily; the good who were killed and
the villainous one who was not are accounted for inside the same period, by
`e se ... e se`. An English that lets the exceptions float has turned a
demonstration into a statistic. Inheritance against adoption (.06) carries on
I.9's rule against hereditary authority.

**The two ages are a vision, and it ends in irony.** Predicate-first
absolutes that the prince "will see", kept in that order (D194), close on
`quanti obblighi ... abbia con Cesare`, "how many debts ... to Caesar", an
irony neither Machiavelli nor the translation flags.

**The close raises I.9's stakes, under a condition.** If ordering the city
meant laying down the principate, the prince who did not would merit "some
excuse"; since it does not, `non si merita scusa alcuna`, impersonal and
general. "No excuse whatever" must not be lifted out as a maxim detached from
that condition. Two paths, and the asymmetry of `l'una, che li fa ...` against
`l'altra li fa ...` stays.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.10

**He grants the title and refuses its examples.** `Il titulo ... è
verissimo`, restated with kingdoms first and blame for vituperation; then `Ma
perché e' casi sono varii, e lo autore confonde gli esempli`, and the reply
becomes a distinction of three cases: the man who makes a tyranny in a free
fatherland, the man who inherits one, the legitimate king. Machiavelli's
judgment stands; the mixing of his examples is what is refused. The English
keeps the grant whole before the `Ma`, and does not make him defend tyranny.

**Necessity excuses, and liberty can be the tyranny.** Inside the first case
he opens another: the citizen whom disordered liberties leave no other way to
save himself `merita essere assai scusato`. Justification is denied the
usurper; excuse is given the citizen. Florence is the example, in the first
person: good citizens `la necessità ha condotti` to desire the change of a
state `che sotto nome di libertà è tirannico`. The popular government is the
tyranny; the citizens are not drawn towards tyranny. No note or standfirst may
restate it the other way round.

**Very few, or perhaps none.** The heir merits less blame for keeping the
tyranny and would merit more praise for leaving it, but `pochissimi o forse
nessuno` have left it `sanza necessità`. The causes are hedged (`può`,
`difficile`, `pare`, `communemente`), and `Sanza che` opens a period without a
main clause. Sulla, who seized his power, is the rare example of laying it
down. The phrase returns as `pochissimi e forse nessuno` in the last line;
the `forse` survives both times and in the title.

**Choice answers adoption; grant, suspension and belief stay distinct.**
Machiavelli's proof was adoption; Guicciardini's reform is succession `per
elezione`, his own design in I.2. He grants (`si truova pure qualcuno`),
suspends (`io non so quali`, which is not "there are none"), and believes
(`credo`) that where it has been, necessity kept it more than will.

**The close: lesser reproof, on the case Machiavelli half excused.** `quanto
... tanto`, the rubric's proportion, measures how often private men reason
about these things against how rarely they are done, and `però` draws the
conclusion: those who do not lay down a tyranny or disinherit their posterity
merit `minore reprensione`. That is close to Machiavelli's "some excuse" for
the prince who would have to lay down the principate; what Guicciardini
denies is the condition of Machiavelli's "no excuse whatever", that the
principate can be kept and the city ordered. Reproof, blame and excuse stay
three words. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.11

**A comparison of two founders, decided by belief and not by verdict.** The
concession comes first (`Ancoraché`: Romulus was first, and Rome is his
daughter); then the heavens send Numa because Romulus' orders `non
bastavano`. The weighing (`a quale principe Roma fusse più obbligata, o a
Romolo o a Numa`) is a hypothetical dispute answered with `credo` and
`piuttosto`, and its reason is an asymmetry: where religion is, arms can
readily be introduced; where arms are and not religion, religion only with
difficulty. The English keeps the dispute hypothetical, the belief a belief,
`piuttosto` on Numa (not his preference), and `a Romolo ... a Numa` in
matching words.

**Fear of God is the operative term, and fear of a prince its stand-in for
one life.** `timore di Dio` (.01, .06 twice) and `il timore d'un Principe`
land in the same English, and no other word takes "fear" from them: Numa
"doubted" whether his authority would be enough (D205). The chapter's close
runs on one verb, `manca` ×4, "fails" (D206).

**The oaths are extorted, and they bind anyway.** Scipio's sword and Titus
Manlius' threat force both oaths; the proof is that a forced oath held men
whom neither love of the fatherland nor the laws held. The English neither
softens the coercion nor moralises it.

**Numa simulated.** `simulò di avere congresso con una Ninfa` is stated
flatly and then made general: no orderer of extraordinary laws did without
recourse to God. The instrumental account runs beside the praise, unflagged,
as in I.12.

**The material is men, and the chapter ends on a modern case it will not
judge.** `grossi` and `rozzi` stay two words, and `rozzo` one word for men
and marble (D207). Florence and Savonarola are set beside Numa and the
nymph, and here alone Machiavelli suspends judgment (`Io non voglio
giudicare`), with his reason.

**The chain and its corollary stay attached.** Religion caused good orders,
good orders make good fortune, and from good fortune arose the successes
(past, present, past). The rule that follows is restrictive: the kingdoms
that depend `solo` on one man's *virtù* (D208). Dante's tercet stays
subordinate to `come prudentemente Dante dice`.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.11

**He grants the foundations and suspends the ranking.** `Certo è`: arms
and religion are both principal foundations (on the emended text, D200),
each so necessary that, failing either, `si può dire` the vital parts fail.
Then `ma io non so già se sia vero`, suspension and not denial, on
Machiavelli's own question in Machiavelli's own words; then `anzi io
inclinerei più presto nel contrario`, a conditional inclination, and `mi
pare si possi mostrare`, a demonstration offered as it seems to him.
Certainty, suspension, inclination, seeming: four acts, kept apart in the
text and in the standfirst. An English that makes him prefer Romulus
outright, or deny religion its necessity, has lost the reply.

**Two reasons: one counterfactual, one borrowed.** The first is time: had
Numa been first, the city `era ... oppressa` and Numa `né lasciava` room for
arms, imperfects of what would have been (D47). Hence `Fu adunque a'
principii più necessario Romulo`, which turns Machiavelli's "necessary to
Numa". The second, `come anche dice lo scrittore`, is the first half of
Machiavelli's concession (the times full of religion), extended to the
neighbouring cities; the gross men are left out. The choice of Numa moves
from the heavens and the senate to the people, still `ferocissimo`, who
chose `volontariamente` and were inclined `per sé medesimi`.

**The close leaves the cause open and the order fixed.** `o la prudenza o
la fortuna ..., o l'uno e l'altro insieme`: he does not choose, and neither
does the English. What he fixes is the sequence: the one of war first,
because arms gave the city life enough to wait for Numa. The unit ends with
a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.16

**Two difficulties, one remedy, inside a limit.** The first difficulty is the
people's: like a brute animal nourished in prison and let go, it cannot feed
itself or find its lairs, and falls to the first who would chain it again.
The second is the state's: it makes for itself `partigiani nimici e non
partigiani amici`, stated, argued and restated with `Però` (D218). The
remedy follows in four comparatives (`più potente ... più valido ... più sano
... più necessario`): kill the sons of Brutus. The whole stands inside `ancora
che la materia non sia in tutto corrotta`, and the limit returns in the last
sentence as a counterfactual (D47) that hands the corrupted people to I.17.
An English that lets the remedy or the simile float free of the corruption
condition has lost the chapter; one that softens the violence has too.

**No debt to a government that does no harm.** Free living rewards by rule,
so no one `confessa avere obbligo` for what he thinks he merits, and no one
knows the common utility while he has it, `perché nissuno confesserà mai
avere obbligo con uno che non l'offenda`. `Obbligo` is "debt" both times
(D17); the French kings `obbligati ad infinite leggi` have bound themselves
(O26).

**The digression is declared, and divides the people.** `Vero è che io
giudico` is a first-person concession, not a maxim; `E benché questo discorso
sia disforme` marks the turn to tyrants as a digression. The people's two
desires are met `in tutto` and `in parte`: vengeance by Clearchus' massacre,
liberty by dividing the few who want to command (removed or contented with
honours) from the infinite who want to live secure (contented with laws).
`libertà` and `sicurtà` stay two words, and the few are secured by honours as
well as by removal.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.16

**A distinction Machiavelli does not draw, defended against Rome.** `Io fo
... grandissima differenza`: a people that never knew liberty against one
that lost it by accident. He raises Rome against himself (`Né mi siano
allegati in contrario`) and answers three ways: they changed nothing but the
kings' power, and if those orders were good, it was fortune more than
prudence that they served liberty (`se furono buoni`; the consuls only `si
crede` from Servius); they had felt Tarquinius; and free neighbours make it
`minore maraviglia`. The condition stays a condition.

**The remedy is an order, and it answers the partisan enemies.**
Machiavelli's beneficiaries of the tyranny `non possono vivere contenti`;
Guicciardini's, received and secure, `vi si contentano dentro`, as `accade
molte volte` and as Florence showed. Rigour and restraint stand in one
design: `rigida e inesorabile` against whoever machinates, and no one
persecuted `per semplice sospetto`. Divisions are the danger; 1512 and the
persecution after `26` are his evidence.

**The sons of Brutus are conceded, not wished for, and narrowed.** `se bene in
simili casi è necessario mettere mano nel sangue` stays inside the period;
`sarebbe stato meglio non avere avuto necessità, e che Bruto non avessi
figliuoli` is a preference, not a denial of the remedy. No title, standfirst
or note presents it without the concession.

**The prince: the enmity divided by its root.** From oppression, easily
provided for by just and humane government; from the desire of liberty, as in
Florence, which wanted it `per participare degli onori` (the motive
Machiavelli gave the few), incurable by treatment. `è puerile credere` is
aimed at the motive, not the deed. The close turns `farsi il Popolo amico`
into `farsi partigiani ... potenti a opprimere il populo`, stated as the
remedy the prince has, neither softened nor made the only one. The unit ends
with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.23

**One error, two cases, closed as a ring.** `tutta la fortuna tua, e non
tutte le forze` (.01) returns as `tutta la tua fortuna con parte delle tue
forze` (.03), the possessive given to both nouns only once the danger has
happened; `Cadesi ancora in questo inconveniente` makes the Horatii and the
guarded pass one mistake. The *virtù* of many staked on the *virtù* of three
answers the *virtù* of the residue never tried. The rule keeps its exception
(`se già ... non potessi`) and its hedge (`quasi sempre`, twice; the rubric
says `spesso`), and `tenere` carries the condition: you may hold the place
only if you can hold all your forces there. The second person changes sides
at .04. Hannibal and 1515 are proofs, not maxims about mountains.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.23

**He grants the conclusion and does not believe it reaches the case.**
`ancora che sia verissima`: the rule stands, but the Albans and Romans each
took from the other what they took from themselves, and equal courses `si
possono male riprendere`. The motive is graded (kinship granted, equality `è
credibile`, restraint `pare verisimile`) and the verdict conditional:
`presupposta questa equalità, io non veggo`. The passes become a case for
`buono occhio`; Marignano is a counterfactual under `forse`; the Roman
example is impossible, then turned. The close keeps Machiavelli's fact (few
captains, no longer "very few virtuous") and refuses a cause, in his own
words reversed: to venture part of the forces with all the fortune `non è da
fuggire` when advantages concur; the reason is difficulty, which is close to
Machiavelli's own reasons at .04. No title or standfirst may make him deny
the rule or prescribe the guarding of passes. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.24

**An appearance reversed, a rule, its reason, and the proofs.** Horatius'
trial looks like popular ingratitude (.01); examined better, the people are
to be blamed for absolving him, not for wishing to condemn him (.02). The rule
follows inside the same period (`e la ragione è questa`): no well-ordered
republic ever cancelled demerits with merits; the same man takes reward and
penalty each in full. The reason is a consequence (.03): the citizen whom
merit exempts grows insolent and civility dissolves; the corollary, that even
a poor republic must reward. Neither the reversal nor the rule floats free
as a maxim. The rule's `senza avere riguardo alcuno` (.02) is executed in
Manlius' `senza rispetto alcuno` (.04), and the last period ends on the
Capitol he had saved: merit and penalty in one place, not set against each
other. `ancora che grande` keeps its attachment open.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.24

**He grants the rule and argues the case.** The rule that ill is not to be
compensated with good is conceded by citing the Discourse (`come dice il
Discorso, saria pernizioso`); he claims only a departure from it where `tante
circumstanze` concur, an example for the judge, not for whoever would
compensate indiscriminately. Two accounts of the absolution are set side by
side, each hedged (`Si può dire forse`; `la verità pare che sia`), and `se lei
aveva fallato` stays a condition. He turns Machiavelli's sentence about
blaming the people under `in tal caso` and by degree (`più reprensibile ...
che non fu`): the killing stays `delitto`, and no title, standfirst or note
may make him say that merits cancel faults. `concorrere` comes five times and
carries the cause. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.25

**A rule about seeming, proved by what Rome kept, and bounded at the close.**
The precept is conditional on its end: whoever would have a reformed state
accepted, and maintain it with the satisfaction of each, is necessitated to
retain at least the shadow of the ancient ways. Its reason is the pair `pare`
/ `è`, `pajono` / `sono`, which lands in the same English verbs both times,
and is not to float free as a maxim. The Roman proof keeps two things: the
number (two consuls held to the one king's twelve lictors) and the name (the
`Re sacrificolo`, set under the supreme priest); .03 names number, authority
and time as what may change, and the name as what must stay. `ritenere` and
`desiderare` are one word each throughout: the reformer who desires, and the
people who must not come to desire the kings. The last period fixes the
precept's scope, republic or kingdom against tyranny (I.26); the object-first
`E questo ... debbe osservare colui` is kept for that contrast, and `ordinare`
/ `fare` carry it in English where `potenza` / `potestà` cannot (D278). The
unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.25

**He grants the conclusion and ranks the cases it fits.** `è più necessaria a
chi ... che a chi`: a comparative, not a denial. The hinge is `riformare`
against `mutare`, reform within a form against a change of species, and
`mutare` stays "change" through `mutando la potestà regia`. The Roman
founding, Machiavelli's own example and his own case at .03, is moved into
the class where the rule matters less, for a reason in men's `opinione`, and
the claim is of degree (`sì esattamente`). The examples are `non ... di molto
momento`, not false: he substitutes reasons, superstition for the king of
sacrifices, and for the lictors that insignia of greater power would not have
been borne. `l'uno ... l'altro` cross the order of naming; the singular
`esemplo` takes `sono`. No standfirst or note may make him say the ancient
orders may be discarded, or that appearances do not matter. The unit ends
with a faint stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.26

**A precept for one case, its verdict, and a necessity against the
verdict, each turning on what the prince wills.** The case is fixed in a
first period whose subject `Qualunque` is left hanging and resumed by
`egli`: a new prince who does not turn, `per via di Regno o di Repubblica`,
to civil life (`tanto più` if his foundations are weak raises the degree and
does not narrow the case); I.25's reformer is the other case. For him the
best remedy is to make every thing anew: `fare` and `nuovo` carry the list,
from new governments to the unmaking of cities (`disfare`) and the transfer
of peoples. The list runs in asyndeton through .01 and is joined by `e` in
.02; it turns to the second person at `da te` and ends on Philip as the
mark. Then the verdict, on the same `modi`: most cruel, enemies of every
living, Christian and human; `qualunque uomo` (the chapter's first word
again) must flee them and *wish* rather to live private than be king. Then
`Nondimeno`, in a second period whose subject `colui` is left hanging:
whoever does not wish to take `quella prima via del bene`, `quando si voglia
mantenere`, it is fitting (`conviene`, not "must") that he enter this ill.
`volere` comes three times and is one English verb; `via` comes three times
(.01, the first path, the middle paths) and is one English word, and no note
closes what the "first path" refers to. The precept is conditional on
wishing to maintain oneself and is not to float free as a maxim; neither
the verdict nor the necessity cancels the other. The middle paths close the
chapter: men cannot be wholly good or wholly bad (`tutti` predicative).
`presente capitolo` is kept (D282). The unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.26

**He sorts the case by the people, not the prince, and limits the rule.**
Machiavelli's case is a prince who does not turn to civil life;
Guicciardini's `Sono alcune città o regni` asks what the governed are used
to. Where a change of prince counts for little and the people are not used
to being governed legitimately, a prince who rules `poco politicamente`
(Machiavelli's prince, seen from below) needs no remedies `sì forti`, and
the discontented private man is gained, `pure che` his discontent rests on
his own interest. The difficulty is where the inclination is contrary; there
he divides again. Kingdoms long under one line `si potria sperare di
guadagnare` with good treatment; those whose inclination is liberty require
strong remedies (`bisogna`): the one real concession. `Però` the part that
can be gained with benefits is to be gained, because violent remedies, `se
da uno canto ti assicurano`, make weakness, `massime` for a prince without
his own arms; and `però` the prince must have the spirit for extraordinary
remedies `quando sia necessario` (against Machiavelli's `quando si voglia
mantenere`) and the prudence to lose no occasion of humanity. The close
denies that the rule is `regola assoluta`, not that it is a rule, and turns
on the man (`al quale sempre piacquono sopra modo`), against Machiavelli's
own verdict that these ways are `crudelissimi`; the remedies land last.
`guadagnare` comes four times, against Machiavelli's `fare`; `ricognoscere`
answers his `riconosca da te`. The three `però` are consecutive (D22) and
draw three separate conclusions; they must not be merged or made
adversative. The unit ends with a doubtful stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.28

**A comparison whose verdict moves the blame from the city to necessity, and whose support is a counterfactual and a conjecture.** The cause is a belief (`credo accadesse`), confined to the two cities (`parlando di Roma e di Atene`): ingratitude follows from cause to suspect, and cause to suspect from whether a city has had its liberty taken away by one of its own citizens. Rome had not, from the kings to Sulla and Marius (`alcuno suo cittadino` and the span carry the claim); Athens had, by Pisistratus, under a deceit of goodness, and avenged even the shadow of errors. The maxim of the writers of civility is reported and then endorsed (`è verissimo quello che dicono`): it stays theirs, attached to the Athenian case. The verdict is neither blame nor praise: `ne accuserà solo la necessità`. Then the counterfactual (`se a Roma fusse suta tolta ... non sarebbe stata`), offered to be seen, and the conjecture from Collatinus and Valerius, Rome suspicious and severe in its first times; both stay what they are, and `arebbe usata ... se ... fusse stata ingiuriata` stays counterfactual. `tolta la libertà` comes three times (.02, .03, .04) and is one English phrase; `sospettare`, `sospetto`, `sospettosa` are one root; `verissimo` and `verissima coniettura` land in the same English. `quella` is Athens throughout. `questa Città` (.04) and the attachment of `ne' primi tempi` (.05) are left as open as the Italian leaves them, and no note closes them. The chapter hands the rest of its matter to I.29 and ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.28

**He reverses Machiavelli's counterfactual, keeps his mechanism, and gives it a new cause.** The opening is conditional and hedged (`si potria forse approvare`), not a concession: if Rome had never lost its liberty after the kings. It did, to the Ten, within Machiavelli's span; and at the recovery, when `si suole procedere più atrocemente`, Rome proceeded `umanissimamente e con somma circumspezione`. The rule is his own, granted as what is wont to happen and denied of Rome; it is close to Machiavelli's Athens and his writers' maxim, but no note may make him cite them. `questa discordia` is the difference between the cities, not civil strife at Rome. Then two causes and one belief: `o ... ovvero, come io credo`, the Romans' nature or the diversity of government; the first is not refuted, and `però` is consecutive (D22). The mechanism stays Machiavelli's, less cause to suspect: `è minore causa di sospettare di loro` and, at the close, `vi fu minore ragione di sospettare di loro` are existential and keep his English. What lessens suspicion is no longer an accident but the mixed government: greatness that needs the senate's consent, grave things treated in the senate, the tribunes' authority used at need, tribune against tribune. `battere` comes three times. The hedge sits on the second tribune (`l'arebbe forse fatto`), and the relative `e' quali` keeps its anacoluthon. The last period draws three consequences from the quality of the government, in order, and the veto must not be made the cause of the first. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.29

**A comparison settled by dividing the cause, in which the excuse is graded and Rome is excused only on a condition.** The vice arises from one of two causes, avarice or suspicion, and the chapter keeps them apart to its last sentence. Ingratitude from avarice is an error `che non ha scusa`; ingratitude from suspicion merits `qualche scusa`, and "some excuse" stays set against "no excuse". The mechanism of the second is one chain: the captain's victory brings him reputation `di necessità`; men's nature, `ambiziosa e sospettosa`, cannot set a measure to its fortune; the suspicion born at once in the prince is necessarily increased by the captain's own insolence; so the prince can think only of securing himself, by death or by taking the reputation away. Antonius Primus and Consalvo are its proofs, and their parallel (`gli tolse ... la ubbidienza dello esercito` / `gli levò la ubbidienza delle genti d'arme`) is kept; the landing `È tanto dunque naturale questo sospetto` is reached from them and is not a free maxim. The people's case follows from the prince's (`da quello che non si difende un Principe`). Its errors are made `per` liberty, out of too much love, and are double-edged: ills in a corrupted republic, Caesar its example; goods in an uncorrupted one, through fear of punishment. Neither edge cancels the other. Rome is the least ungrateful, `si può dire` with Scipio its one example; Coriolanus and Camillus are set aside, not counted. Scipio's case is told as suspicion, and Rome's excuse is attached to a condition, `se il Popolo di Roma seguì ... la opinione di Catone`. `usare` is the chapter's one verb for ingratitude and gratitude; `sospettare` keeps I.28's "less cause to suspect". The close is a prospect, `si vedrà come`, its reason deferred, `come di sotto si dirà`, and no note says where it comes. Whose army and peoples are meant at `nel suo esercito, e nei suoi Popoli` is left as open as the Italian leaves it. The unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.29

**He adds two causes, turns the comparison by them, and grants Machiavelli what he can.** The opening is concessive (`Se bene`): avarice and suspicion are granted, ignorance and malignity rooted in envy added. The verdict on the rubric's question is a negated belief (`non credo ... anzi tutto il contrario`) and then a judgment (`a giudicio mio più`). The causes are taken in order, each with its own act. Avarice: a belief under a condition (`se poco ci pecca il populo`), with ingratitude by nature slipped in as a further cause, and `sanza dubio` for the princes' rewards. Suspicion: a belief qualified `per lo ordinario`, with a real concession (`Confesso bene`) that princes' bites are harder. Ignorance and envy: a belief `sanza comparazione`, with the prince's exception kept (`dove ... non sia tale che gli generi sospetto`); `però` is consecutive (D22). The examples are refused for what they show: Mucianus is not the prince; Ferdinand had just suspicion, and Consalvo lived `ricco e onoratissimo`, against Machiavelli's `inonorato`. Rome is allowed fewer examples, because of its ordered government (his cause of I.28), and then more than the Discourse allows, `come di sotto si dirà`. Camillus, whom Machiavelli set aside, comes back; Scipio is moved from suspicion to envy and ignorance under `mi maraviglio`; `dispiacquono agli uomini savii` answers `spiaceva agli uomini savj` nearly in its words, and `santità` answers `riputato santo` in one root. `alieno` opens and closes the reply. The close calls Machiavelli's claim on the uncorrupted republic `molto alieno dalla verità`, with `non ancora` and `qualche volta` kept as his restatement, grants the lesser error (`Confesso bene`), and limits the denial by `quando non s'ha necessità di eleggere`. No note or summary may drop that condition, make him say princes are seldom ungrateful, or make him deny that suspicion excuses any ingratitude. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.30

**Four remedies for four men, unequal by design: a precept the prince may refuse, two ways the captain cannot take, and for the republic an order, not a precept.** The rubric names the rulers and then the men they send; the chapter takes each ruler with his man, prince and captain, then republic and citizen, and the order is kept visible. The prince's remedy removes the cause (`debbe personalmente andare`): when he does not go the glory is another's, and the acquisition seems unusable unless he extinguishes that glory, `e diventare ingrati ed ingiusti`, an infinitive with no clear governor that no note settles; `senza dubbio` the loss is greater than the gain. `o essere ingrato` hangs on `avere a`, not on `per fuggire`. The prince who stays home `o per negligenza o per poca prudenza` gets no precept, and the refusal stays a refusal. The captain gets `una delle due cose`, the only two (`Altre vie non ci sono`): submit at once, or, `quando questo non gli paja di fare`, take the contrary part; the second is set out at greater length than the first, called punishment and not otherwise judged, and its punishment is pre-emptive (`userebbe`). Whose judgment is `giudicando` is left open (D320). Then nature takes both ways away: `non sanno essere né al tutto tristi, né al tutto buoni` takes up I.26 and I.27; will, power and knowledge fail in a fronted catalogue (D263), `sempre interviene`, and the paragraph lands on `sono oppressi`, the rubric's verb, by `ambigui` and `ambiguità`, one root. The republic cannot go; its remedy is Rome's ways, which `nacque dai modi del suo Governo`. Many virtuous men watch one another, `che` without `tanti` (D321), restraint `intanto ... che` (D174), and glory to whoever laid the dictatorship down the sooner. The chain of I.29 runs backwards: no suspicion, no ingratitude. No emperor or dictator is named, and no note names one for him. Two `debbe` close it, the republic to govern itself `come Roma`, with no verb supplied (D242), the citizen to observe the `termini` observed by Roman citizens. `quelli suoi morsi` is left as open as the Italian leaves it, and the citizen's part is one clause. `fuggire`, `modi`, `usare`, `cagione` and `morsi` each keep one English word. The unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.30

**He praises the remedy, limits where it is needed, and answers with the practice of his own princes.** One period in four members, answering the prince's part only; the captain, the republic, Rome and the citizen are passed over, except as `questi sinistri`, and no note or summary may supply his view of them. The opening is praise (`Io laudo`), not a concession: the remedy is Machiavelli's, the two reasons are Guicciardini's own, the expeditions `procedono con altra reputazione` and the prince `altrimenti è servito`, and both look to the conduct of the war, not to glory, suspicion or ingratitude; `altra` and `altrimenti ... che` stay comparative in the translation. Then a belief hedged inside itself (`credo ... forse necessario`): `forse` sits on `necessario` only, `credo` runs over the semicolon into `ma di poco frutto`, and the verb is carried over. The `ricordo del Discorso` is the advice of the first sentence, set against Machiavelli's own `non ho che precetto`. The division is the tyrant, or whoever `non abbia bene fermo lo Stato suo`, against `uno re grande e naturale`; `bene` stays. The evidence is what `vediamo tutto dì`, `lo esemplo de' principi nostri`, and whose princes is not said. The last member is concessive: `se bene communemente fanno le guerre per capitani` admits the practice Machiavelli put down to negligence or little prudence, without his words for it, and `non gli accade però` is consecutive (D22). The negation is corrected downward, `o rarissime volte`, and both stay. `e' quali` keeps its anacoluthon, resumed by `gli`, and `uno di questi sinistri` holds the landing (D18). The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.32

**A concession outweighed by a prohibition in the same period, an exception explained so that it cannot be copied, and a precept drawn from its rarity.** The first period is one: `Ancora che` grants the Roman example (liberality to the people when Porsena came, the salt gabelles and every burden lifted, the senate's saying that the poor did enough, and, on the reading the grammar favours, the people's bearing siege, hunger and war for it), and `non sia però alcuno` refuses it as a model; `però` is consecutive (D22), not on that account, never "however". The refusal has two grounds in two `perché`, never succeeding and the generality's judgment, and the person narrows as it goes, `alcuno`, `gli`, `te`, the second person holding to the period's end. The generality will credit `quel bene` to the adversaries and, fearing that it will be taken back once the necessity passes, will have no `obbligo` with you: the period lands on the debt that is not created. .02 says why the Romans could do it: a new state, not yet firm; laws already made for the people, the appeal its example; the kings' injuries fresh. It turns .01's judgment round in its own words: the people could persuade itself that `quel bene` came `non ... tanto` from the coming of the enemies `quanto` from the senate's disposition, and `quel bene` lands in one English phrase both times. The exception is closed by rarity: `simili cagioni` / `simili rimedj`, `rade volte` twice; .01's `mai` and .02's `rade volte` are left as they stand. Then `Però` the precept, for `qualunque tiene Stato`, republic or prince: consider beforehand the contrary times and the men that will be needed, and live with them now as one judges one would be necessitated to live then. The precept rests on foresight and is not to float free as a maxim. Its negative case closes the chapter: whoever governs himself otherwise, `massime un Principe` (no reason is given, and no note supplies one), and later believes he will regain men with benefits when the danger supervenes, deceives himself, and `non solamente ... ma` accelerates his ruin; the ruin is his who governed otherwise, not the lateness of every benefit. The benefit family (`beneficare`, `beneficio`, `benefizj`), `sopravvenire` (three times), `riuscire` (twice), `guadagnarsi` / `riguadagnarsi` and `necessità` / `necessitato` / `necessitadi` each keep one English root; `avversarj` and `nimici` are kept apart. The unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.32

**He divides Machiavelli's men by what they already are to the giver, grants the precept in his own degree, and withholds blame from the man who comes late.** `Altro è ..., altro è`: the man who `per lo ordinario` is a friend, to be made more one, against the man `totalmente` an enemy, to be gained; one period, the opposition held with "it is" twice (D121), both members `cercare di`, and `per lo ordinario` and `totalmente` stay on the men they qualify. The first is easier, `come intervenne a' Romani`, the second `difficilissimo`, joined by a comma and no conjunction (D331); no note supplies the reason Rome was the easy case. Then the real concession, `e non dimeno nel primo ancora`: even in the easier case it is `sanza comparazione più utile` to have done it `innanzi al bisogno`, part of Machiavelli's precept granted as a comparison, not restated as his rule. `Ma` turns, `nell'uno caso e l'altro`, to both cases: `non biasimo` is a withholding of blame, not praise and not absolution, its object `è stato imprudente`, and it holds only under `se condotto alla necessità`. The relative `il quale` carries the reason after the semicolon, concessive (`se bene`): little hope of helping, which concedes Machiavelli's conclusion (`rade volte ... giovino`) and not his ground; no danger of doing harm, set against his `accelera la sua rovina` without engaging his reasons. `remedio`, `giovare`, `bisogno`, `guadagnarsi`, `necessità` keep Machiavelli's English. The persons pass from the impersonal `farsi`, `guadagnarsi` to `ti`. No summary or note may drop `per lo ordinario`, the imprudence or the condition, or make him recommend the late remedy: he says only that it does no harm. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.39

**A premise of sameness, made useful by diligent examination, and two cases, the modern told first, in which a people misjudges the cause of its ill and has to undo its remedy.** The first period claims that the same desires and humours are in all cities and peoples and always were; the second makes foresight `facil cosa` for `chi esamina con diligenza`, and grounds the remedies, used or new, `per la similitudine degli accidenti`, whose scope (the remedies, perhaps the foresight) stays open. The third lays the recurring scandals on readers and governors, kept apart: neglected, or not understood by whoever reads, or, understood, not known by whoever governs; `intendere` and `conoscere` stay two verbs, and `sempre ... in ogni tempo` keeps its redundancy. Florence after [14]94: the chain war, spending, burdens, complaints runs in colons and is one period, and the spending has its cause, the occupiers' power. The generality's grievance is a belief (`fusse cagione`, `cominciò a persuadersi`), and the identity it believes in (`tolto via detto Magistrato, fusse tolto via la guerra`) must not become Machiavelli's claim. The remedy is an omission, and its result reverses the belief: `non solamente non levò la guerra ... ma` disorder, Arezzo lost besides Pisa. The fever and the physician are the people's own recognition, and the magistracy is remade. Rome: `Questo medesimo umore` makes the two one case. The misattribution is set as `dove e' dovevano pensare ... pensavano`, an unmet obligation against what was thought, and the nobles' design, to lead the plebs out of the city where it had no aid, is what the people thought; no note may assert it. Terentilius' proposal is a proposal (`dovessero considerare ... e limitarla`). Who was content with the consular tribunes is not said. The paragraph lands on `il nome` against `l'autorità` (D18). The close sets the cases side by side with `come ... così`, and `conosciuto l'error loro` closes the chain of `conoscere` that opened the chapter. `medesimo`, `umore`, `nascere`, `togliere via`, `persuadersi`, `nome` and `error` each keep one English word; `rifare` and `ricreare` are kept apart; `Imperio` is "empire" in .02 and "command" in .04 (D124); `dovere` is "should" and the bare subjunctive is not (D348). No note may date the Ten's lapse or Arezzo's loss for him. The unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.39

**He keeps the Florentine example by giving the popular opinion a cause in the office, and refuses the Roman one as not similar.** The opening is a negated belief (`Io non credo ... al tutto sanza ragione`): neither a concession to Machiavelli nor a defence of the people's decision. The complaint had some reason, because the Ten, under orders made in another species of government, had more authority than a well-ordered liberty bears. The catalogue of their powers runs in asyndeton and is kept, with `generalmente` on their authority in war. The popular opinions arose from that excess `in buona parte`, his limit on the cause; of the cause of the war, and of Machiavelli's fever and physician, he says nothing. Then experience shows both clauses under `se bene ... anche`: the excess was pernicious, Machiavelli's word for the lapse turned on the authority, and lacking such a magistracy `ne' tempi difficili` was most damaging. The remedy is limitation, not abolition: the Ten created anew, their authority limited in the things `giudicate` dangerous by the participation of the Eighty, and the name changed from Balìa to Liberty and Peace; the reason for the old name is given and the new is left to the name. `creare` and `deliberazione` answer Machiavelli's `ricrearono` and `La qual deliberazione`. In .02 `Non è simile` denies the likeness of the two examples, but he does not take up the chapter's premise of .01, and no note may make him deny that the same humours recur. The consuls' authority is limited three ways, appeal, intercession, the senate's views, all `quando non erano nelle espedizioni`; the field, where Machiavelli's people feared them, is left unjudged. `però` is consecutive (D22) and draws only `manco cagione di moderarla`; `anzi` adds the sedition, and the subject of `era moto` is unstated. `Donde nacque` draws the consequence, with the concessive kept (`ancora che ... la plebe potessi assai`): the moderate authority defended the patrician magistrates (`li`, D346) more readily. `assoluta`, `troppa autorità` / `non troppa` and `moderare` / `moderata` each keep one English word, and the reply ends on the measure the Ten lacked. No summary or note may make him say the Florentines were right, that the Ten should not have been restored, or that the consuls' authority in the field was moderate. The unit ends with a stop.

## Machiavelli, NM-DISC.1.40

**A story told so that it can be disputed: the decemvirate from its agreed creation to its fall (.02-.06), then the errors it shows on both sides (.07-.10), to two readers at once, those who would keep a republic free and those who should design to subject it.** The first period sets out the plan (`narrare prima ... e dipoi disputare`) and the double audience, the second in the subjunctive (`disegnassero`); the errors are many on each side, the senate's and the plebs' against liberty, Appius' against the tyranny he `si aveva presupposto` to establish. The narrative is Livy's, told without verdicts but the one adverb of the first year (`assai civilmente`); the Latin is left as printed, and the verbless `Perché subito Appio:` keeps its colon. Appius' change of nature is how it seemed (`pareva maraviglia`). The second creation comes by the companions' art turned against them, and the senate's refusal to show its authority is from envy of the plebs, with its reasoning (`che se ... che potesse essere`) kept resumptive. The dispute opens on the cause, two excesses of desire, the people's to be free and the nobles' to command; then a general rule with two conditions, a people that gives reputation to one so that he may beat those it hates, `e che quello uno sia savio`, the second kept inside the `quando` and not made a gloss on Appius. The rule's futures (`attenderà`, `non si volterà`, `arà spenta`) and its subjunctive of result (`non abbia dove rifuggire`) are the tyrant's method, and the counterfactual (`se questo modo avesse tenuto Appio ... arebbe preso`) turns it on him. In .08 the antithesis of friends and enemies runs in four members with `amici` three times and the witness's semicolon; the `che` after `più imprudentemente` is causal or comparative and no note may claim both. The maxim on violence is the second of two reasons (`e per le ragioni dette di sopra, e perché`) and is not lifted out. In .09 tyranny has two degrees; the three outside forces move from a noun to two infinitives in asyndeton, the second person enters with `ti` and stays, Nabis is the example of the first degree, and Appius fails the second twice over, by circumstance (`non poteva`) and by not knowing how (`non seppe`). .10 returns to the senate and people: the concession to the discourse on the dictator (`ancoraché ... nondimeno`) qualifies that rule without withdrawing it, the guard is removed by the same two longings, and the Ferdinand saying explains the blindness (`Perché`). The close narrows the opening, from many errors of senate and plebs (.01) and very great errors of senate and people (.10) to `l'errore del Popolo romano`, singular, against `gli errori di Appio`, with `volendo` twice. `creare` / `creazione`, `errore`, `spegnere`, `battere`, `rifare`, `ridurre`, `conoscere` and `concorrere` (.08, .10) each keep one English word; `Popolo`, `Plebe`, `Nobili` / `Nobiltà`, `Grandi` and `l'universale` are kept apart, as are `sagace` (.02) and `savio` (.07); `proporre` keeps its three uses distinct. No note may resolve `sendo loro giudici`, identify Ferdinand beyond names.md, correct Machiavelli's Volsci, or say that Appius was not `savio`. The unit ends with a stop.

## Guicciardini, FG-CONS.1.40

**He is persuaded that the principal error was the attempt itself, refuses Machiavelli's counterfactual with a negated belief, and answers the general doctrine with the refusal of a firm rule.** The reply opens with a conviction (`Io mi persuado`) set against the error it judges (`il persuadersi`), one verb and one English word, kept apart from `non credo` in the same period and from `credo`'s "believes" in any summary. The error is none of Machiavelli's (not the turn from the people of Machiavelli .08) but the persuasion that a tyranny could be founded `in quelli tempi` in a Rome then ordered with very good laws, most holy customs, most ardent for liberty, and hard to do violence to because its people was military; `in quelli tempi` and `allora` limit the claim to that Rome, and no summary may make tyranny there impossible at all: Sulla's comes in .02. `però` is consecutive (D22): the tyranny lasted while the pretext of finishing the laws held. Which `accidente` destroyed it is not said. `non credo ... se bene si fussino vòlti a battere col favore della plebe la nobiltà` answers Machiavelli's `se questo modo avesse tenuto Appio` (Machiavelli .07) in his own `volgersi` and `battere`; the supposition is contrary to fact, and the English does not report the turn as having happened (D363). The ground is the people's love of the name of liberty, its double intensive kept (`troppo amicissimo`). Manlius is an example (`si vede lo esemplo`), not the ground: against the senate, with merely popular art, put down `subito che fu fatto capace`. In .02 both examples are tyrannies made with the nobles: Sulla stood on them, and the Duke of Athens fell because he did not know how to keep the nobles who made him (`e' quali` the object of `mantenere`). Each side has its reasons, in parallel (`chi ha ... chi ha`): numbers and tolerance of greatness for the people, against a foundation `più nervoso, più efficace e più gagliardo`, `più` three times, and constancy `sì facilmente e spesso` for the nobility; `nondimeno` weighs the second without settling it. `Sono partiti che non si possono pigliare con una regola ferma` refuses a rule, not the question, and the conclusion is to be drawn from the humours of `quella città`, any city in question, from the being of things that varies, and from `occorrenze`, the *currere* family's last word in the reply. He does not say Machiavelli's rule is false, and no summary may make him prefer the nobility. The unit ends with a stop.

## The pair

Machiavelli argues towards a demonstration. Guicciardini declines to be
demonstrated to. Read side by side, that is the book. Every chunk is checked
against whether it still reads that way.
