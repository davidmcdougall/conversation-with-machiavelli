# Introduction

In a letter dated 10 December 1513, Niccolò Machiavelli told his friend Francesco Vettori how he was spending his days. He had lost his office in Florence the year before and was living in the country. The mornings went on the wood he was having cut, the afternoons on the inn by the road and on games of cards with the innkeeper, a butcher and a miller. Then the evening:

> la sera mi ritorno a casa, ed entro nel mio Scrittojo; ed in sull'uscio mi spoglio quella veste contadina, piena di fango e di loto, e mi metto panni reali e curiali, e rivestito condecentemente entro nelle antiche corti degli antichi uomini, dove da loro ricevuto amorevolmente mi pasco di quel cibo, che solum è mio, e che io nacqui per lui; dove io non mi vergogno parlare con loro, e domandare della ragione delle loro azioni; e quelli per loro umanità mi rispondono

At the door of his study he takes off the muddy clothes of the day, puts on "royal and curial garments", and enters "the ancient courts of ancient men". There he feeds on the one food that is his, the food he was born for; he is not ashamed to speak with them and ask them the reason for their actions; and "they, for their humanity, answer me".

The letter goes on to announce a little work on principalities. The *Discorsi sopra la prima Deca di Tito Livio*, the *Discourses on the First Decade of Livy*, written in the years that followed, carry on the same kind of conversation at length: the ancients answer, and Machiavelli answers back. Chapter 58 of Book I opens with Livy's verdict that nothing is more vain and inconstant than the multitude, gives it in Livy's Latin, *e le parole dell'autore sono queste*, "and the words of the author are these", and then sets out to overturn it. "I do not know whether I shall take upon myself a hard province," Machiavelli writes, "… wishing to defend a thing which, as I have said, is accused by all the writers."

About 1530, Francesco Guicciardini took the *Discorsi* chapter by chapter and did to Machiavelli what Machiavelli had done to Livy. His reply to that chapter begins: "A difficult enterprise, and very alien from the opinion of men, he takes up, without doubt, who attributes to the people constancy and prudence." Machiavelli's hard province has become Guicciardini's difficult enterprise, and the author who wrote against all the writers is now the author written against.

But Machiavelli had been dead since June 1527. Livy, in the study at evening, answered; Machiavelli does not. The *Discorsi* were not yet printed when Guicciardini read them, and his *Considerazioni* stayed unprinted for more than three centuries, until 1857. Machiavelli never read a word of them.

So the conversation of the title is the edition's framing, not Guicciardini's. He wrote notes on a book, not letters to a friend, and nothing in them expects a reply. The title borrows the conceit of the 1513 letter and withholds its consolation: Guicciardini does to Machiavelli what Machiavelli did to Livy, and is denied the answer Machiavelli claimed. That asymmetry is why the edition is called what it is. What it contains is the disagreement itself, which is real, and political.

## Two Florentines

Machiavelli was born in 1469, Guicciardini in 1483. The fourteen years mattered less than the difference in station. Machiavelli was a functionary: secretary to the Second Chancery of the republic from 1498, sent on embassies without an ambassador's rank, the man who built the republic's militia. When the Medici returned behind a Spanish army in 1512 he lost his office, and he never held a comparable one again. Guicciardini came from one of the great families of the city. He trained in law, went to Spain as ambassador before he was thirty, and then spent more than a decade in the service of the Medici popes: governor of Modena and Reggio under Leo X, president of the Romagna and lieutenant-general of the papal army under Clement VII.

The servant of the republic and the servant of the popes became friends in the 1520s, when Machiavelli was finding his way back into employment and Guicciardini was governing papal provinces. They wrote to each other, joked, and argued about soldiers; in the war of 1526 Machiavelli was sent to the papal camp, where Guicciardini was the pope's lieutenant. Then the year 1527 took both men's footing away. Rome was sacked in May, the Medici were driven from Florence, and the restored republic had no use for either of them. Machiavelli died in June. Guicciardini, a Medici man in a city that now proceeded against Medici men, left Florence and was declared a rebel.

He wrote the *Considerazioni* in that enforced leisure. At least one reply was written during the siege of Florence, which began in the autumn of 1529: on I.10 he speaks of citizens persecuted "at the coming of the prince of Orange", the imperial commander, and of "a state that under the name of liberty is tyrannical", in the present tense. He was reading a dead friend's unpublished book, in exile from the city both had served, and the book came close to home. In I.8 Machiavelli's Florentine example of calumny is messer Giovanni Guicciardini, Francesco's own kinsman. The reply grants the conclusion and treats the evidence with contempt: the example of Cosimo de' Medici "is a dream", and "because this matter, for proving the conclusion of the Discourse, is narrow of examples, that of messer Giovanni Guicciardini was begged."

## The *Considerazioni*

The *Considerazioni* are thirty-nine replies, each headed with the number and rubric of the chapter it answers. They range from 66 words (on I.14) to more than 1,200 (on I.2, I.16 and I.29), and come to nearly nineteen thousand words of Italian in all, set against some thirty-five thousand of Machiavelli's. They are unrevised working notes, not a treatise, and say so: "I have at another time written more largely, therefore I shall now pass through it with brevity" (I.4), where the other time is his own *Dialogo del reggimento di Firenze*.

He never names Machiavelli. The man he is answering is *lo Scrittore*, the Writer; *lo Autore*, the author, as Livy was the author to Machiavelli; or simply *il Discorso*, the Discourse, which "does not remember" (I.60) and which he marvels "should excuse the case of Scipio" (I.29). These are ordinary ways of citing a book. They also mean that the argument is with the text, and only through it with the man.

The manner is consistent. About a third of the replies open by granting something. "It is a true conclusion that calumnies are detestable" (I.8). "The title of this Discourse is most true" (I.10). "The conclusion is most true, that the ancient times are often praised more than is due" (II.proem). Then comes the turn. The grant is usually sincere, and the turn is usually against the reasons rather than the conclusion: Guicciardini will accept where Machiavelli arrives and dispute how he got there, his examples, his Rome, his rules. When he refuses outright he refuses with an image. To praise Rome's disunions, as Machiavelli does in I.4, "is like praising in a sick man the sickness, for the goodness of the remedy that has been applied to him." The argument that fortresses make princes insolent "is very frivolous" (II.24).

Behind the manner is a quarrel about how liberty is made and who can be trusted with it, and Machiavelli's side of it is strong. In I.4 he argues that those who condemn Rome's tumults "blame those things that were the first cause of keeping Rome free", attending to the noise rather than the effects, and that "all the laws that are made in favour of liberty arise from their disunion": three centuries of conflict between people and nobles cost Rome a handful of exiles and very little blood, and produced the orders that kept it free. In I.58 he defends the people against every historian, Livy included. A people bound by laws, he concludes, is as prudent and as constant as a prince, and more so; unbound, each is as bad as the other, for "a prince who can do what he wishes is mad, a people that can do what it wishes is not wise."

Guicciardini contests all of this, and three objections recur. The first is that Rome is not a model. Its military discipline was excellent, he grants in I.4, and "its *virtù* sustained all the other defects of the government"; but he does not believe its orders "were such that whoever were to order a republic ought to take them for an example." He returns to it in I.49, "against the opinion of the Writer", and generalises it in II.24: "One must not praise antiquity so much that a man blame all the modern orders that were not in use among the Romans." The second is that history does not yield rules so easily: "the cases are various, and the author confounds the examples" (I.10). The third is the people. On I.58 he calls a people by itself "an ark of ignorance and of confusion", and says that merely popular governments have everywhere been short-lived.

None of this was academic in 1530. Whether conflict between classes is a republic's engine or its disease, whether a popular government can judge, and whether the Roman past gives a city rules it can follow were the questions on which Florence had been fighting with itself since 1494, and on which it was now fighting for its life. Machiavelli had served its popular republic; Guicciardini had been declared a rebel by it.

The disagreement is conducted in a shared vocabulary, and the English makes one deliberate choice to keep it visible. Machiavelli, in I.12, wishes "to discourse against" the Church of Rome "those reasons which occur to me", and the first is that through the evil examples of its court Italy has lost all religion. Guicciardini begins his reply with worse than Machiavelli said, "One cannot speak so much ill of the Roman court that it does not deserve that more be said", and then refuses the political conclusion: "I do not readily concur" that the Church's opposition to monarchy has been Italy's misfortune. *Concorrere* could be rendered "agree" or "be persuaded". The translation uses "concur" throughout, so that a reader can see Guicciardini answer in the same family of words, discourse, occur and concur, that Machiavelli used to set out his case.

## What he answered

Thirty-nine replies survive. Guicciardini answered every chapter of Book I from the first to the twelfth. After that he answered sixteen of the remaining forty-eight in Book I; in Book II, the proem and seven of thirty-three chapters; in Book III, three of forty-nine. The last answers III.24, halfway through the last book. Whether he meant to answer more is not known; he never says why he chose what he chose, and this edition does not guess.

The pattern can be described without being explained. The opening run covers Rome's founding, its mixed government, the tribunes, the tumults, accusations and calumnies, the founders of states and of tyrannies, and religion. After it the replies come in clusters: on a people newly free (I.16, I.25, I.26); on ingratitude to those who serve (I.28, I.29, I.30, I.32); on the judgment of the multitude (I.47, I.58); in Book II, on money, war and weak states (II.10, II.12–15) and on fortresses (II.24); in Book III, on whom to trust with office and how to govern men (III.17, III.19). Even the clusters have gaps, and length does not follow importance in any obvious way. The longest reply of all, on I.16, is the one in which Florence and its liberty come most directly into the argument.

The chapters he did not answer are not translated here; this is an edition of the conversation, not of the *Discorsi*.

## Reading a pair

Each pair is one page. Machiavelli's chapter comes first, under its rubric, then Guicciardini's reply. The Italian and the English face each other paragraph by paragraph, and the buttons at the head of the text show the English, the Italian, or both.

The number in the margin of each paragraph, such as 1.4.02, is its address: book, chapter, paragraph. Where the old printing sets a chapter as one unbroken block, the paragraph divisions are the edition's own; the source note at the foot of each pair says which. The addresses are stable, so they can be cited.

At the head of the page is an editor's summary of both texts. Under each paragraph is a short commentary, in three kinds: Argument, which says what the paragraph claims; History, which identifies the people and events; and Cross-reference, which links the chapter and the reply at the point where one meets the other. A link marked "Guicciardini .02" goes to the paragraph of the reply that answers this one; "Machiavelli .02" goes back. At the foot are the variants, where the two printings of each text differ, and the notes, including the translator's.

A first reading can take the chapter whole and then the reply, as Guicciardini did; a second can start with the reply and see what he took hold of. The commentary is there to be skipped.

The English is close to the Italian on purpose. It keeps the order of the argument where English will bear it, renders a word the same way each time it carries weight, and leaves *virtù* in Italian because no English word holds it. The cost is English that is sometimes stiff. The gain is that a reader with a little Italian can follow each phrase across the page, and that a word carrying the argument in one text can be found again in the other. It is an AI-assisted translation edited by David McDougall. Machiavelli's chapters are given from the Milan edition of 1824, because a complete scan of it is freely available and legible; a future edition may rely on the first printing, Blado's of 1531. How the translation was made and the Italian established is on the About page.

Machiavelli asked the ancients the reason for their actions and reported that they answered him. Guicciardini asked Machiavelli for his, chapter by chapter, with no answer to expect. No edition can change that. What this one does is set each reply beside the passage it answers, so that a reader can weigh the two arguments together, and trace every word of both to the page it came from.
