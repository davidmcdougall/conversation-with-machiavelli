# Discourses II.24

## NM-DISC.2.24.01

It will perhaps seem to these wise [men] of our times a thing not well considered that the Romans, in wishing to secure themselves of the peoples of Latium and of the city of Privernum, did not think of building there some fortress which should be a rein to hold them in faith; it being, above all, a saying in Florence, cited by our wise [men], that Pisa and the other similar cities must be held with fortresses. And truly, if the Romans had been made like them, they would have thought of building them; but because they were of another *virtù*, of another judgment, of another power, they did not build them. And while Rome lived free, and while it followed its orders and its virtuous constitutions, it never built one to hold either cities or provinces, but it did save some of those built.

## NM-DISC.2.24.02

Whence, having seen the way of proceeding of the Romans in this part, and that of the princes of our times, it seems to me [a thing] to put into consideration whether it is good to build fortresses, and whether they do damage or advantage to him who builds them. One must, then, consider that fortresses are made either to defend oneself from enemies or to defend oneself from subjects. In the first case they are not necessary; in the second, damaging.

## NM-DISC.2.24.03

And beginning to render reason why in the second case they are damaging, I say that that prince or that republic which has fear of its subjects and of their rebellion, first it is fitting that such fear arise from hatred that its subjects have toward it; the hatred from its ill carriages; the ill carriages arise either from being able to believe [it] holds them with force, or from little prudence in whoever governs them; and one of the things that makes [it] believe [it] can force them is having the fortresses upon them: because the ill treatments, which are the cause of the hatred, arise in good part from that prince or that republic having the fortresses, which, when this is true, are by far more harmful than useful. Because, first, as has been said, they make you more audacious and more violent toward the subjects; afterwards, there is not that security within that you persuade yourself of; because all the forces, all the violences that are used to hold a people, are nothing, except two: either that you always have a good army to put into the open country, as the Romans had, or that you scatter, extinguish, disorder and disjoin them in such a way that they cannot convene to offend you; because if you impoverish them, *spoliatis arma supersunt*; if you disarm them, *furor arma ministrat*. If you kill the heads and follow on injuring the others, the heads are reborn, like those of the Hydra. If you make fortresses, they are useful in times of peace, because they give you more spirit to do them ill; but in times of war they are most useless, because they are assailed by the enemy and by the subjects, nor is it possible that they make resistance to the one and to the other. And if ever they were unuseful, they are [so] in our times, in respect of the artillery, through the fury of which small places, and [places] where one cannot withdraw with ramparts, it is impossible to defend, as we discoursed above.

## NM-DISC.2.24.04

I wish to dispute this matter more minutely. Either you, prince, wish with these fortresses to hold the people of your city reined in; or you, prince, or you, republic, wish to rein in a city occupied by war. I wish to turn to the prince, and I say to him that such a fortress, to hold his citizens reined in, cannot be more useless than it is, for the causes said above; because it makes you more prompt and less respectful in oppressing them, and that oppression makes them so exposed to [working] your ruin, and kindles them in such a way, that that fortress which is the cause of it cannot later defend you. Insomuch that a wise and good prince, to maintain himself good, so as not to give cause or daring to his sons to become wicked, will never make a fortress, in order that they may found themselves not upon the fortress but upon the benevolence of men.

## NM-DISC.2.24.05

And if Count Francesco Sforza, having become Duke of Milan, was deemed wise, and nonetheless made a fortress in Milan, I say that in this case he was not wise, and the effect has demonstrated how such a fortress was to the damage and not to the security of his heirs: because, judging that by means of it they could live secure and could offend their citizens and subjects, they spared no generation of violence; so that, having become odious beyond measure, they lost that state as soon as the enemy assailed them; nor did that fortress defend them, nor did it do them any advantage in the war, and in the peace it had done them much damage; because if they had not had it, and if through little prudence they had handled their citizens sourly, they would have uncovered the danger sooner and would have withdrawn from it, and would later have been able to resist the French onset more spiritedly with the subjects [as] friends without a fortress than with them [as] enemies with the fortress: which do not help you in any part; because either they are lost through the fraud of whoever guards them, or through the violence of whoever assails them, or through hunger.

## NM-DISC.2.24.06

And if you wish that they help you and aid you to recover a lost state, where the fortress alone has remained to you, it is fitting for you to have an army with which you can assail him who has driven you out; and when you have this army, you would have the state again in any case, even if the fortress were not there; and so much the more readily as men would be more friends to you than they were, [you] having ill-treated them through the haughtiness of the fortress. And by experience it has been seen how this fortress of Milan, neither to the Sforza nor to the French, in the adverse times of the one and of the other, has done any advantage to any of them; on the contrary, to all it has brought many damages and ruins, [they] not having thought, by means of it, of a more honest way of holding that state.

## NM-DISC.2.24.07

Guido Ubaldo, Duke of Urbino, son of Federigo, who was in his times so esteemed a captain, being driven out of the state by Cesare Borgia, son of Pope Alexander VI, when afterwards through an accident that arose he returned there, had all the fortresses that were in that province ruined, judging them damaging. Because, he being loved by men, in respect of them he did not want them; and on account of the enemies, he saw he could not defend them, they having need of an army in the open country to defend them; so that he turned to ruining them. Pope Julius, the Bentivogli driven out of Bologna, made a fortress in that city; and afterwards had that people despoiled, [it] being ill-treated by a governor of his; so that that people rebelled, and at once [he] lost the fortress; and so the fortress did not help him, and it offended him, whereas, [he] carrying himself otherwise, it would have helped him. Niccolò da Castello, father of the Vitelli, having returned to his fatherland, whence he was banished, at once unmade two fortresses that Pope Sixtus IV had built there, judging that not the fortress but the benevolence of the people was to hold him in that state.

## NM-DISC.2.24.08

But of all the other examples, the freshest, the most notable in every part, and apt to show the inutility of building them and the utility of unmaking them, is that of Genoa, which ensued in the nearest times. Each one knows how in 1507 Genoa rebelled from Louis XII, King of France, who came personally and with all his forces to reacquire it; and, having recovered it, he made a fortress the strongest of all the others of which at present one has notice; because it was, for site and for every other circumstance, inexpugnable, set upon a point of a hill, which stretches into the sea, [a hill] called by the Genoese Codefa; and for this it beat all the port and a great part of the town of Genoa. It occurred later, in 1512, that, the French troops being driven out of Italy, Genoa, notwithstanding the fortress, rebelled; and took the state of it Ottaviano Fregoso, who with every industry, within the term of sixteen months, expugned it [the fortress] by hunger. And each one believed, and by many he was counselled, that he should conserve it for his refuge in every accident; but he, as most prudent, knowing that not fortresses but the will of men maintained princes in state, ruined it. And so, without founding his state upon the fortress, but upon his *virtù* and prudence, he has held it and holds it. And where, to vary the state of Genoa, a thousand foot used to be enough, his adversaries have assailed him with ten thousand and have not been able to offend him. It is seen, then, by this, how unmaking the fortress has not offended Ottaviano, and making it did not defend the King of France. Because when he could come into Italy with the army, he could recover Genoa, not having a fortress there; but when he could not come into Italy with the army, he could not hold Genoa, having the fortress there. It was, then, of expense to the king to make it, and shameful to lose it; to Ottaviano glorious to reacquire it, and useful to ruin it.

## NM-DISC.2.24.09

But let us come to the republics that make fortresses not in the fatherland but in the towns that they acquire. And to show this fallacy, if the example said of France and of Genoa were not enough, I wish Florence and Pisa to be enough for me, where the Florentines made the fortresses to hold that city, and did not know that a city always enemy to the Florentine name, that had lived free, and that has liberty for refuge in rebellion, it was necessary, wishing to hold it, to observe the Roman way: either to make it a companion for themselves or to unmake it; because the *virtù* of the fortresses was seen at the coming of King Charles, to whom they gave themselves up, either through the little faith of whoever guarded them or through fear of a greater ill; where, if they had not been, the Florentines would not have founded the being able to hold Pisa upon them, and that king could not by that path have deprived the Florentines of that city; and the ways with which it had been maintained until that time would perchance have been sufficient to conserve it; and without doubt they would not have made worse proof than the fortresses.

## NM-DISC.2.24.10

I conclude, then, that to hold one's own fatherland the fortress is damaging; to hold the towns that are acquired, fortresses are useless; and I wish the authority of the Romans to be enough for me, who, in the towns that they wished to hold with violence, unwalled and did not wall.

## NM-DISC.2.24.11

And whoever should cite to me against this opinion, in ancient times Tarentum, and in modern ones Brescia, which places were recovered from the rebellion of the subjects by means of the fortresses, I answer that for the recovery of Tarentum, at the end of a year, Fabius Maximus was sent with the whole army, which would have been apt to recover it even if the fortress had not been there; and if Fabius used that path, if it had not been there, he would have used another that would have made the same effect. And I do not know of what utility a fortress is which, to render you the town, has need, for its recovery, of a consular army and of a Fabius Maximus for captain. And that the Romans would have retaken it in any case was seen by the example of Capua, where there was no fortress, and by the *virtù* of the army they reacquired it.

## NM-DISC.2.24.12

But let us come to Brescia. I say that seldom does that occur which has occurred in that rebellion: that the fortress which remains in your forces, the town having rebelled, has an army in force and near, as was that of the French; because, Monsignor de Foix, captain of the king, being with the army at Bologna, having understood the loss of Brescia, went that way without deferring, and, having arrived at Brescia in three days, had the town again through the fortress. The fortress of Brescia also had, on that account, need, if it was to help, of a Monsignor de Foix and of a French army that should succour it in three days. So that the example of this, over against the contrary examples, is not enough; because many fortresses have been taken and retaken in the wars of our times with the same fortune with which the open country has been retaken and taken, not only in Lombardy but in Romagna, in the Kingdom of Naples, and through all the parts of Italy.

## NM-DISC.2.24.13

But as to building fortresses to defend oneself from enemies outside, I say that they are not necessary to those peoples or to those kingdoms that have good armies, and to those that do not have good armies they are useless; because good armies without fortresses are sufficient to defend oneself, fortresses without good armies cannot defend you. And this is seen by the experience of those who have been held excellent both in governments and in the other things, as is seen of the Romans and of the Spartans; that, if the Romans did not build fortresses, the Spartans not only abstained from them but did not permit [themselves] to have walls to their city, because they wished that the *virtù* of the particular man, not another defensive [thing], should defend them. Whence, a Spartan being asked by an Athenian whether the walls of Athens seemed beautiful to him, he answered him: Yes, if they were inhabited by women.

## NM-DISC.2.24.14

That prince, then, who has good armies, when on the coasts, at the front of his state, he has some fortress that can for some days sustain the enemy until he is in order, it would sometimes be a useful thing, but it is not necessary. But when the prince does not have a good army, to have fortresses for his state, or at the frontiers, they are to him either damaging or useless: damaging, because he readily loses them, and, lost, they make war on him; or if yet they were so strong that the enemy could not occupy them, they are left behind by the enemy army and come to be of no fruit: because good armies, when they do not have a most hard encounter, enter enemy countries without respect for city or fortress that they leave behind; as is seen in the ancient histories, and as is seen that Francesco Maria did, who in the nearest times, to assail Urbino, left behind ten enemy cities without any respect. That prince, then, who can make a good army can do without building fortresses; he who does not have a good army must not build. He must indeed fortify the city where he lives, keep it furnished and its citizens well disposed, so as to be able to sustain an enemy onset for so long that either accord or external aid frees him. All the other designs are of expense in times of peace, and useless in times of war. And so whoever considers all that I have said will know that the Romans, as [they were] wise in every other order of theirs, so were prudent in this judgment of the Latins and of the Privernates, where, not thinking of fortresses, they secured themselves of them with more virtuous ways and wiser.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Le Fortezze generalmente sono molto più dannose che utili.` "Fortresses generally are much more damaging than useful": `Fortezze`, fortresses (D312), the stronghold in every instance of this chapter, never the virtue (fortitude, FG I.10) nor a country's strength (D432); lower case in English, as `Città` city; `dannoso`, damaging (D106); `utile`, useful (D135). The 1554 sets the same sentence as its heading, and Canestrini sets it over Guicciardini's reply (NM-DISC-II24-D013; D487).

**`Parrà forse a questi savj de' nostri tempi cosa non bene considerata`.** "It will perhaps seem to these wise [men] of our times a thing not well considered": `savj`, wise [men], the adjective as a noun (D53); `savio` wise (D402), kept apart from `prudente` prudent, which Machiavelli sets beside it at the close (.14). The 1554 reads `coſi`, so, for `cosa` (NM-DISC-II24-D001; D485).

**`assicurarsi de' Popoli di Lazio e della Città di Priverno`.** "To secure themselves of the peoples of Latium and of the city of Privernum": `assicurarsi di`, secure oneself of (D219), the chapter's first concern and its last verb (.14 `se ne assicurarono`). The Privernates' case is Livy 8.20-21 (the editor's reference); the chapter returns to it at the close as `questo giudizio`.

**`la qual fusse un freno a tenerli in fede`.** "Which should be a rein to hold them in faith": `freno`, rein (D389); `tenere in freno`, hold reined in (.04), as D389's `tenere a freno`, and `frenare`, rein in (.04), keep the root; `fede`, faith (D431).

**`sendo massime un detto in Firenze, allegato da' nostri savj`.** "It being, above all, a saying in Florence, cited by our wise [men]": `massime`, above all (glossary); `allegare`, cite (D431). The saying about Pisa returns at .09.

**`se i Romani fussero stati fatti come loro`.** "If the Romans had been made like them": `loro`, the wise men of our times; the 1554 `queſti`, these (NM-DISC-II24-D001; D485).

**`d'altra virtù, d'altro giudizio, d'altra potenza`.** "Of another *virtù*, of another judgment, of another power": *virtù* untranslated (D16); `giudizio`, judgment (D374); `potenza`, power (D06). The triad kept in its order.

**`E mentre che Roma visse libera, e che la seguì gli ordini suoi`.** "And while Rome lived free, and while it followed its orders": the second `che` resumes `mentre che`, so "while" is repeated; `la`, it, Rome, the pleonastic subject pronoun; `ordini`, orders (glossary); `constituzioni`, constitutions. The 1554 lacks `che la` (NM-DISC-II24-D001).

**`ma salvò bene alcune delle edificate`.** "But it did save some of those built": `bene` emphatic. The 1554 reads `alcuna`, a difference at a/e, read `alcune` by both 1824 readers without doubt (NM-DISC-II24-D001; D485).

**`se gli è bene edificare Fortezze, e se le fanno danno o utile`.** "Whether it is good to build fortresses, and whether they do damage or advantage": `utile` as a noun, advantage (D283), against `danno`, damage (D106); `edificare`, build (glossary). The 1554 has `o ſe`, setting the two questions as alternatives (NM-DISC-II24-D002; D485).

**`Debbesi adunque considerare`.** "One must, then, consider": `dovere`, must (D274); `adunque`, then (D74).

**`da' soggetti`.** "From subjects": `soggetti` and, from .03 on, `sudditi`, both "subjects": a recorded collapse (D488). The glossary's `soggetto` "subject" (D388) is the same word.

**`E cominciando a render ragione`.** "And beginning to render reason": the idiom kept.

**`dico: Che quel Principe o quella Repubblica che ha paura de' suoi sudditi ... prima conviene che tal paura nasca`.** "I say that that prince or that republic which has fear of its subjects ..., first it is fitting that such fear arise": the subject announced and dropped, the anacoluthon kept (D85, D434); `conviene`, it is fitting (D85), here of a necessary inference, not of propriety; `nascere`, arise (D04); `paura`, fear (D313).

**`l'odio da' mali suoi portamenti; i mali portamenti nascono`.** "The hatred from its ill carriages; the ill carriages arise": `portamenti`, carriages, conduct in the old sense, of the root of `portarsi`, carry oneself (.07); `mali`, ill (D480); the verb understood in the first clause, as printed. Guicciardini takes up the phrase (`fare mali portamenti`) (D488).

**`o da poter credere tenerli con forza, o da poca prudenza di chi li governa`.** "Either from being able to believe [it] holds them with force, or from little prudence in whoever governs them": `poter` governs `credere`, and the believer, the prince or republic, is unexpressed in both clauses (D53); `forza`, force (D452); `prudenza`, prudence (glossary); `governare`, govern (D64).

**`è l'avere loro addosso le Fortezze`.** "Is having the fortresses upon them": `addosso`, upon them.

**`i mali trattamenti`.** "The ill treatments": `trattamento`, treatment; Guicciardini's `bene trattati`, well treated, answers it.

**`le quali quando sia vero questo, di gran lunga sono più nocive, che utili`.** "Which, when this is true, are by far more harmful than useful": `nocivo`, harmful (D106), the rubric's `dannose` put in another word, in the editor's reading; `quando sia vero questo`, the condition kept with the claim.

**`le ti fanno esser più audace, e più violento nei sudditi`.** "They make you more audacious and more violent toward the subjects": the second person enters here and recurs (.04-.06, .12, .13) beside the third.

**`dipoi non ci è quella sicurtà dentro che tu ti persuadi`.** "Afterwards, there is not that security within that you persuade yourself of": `dipoi`, afterwards (D187); `sicurtà`, security (glossary); `persuadersi`, persuade oneself (D358). The 1554 drops `non` and so reverses the reason: the security would be there (NM-DISC-II24-D003; D485).

**`sono nulla, eccetto che due`.** "Are nothing, except two": the two are the army in the field and the dispersal of the people.

**`o che li dissipi, spenga, disordini e disgiunga in modo che non possano convenire ad offenderti`.** "Or that you scatter, extinguish, disorder and disjoin them in such a way that they cannot convene to offend you": `spegnere`, extinguish (glossary); `disordinare`, disorder (glossary, with `ordine`); `convenire`, personal, convene (D358); `offendere`, offend, injure (glossary).

**`se tu gl'impoverisci, *spoliatis arma supersunt*: se tu gli disarmi, *furor arma ministrat*`.** "If you impoverish them, *spoliatis arma supersunt*; if you disarm them, *furor arma ministrat*": `impoverire`, transitive, impoverish (D488), D480's "grow poor" staying for the intransitive; Juvenal 8.124, "to the despoiled, arms remain"; Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.150, "fury furnishes arms" (latin.md). The 1554 prints the tags with no Italian rendering.

**`Se tu ammazzi i Capi, e gli altri segui d'ingiuriare, rinascono i Capi, come quelli dell'Idra`.** "If you kill the heads and follow on injuring the others, the heads are reborn, like those of the Hydra": `ammazzare`, kill (D262); `Capi`, heads (glossary), the Hydra's and the people's in one word; `ingiuriare`, injure (glossary `ingiuria`).

**`Se tu fai le Fortezze, le sono utili nei tempi di pace, perché ti danno più animo a far loro male`.** "If you make fortresses, they are useful in times of peace, because they give you more spirit to do them ill": the use in peace is the spirit to do the subjects ill, set against `inutilissime` in war. The 1554 reads `inutili`, useless, in peace too (NM-DISC-II24-D004; D485). `animo`, spirit (glossary), Guicciardini's word at FG .01 `danno animo`.

**`E se mai furono disutili`.** "And if ever they were unuseful": `disutile`, unuseful, kept apart from `inutile`, useless, and from `inutilità`, inutility (.08) (D488).

**`rispetto all'artiglierie ... dove altri non si possa ritirare con li ripari`.** "In respect of the artillery ... [places] where one cannot withdraw with ramparts": `rispetto a`, in respect of (glossary); `ripari`, ramparts, earthworks thrown up behind a breach (D488); `altri`, one. `come di sopra discorremmo`, "as we discoursed above", refers to an earlier chapter on artillery (II.17, in the editor's reading); `discorrere`, discourse (D01).

**`Io voglio questa materia disputarla più tritamente`.** "I wish to dispute this matter more minutely": the fronted object, resumed by `-la`, set in plain order; `disputare`, dispute (D410).

**`non può essere più inutile di quello ch'ella è`.** "Cannot be more useless than it is": the 1554 omits `di quello ch'ella è` (NM-DISC-II24-D005; D485).

**`la ti fa più pronto e men rispettivo ad oppressarli`.** "It makes you more prompt and less respectful in oppressing them": `pronto`, prompt (D298); `rispettivo`, respectful (D155); `oppressare`, oppress, with `oppressione`, oppression (glossary `opprimere`). The 1554 `riſpettoſo` (NM-DISC-II24-D005).

**`li fa sì esposti alla tua rovina, e gli accende`.** "Makes them so exposed to [working] your ruin, and kindles them": the citizens set toward your ruin, not its victims, the supplied word marking the editor's reading; `accendere`, kindle (D464).

**`per non dare cagione, né ardire ai figliuoli di diventare tristi`.** "So as not to give cause or daring to his sons to become wicked": `tristo`, wicked (D03).

**`acciocché quelli non in su la Fortezza, ma in su la benivolenza degli uomini si fondino`.** "In order that they may found themselves not upon the fortress but upon the benevolence of men": `acciocché`, in order that (D423); `benivolenza`, benevolence (D431); `fondarsi`, found oneself (glossary `fondare`). The 1554's `le forteZze` (NM-DISC-II24-D005).

**`E se il Conte Francesco Sforza, diventato Duca di Milano, fu riputato savio`.** "And if Count Francesco Sforza, having become Duke of Milan, was deemed wise": `riputare`, deem (D259). Francesco Sforza began the castle of Milan in 1450 (names.md; the editor's date).

**`non perdonarono ad alcuna generazione di violenza`.** "They spared no generation of violence": `generazione`, generation, in the old sense of kind, not of producing (D488); the 1554 `ſorte` (NM-DISC-II24-D006; D485).

**`come prima il nimico gli assaltò`.** "As soon as the enemy assailed them": `come prima`, as soon as (D374); `assaltare`, assail (D444). The heirs are Lodovico Sforza, and the French of 1499 the enemy, in the editor's reading.

**`se per poca prudenza avessero maneggiati agramente li loro cittadini`.** "If through little prudence they had handled their citizens sourly": `maneggiare`, handle (glossary); `agramente`, sourly, kept apart from `acerbamente`, bitterly (FG I.16).

**`che con quelli inimici con la Fortezza`.** "Than with them [as] enemies with the fortress": `amico` / `inimico`, friend / enemy (glossary), set in chiasmus with `senza Fortezza` / `con la Fortezza`.

**`le quali non ti giovano in alcuna parte`.** "Which do not help you in any part": `le quali`, the fortresses, plural after the singular; `giovare`, help (D334).

**`o le si perdono per fraude di chi le guarda`.** "Either they are lost through the fraud of whoever guards them": `fraude`, fraud (D452). The 1554 has `la guarda`, a difference at a/e (NM-DISC-II24-D006).

**`e ti ajutino a ricuperare uno Stato perduto`.** "And aid you to recover a lost state": `ajutare`, aid (D348), kept apart from `giovare`, help.

**`per l'orgoglio della Fortezza`.** "Through the haughtiness of the fortress": `orgoglio`, haughtiness, kept apart from `superbia`, pride (D464) (D488).

**`né agli Sforzeschi, né a' Francesi`.** "Neither to the Sforza nor to the French": `Sforzeschi`, the Sforza house and its party (names.md).

**`anzi a tutti ha recato danni e rovine assai`.** "On the contrary, to all it has brought many damages and ruins": `anzi` reversing, on the contrary (D135); `assai` of number, many (glossary).

**`Guido Ubaldo Duca d'Urbino, figliuolo di Federigo`.** Guidobaldo da Montefeltro; Federico da Montefeltro; the return of 1503 after Alexander VI's death (names.md; the editor's identifications). `stimato`, esteemed (D452); `Capitano`, captain (glossary).

**`fece rovinare tutte le Fortezze ... giudicandole dannose`.** "Had all the fortresses ... ruined, judging them damaging": `rovinare`, ruin, the verb of `rovina` (glossary). `giudicandole dannose` is the rubric's word.

**`Papa Giulio, cacciati i Bentivogli di Bologna`.** Julius II; Bologna 1506, the fortress 1507, the rising of 1511 (the editor's dates).

**`e dipoi faceva assassinare quel Popolo essendo maltrattato da un suo Governatore`.** "And afterwards had that people despoiled, [it] being ill-treated by a governor of his": `assassinare` in its old sense, to rob or ruin, not to murder (D488); the imperfect `faceva`, among perfects, a continuing course; the participle `essendo maltrattato` without an expressed subject, read with `quel Popolo` (D53). The 1554 omits `essendo maltrattato`, so that the governor is the agent of `assassinare` (NM-DISC-II24-D007; D485).

**`intanto che portandosi altrimenti gli arebbe giovato`.** "Whereas, [he] carrying himself otherwise, it would have helped him": `portarsi`, carry oneself (D325); `intanto che` adversative, whereas, in the editor's reading, the consecutive "insomuch that" giving no sense here (D488).

**`Niccolò da Castello, padre de' Vitelli`.** Niccolò Vitelli of Città di Castello; his return of 1482 (names.md; the editor's date). `sbandito`, banished; `disfare`, unmake (glossary), the chapter's verb against `edificare`, build; `l'avesse a tenere`, was to hold him (D261), `tenere` hold throughout, `mantenere` maintain.

**`seguìto nei prossimi tempi`.** "Which ensued in the nearest times": `seguire`, of an event, ensue; `prossimi tempi`, the nearest times, also at .14.

**`fece una Fortezza fortissima di tutte l'altre, delle quali al presente si abbia notizia`.** "He made a fortress the strongest of all the others of which at present one has notice": `notizia`, notice (D373). The 1554's `ſi haueſſe` (NM-DISC-II24-D008).

**`inespugnabile`; `la espugnò`.** "Inexpugnable"; "expugned it": one root kept in the cognate (D488).

**`chiamato da' Genovesi Codefa`.** "[A hill] called by the Genoese Codefa": `chiamato`, masculine, names the `colle`; the Briglia ("bridle") built on the Capo di Faro, in the editor's reading; the 1554 prints `Codeſa` (NM-DISC-II24-D008; names.md).

**`e per questo batteva tutto il porto, e gran parte della Terra di Genova`.** "And for this it beat all the port and a great part of the town of Genoa": `battere`, beat (D120), of a gun's reach; `Terra`, town, the walled place (D488).

**`in termine di sedici mesi, per fame, la espugnò`.** "Within the term of sixteen months ... expugned it [the fortress] by hunger": the delayed subject `Ottaviano Fregoso` kept after its verb, so that `il quale` attaches (D263); `termine`, term, a span of time, a sense beside D464's (D488); `industria`, industry (D452); `fame`, hunger.

**`conoscendo che non le Fortezze, ma la volontà degli uomini mantenevano i Principi in Stato`.** "Knowing that not fortresses but the will of men maintained princes in state": `volontà`, will (D117); the plural verb after the nearer singular, as printed.

**`lo ha tenuto, e tiene`.** "He has held it and holds it": written while Ottaviano held Genoa (1513-1522, as doge and then as governor for Francis I), in the editor's reading.

**`E dove a variare lo Stato di Genova solevano bastare mille fanti`.** "And where, to vary the state of Genoa, a thousand foot used to be enough": `variare`, vary (D423).

**`Fu adunque di spesa al Re il farla, e vergognoso il perderla; a Ottaviano glorioso il riacquistarla, ed utile il rovinarla`.** The four infinitives in two pairs; the 1554 omits the last member (NM-DISC-II24-D008; D485).

**`Ma vegnamo alle Repubbliche`.** "But let us come to the republics": the 1554 `alla Repub.` (NM-DISC-II24-D009).

**`nelle Terre ch'elle acquistano`.** "In the towns that they acquire": `Terre`, towns (D488); D480's "lands" stays for `Terre peregrine`.

**`voglio mi basti Firenze e Pisa`.** "I wish Florence and Pisa to be enough for me": `bastare`, be enough (D205). Pass A doubted `voglio` against `voglia`; pass B reads `voglio`, and the 1554 `uoglio` (D485).

**`o farsela compagna, o disfarla`.** "Either to make it a companion for themselves or to unmake it": `farsi compagni` (D452, D480).

**`perché la virtù delle Fortezze si vide nella venuta del Re Carlo`.** "Because the *virtù* of the fortresses was seen at the coming of King Charles": *virtù* of the fortresses, ironic, in the editor's reading; Charles VIII, 1494 (names.md).

**`e senza dubbio non arebbero fatto più cattiva prova che le Fortezze`.** "And without doubt they would not have made worse proof than the fortresses": `cattivo`, bad (D03); `prova`, proof, a test.

**`smuravano e non muravano`.** "Unwalled and did not wall": the two verbs of one root kept. Guicciardini's last words answer it: `non le smurorono`.

**`E chi contro a questa opinione mi allegasse negli antichi tempi Taranto, e ne' moderni Brescia`.** "And whoever should cite to me against this opinion, in ancient times Tarentum, and in modern ones Brescia": `opinione`, opinion (D02). Tarentum, retaken by Fabius in 209 BC (Livy 27.15-16); Brescia, 1512 (the editor's references).

**`Ed io non so di che utilità sia una Fortezza`.** "And I do not know of what utility a fortress is": a question put as an ignorance, as Guicciardini's `non so perché` (D482); `utilità`, utility (D472).

**`d'uno esercito consolare, e d'un Fabio Massimo per Capitano`.** "Of a consular army and of a Fabius Maximus for captain": `Fabio Massimo`, Fabius Maximus (names.md).

**`Dico come rade volte occorre quello che è occorso in quella ribellione`.** "I say that seldom does that occur which has occurred in that rebellion": `rade volte`, seldom (D97); `occorrere`, occur (D01), twice; `come` after a verb of saying, "that" (D334).

**`abbia uno esercito grosso e propinquo`.** "Has an army in force and near": `grosso`, in force (D238); `propinquo`, near (glossary).

**`essendo Monsignor di Fois Capitano del Re con l'esercito a Bologna`.** Gaston de Foix, February 1512 (names.md; the editor's date). `senza differire`, without deferring (D334).

**`Ebbe pertanto ancora la Fortezza di Brescia, a volere che la giovasse, bisogno`.** "The fortress of Brescia also had, on that account, need, if it was to help": `pertanto`, on that account (D98), kept apart from `però`, therefore, which does not occur in this chapter; `ancora`, also; `bisogno`, need (glossary); `a volere che la giovasse`, literally "to wish that it help"; the verb's long delay before `bisogno` kept.

**`l'esempio di questo all'incontro degli esempj contrarj`.** "The example of this, over against the contrary examples": `all'incontro`, over against (D401).

**`prese e riprese con la medesima fortuna che si è ripresa e presa la campagna`.** "Taken and retaken with the same fortune with which the open country has been retaken and taken": the 1824's chiasmus kept; the 1554 repeats the first order (NM-DISC-II24-D010; D485). `campagna`, open country (glossary).

**`dico che le non sono necessarie a quelli Popoli, né a quelli Regni che hanno buoni eserciti, ed a quelli che non hanno buoni eserciti sono inutili`.** The 1554 skips from the first `buoni eserciti` to the second and so says fortresses are useless to those who have good armies (NM-DISC-II24-D011; D485).

**`perché volevano che la virtù dell'uomo particolare, non altro difensivo, gli difendesse`.** "Because they wished that the *virtù* of the particular man, not another defensive [thing], should defend them": `particolare`, particular (D374); `difensivo`, defensive [thing] (D53).

**`sì se le fussero abitate da donne`.** "Yes, if they were inhabited by women": the Spartan's answer as Machiavelli gives it, a saying of the kind collected among the sayings of the Spartans (the editor's note).

**`Quel Principe adunque che abbia buoni eserciti, quando ... abbia qualche Fortezza ..., sarebbe qualche volta cosa utile`.** "That prince, then, who has good armies, when ... he has some fortress ..., it would sometimes be a useful thing": the subject dropped for an impersonal predicate, as printed (D85); `qualche volta`, sometimes (D431); `ad ordine`, in order. The 1554 omits `la ... è` in the next clause (NM-DISC-II24-D012).

**`avere le Fortezze per il suo Stato, o alle frontiere, gli sono o dannose o inutili`.** "To have fortresses for his state, or at the frontiers, they are to him either damaging or useless": the infinitive subject and the plural verb, as printed.

**`quando non hanno gagliardissimo riscontro`.** "When they do not have a most hard encounter": `gagliardissimo`, absolute superlative, of `gagliardo`, hard (D313); `riscontro`, encounter, opposition met in the field, a sense split from D160's "corroboration" (D488).

**`Francesco Maria ... per assaltare Urbino`.** Francesco Maria della Rovere's recovery of Urbino in 1517 (names.md; the editor's date).

**`Debbe bene afforzare la Città dove abita, tenerla munita`.** "He must indeed fortify the city where he lives, keep it furnished": `afforzare`, fortify; `munito`, furnished, provisioned; `tanto ... o che accordo o che ajuto esterno lo liberi`, for so long that either accord or external aid frees him: `accordo`, accord (D452); `ajuto`, aid (D348).

**`come savj in ogni altro loro ordine, così furono prudenti in questo giudizio de' Latini e de' Privernati`.** "As [they were] wise in every other order of theirs, so were prudent in this judgment of the Latins and of the Privernates": `savio` and `prudente` kept apart, the last adjective, `più savj` after its noun, answering the first `savj` (.01); `giudizio de' Latini`, the judgment passed on the Latins (Livy 8.13-14) and the Privernates (Livy 8.21), in the editor's reading; `con più virtuosi modi`, with more virtuous ways, the adjective of *virtù* (glossary).

**`sono sufficienti a difendersi, le Fortezze`.** "Are sufficient to defend oneself, fortresses": `difendersi` impersonal, as at .02, so that the armies suffice for whoever has them; the comma kept, the two members under one `perché`.

**`fu mandato Fabio Massimo con tutto lo esercito, il quale sarebbe stato atto a ricuperarlo`.** `il quale` may be Fabius or the army; "which" takes the army, in the editor's reading.

**`che possa qualche dì sostenere lo inimico`.** "That can for some days sustain the enemy": `sostenere`, sustain (D431), withstand.

**`per rispetto di loro`.** "In respect of them", parallel with `per conto dei nimici`, "on account of the enemies": a reason concerning the men, not deference (glossary `rispetto`).

**`intesa la perdita di Brescia`.** "Having understood the loss of Brescia": `intendere`, understand (D374).

**`quando la non vi fusse stata`.** "If it had not been there": `quando` with the pluperfect subjunctive, an unreal condition.

**`Monsignor di Fois`.** "Monsignor de Foix": the title kept, the name in its French form (names.md; D488).
