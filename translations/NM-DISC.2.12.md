# Discourses II.12

## NM-DISC.2.12.01

I have heard men very practised in the things of war sometimes dispute, if there are two princes of almost equal forces, if that harder [one] has proclaimed war against that other, which is the better course for the other: either to await the enemy within his borders, or to go to find him at home and assail him. And I have heard reasons adduced for it on every side.

## NM-DISC.2.12.02

And whoever defends going to assail others cites for it the counsel that Croesus gave to Cyrus, when, [Cyrus] having arrived on the borders of the Massagetae to make war on them, their queen Tomyris sent to tell him that he choose which of the two courses he wished: either to enter her kingdom, where she would await him, or wished that she come to find him. And the thing having come into disputation, Croesus, against the opinion of the others, said that they go to find her; citing that if he vanquished her at a distance from her kingdom, he would not take the kingdom away from her, because she would have time to remake herself; but if he vanquished her within her borders, he could follow her in her flight, and, not giving her space to remake herself, take the state away from her. They cite also the counsel that Hannibal gave to Antiochus, when that king designed to make war on the Romans, where he showed how the Romans could not be vanquished except in Italy, because there another could avail himself of their arms and riches and friends; but whoever fought them outside Italy, and left them Italy free, left them that spring to which life never fails for supplying forces where it is required, and he concluded that from the Romans Rome could be taken away earlier than the empire, and Italy earlier than the other provinces. He cites also Agathocles, who, not being able to sustain the war at home, assailed the Carthaginians, who were making it on him, and brought them to ask for peace. He cites Scipio, who, to remove the war from Italy, assailed Africa.

## NM-DISC.2.12.03

Whoever speaks to the contrary says that whoever wishes to make an enemy come to a bad end, let him distance him from home. They cite the Athenians, who, while they made war convenient to their home, remained superior; when they went to a distance, and went with their armies into Sicily, they lost their liberty. He cites the poetic fables, where it is shown that Antaeus, king of Libya, assailed by Hercules the Egyptian, was insuperable while he awaited him within the borders of his kingdom, but when he went to a distance from it, through the craft of Hercules, he lost the state and his life. Whence place is given to the fable of Antaeus, that, being on the earth, he took up force again from his mother, who was the Earth, and that Hercules, having perceived this, raised him on high, and distanced him from the earth. He cites for it also modern judgments. Each one knows how Ferdinand, king of Naples, was held in his times a most wise prince, and, the report coming, two years before his death, how the king of France, Charles VIII, wished to come to assail him, [Ferdinand], having made many preparations, fell ill; and, coming to death, among the other counsels that he left to Alfonso his son was that he await the enemy within the kingdom, and for nothing in the world draw forces out of his state, but await him within his borders all entire; which was not observed by him; but, having sent an army into Romagna, without fighting he lost it and the state.

## NM-DISC.2.12.04

The reasons which, beyond the things said, are adduced on every side are: that whoever assails comes with greater spirit than whoever awaits, which makes the army more confident; he takes away, beyond this, many conveniences from the enemy for being able to avail himself of his things, he not being able to avail himself of those subjects who are sacked; and, through having the enemy at home, the lord is constrained to have more respect to drawing money from them and wearying them, so that he comes to dry up that spring, as Hannibal says, which makes it that that man can sustain the war. Beyond this, his soldiers, through finding themselves in others' countries, are more necessitated to fight, and that necessity makes *virtù*, as we have said more times. On the other hand it is said that in awaiting the enemy one awaits with much advantage, because without any hardship [to yourself] you can give him many hardships of victuals, and of every other thing that an army has need of; you can better impede his designs, through the notice of the country that you have more than he: you can meet him with more forces, through being able readily to unite them all, but not being able to distance them all from home; you can, being routed, remake yourself readily, both because much of your army will be saved, through having refuges near, and because the supplement does not have to come from a distance; so that you come to hazard all the forces, and not all the fortune, and, going to a distance, you hazard all the fortune, and not all the forces. And there have been some who, to weaken their enemy better, let him enter several days' [march] into their country, and take many towns, in order that, leaving garrisons in all of them, he may weaken his army, and they may afterwards fight him more readily.

## NM-DISC.2.12.05

But to say now what I understand of it, I believe that this distinction is to be made: either I have my country armed, as the Romans [had], and as the Swiss have it; or I have it disarmed, as the Carthaginians had it, and as the kings of France and the Italians have it. In this case one must keep the enemy at a distance from home; because, your *virtù* being in money and not in men, whenever the path to it is impeded for you, you are despatched; nor does any thing impede it for you so much as war at home. For examples there are the Carthaginians, who, while they had their home free, were able with the revenues to make war with the Romans, and, when they had it assailed, were not able to resist Agathocles. The Florentines had no remedy whatever against Castruccio, lord of Lucca, because he made war on them at home, so that they had to give themselves, in order to be defended, to King Robert of Naples. But, Castruccio dead, those same Florentines had the spirit to assail the duke of Milan at home, and to work to take his kingdom away from him: so much *virtù* did they show in far-off wars, and so much baseness in near ones!

## NM-DISC.2.12.06

But when kingdoms are armed, as Rome was armed, and as the Swiss are, they are the more difficult to vanquish the more you approach them. For these bodies can unite more forces to resist an onset than they can to assail others. Nor does the authority of Hannibal move me in this case, because his passion and his advantage made him speak so to Antiochus. For, if the Romans had had, in so much space of time, those three routs in France that they had in Italy from Hannibal, without doubt they were despatched; because they would not have availed themselves of the residues of the armies as they availed themselves of them in Italy, they would not have had those conveniences for remaking themselves, nor could they resist the enemy with those forces with which they could. It is not found that, to assail a province, they ever sent out armies that exceeded fifty thousand persons; but to defend home they put in arms against the French, after the first Punic war, eighteen hundred thousand. Nor could they later have routed those in Lombardy as they routed them in Tuscany; because against so great a number of enemies they could not have conducted so many forces at such a distance, nor fought them with that convenience. The Cimbri routed a Roman army in Germany, nor did the Romans have a remedy there. But when they arrived in Italy, and [the Romans] could put all their forces together, they despatched them. The Swiss it is easy to vanquish outside their home, where they cannot send more than some thirty or forty thousand men; but to vanquish them at home, where they can muster a hundred thousand of them, is most difficult.

## NM-DISC.2.12.07

I conclude, then, anew, that the prince who has his peoples armed and ordered for war always await at home a powerful and dangerous war, and not go to meet it. But he who has his subjects disarmed, and the country unused to war, let him always distance it from home as far as he can. And so the one and the other, each in his degree, will defend himself better.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `S'egli è meglio, temendo di essere assaltato, inferire, o aspettare la guerra`: "Whether it is better, fearing to be assailed, to bring, or to await, war": `la guerra` is the object of both infinitives and stands last, as in the Italian. `inferire`, the Latin *bellum inferre*, to carry war into: "bring". The 1554 reads `muouere`, "move [war]" (NM-DISC-II12-D007). `assaltare`, assail, through the chapter, as at NM II.10 and I.23. Guicciardini's rubric over his reply is the same sentence (Guicciardini, heading).

**`se quello più gagliardo abbia bandito la guerra`.** "[And] the harder has proclaimed war": `gagliardo`, hard (D313), against `forte` strong; the comparative of two princes `quasi di eguali forze`, `eguale` "equal" (D262). Both `se` are conditional; the question is `quale`, and "that harder [one]" keeps `quello ... quell'altro`.

**`quale sia miglior partito per l'altro`.** "Which is the better course for the other": `partito`, course (D236), here and at `quale de' duoi partiti`.

**`il nimico`.** "The enemy": `nimico`, `inimico` (.06), enemy (D334), throughout; the 1554 spells it `nemico`.

**`allega`, `Allegano`, `Allegane`.** "Cites", "they cite", "he cites for it": `allegare`, cite (D431). Machiavelli moves between singular and plural for the same party, `chi difende` and `chi parla al contrario` with `Allega` and `Allegano`, and the English keeps each number. `Allegane` is `allega` + `ne`, "cites for it".

**`discosto al suo Regno`; `lo discosti da casa`.** "At a distance from her kingdom"; "let him distance him from home": `discosto`, at a distance; `discostare`, distance (.03 ×2, .04, .07); `discostarsi`, go to a distance (.03 ×2, .04). One root held through the chapter, `sì discosto` (.06) included: the question is the distance of the war from home. The English keeps `da` "from" where the Italian has `al` or `a casa` (.05). `trarre`, draw (.03, .04), is kept apart (D444).

**`torrebbe`, `torle`, `torre`, `torgli`.** "Take away": `torre`, take away (D131, D313), four times. At `torrebbe` the 1554 reads `terrebbe`, "would hold" (NM-DISC-II12-D004, D438).

**`arebbe tempo a rifarsi`.** "Would have time to remake herself": `rifare`, remake (D348), reflexive here and at .04 (`rifarti`, `a rifarsi`); "recover" is not used, so that the verb holds across both authors (Guicciardini .01 `a rifarti`, `riordinarti e rifarti`).

**`quella fonte, che mai le manca vita a somministrare forze dove bisogna`.** "That spring to which life never fails for supplying forces where it is required": `fonte`, spring, taken up again in .04 (`seccare quella fonte, come dice Annibale`), so the figure is Hannibal's both times. The 1554 reads `quella forza` (NM-DISC-II12-D001, D438). `mancare`, fail (D206); `le` is the spring. `bisogna`, it is required (D260): see the note on `bisogni` at Machiavelli I.2.

**`prima torre Roma che lo Imperio, e prima la Italia che le altre Provincie`.** "Rome could be taken away earlier than the empire, and Italy earlier than the other provinces": `prima`, earlier (D334); `Imperio`, empire (D124), dominion over other peoples; `Provincia`, province (glossary).

**`levare la guerra d'Italia`.** "To remove the war from Italy": `levare`, remove (D131).

**`fare capitare male`.** "Make ... come to a bad end", the idiom. `chi vuole ...` is the subject of the jussive `lo discosti`, which the English resumes with "let him", as at .07.

**`mentre che feciono la guerra comoda alla casa loro`.** "While they made war convenient to their home": `comodo`, convenient (D59); `comodità`, convenience, through .04 and .06.

**`Ercole Egizio`.** "Hercules the Egyptian": Machiavelli's name for the Hercules of the Antaeus story; the epithet is his and is not corrected. `astuzia`, craft, with `astutamente`, craftily (D52).

**`ripigliava le forze`.** "Took up force again": `ripigliare`, take up again (D224); `forze`, force, the singular of strength the glossary allows (`ingegno e forze`), so that a wrestler's strength does not read as troops.

**`Ferrando Re di Napoli`.** "Ferdinand, king of Naples": on the editor's identification Ferdinand I of Naples (Ferrante), who died in January 1494; not the Ferdinand of Aragon of Machiavelli I.29. Guicciardini names him three times (Guicciardini .01, .02).

**`la fama`.** "The report": what was heard of the French king's design, not renown.

**`tra gli altri ricordi`.** "Among the other counsels": `ricordo`, counsel (D325, extended to Machiavelli, D444), here Ferdinand's last advice to his son; a recorded collapse with `consiglio`, counsel (.02 ×2).

**`lo aspetta[s]se`.** "Await him": the 1824 prints `aspettase`, with one s; emended from the 1554 (NM-DISC-II12-D006, D438).

**`perdè quello e lo Stato`.** "He lost ... it and the state": `quello`, the army.

**`toglie oltra di questo molte comodità al nimico`.** "He takes away, beyond this, many conveniences from the enemy": the subject is `chi assalta`, unexpressed; `Che` governs only the first reason and is not repeated. `togliere`, take away (D131); `oltra di questo`, `Oltre di questo`, "beyond this" (NM II.proem, NM II.10).

**`è costretto il signore avere più rispetto a trarre da loro danari`.** "The lord is constrained to have more respect to drawing money from them": `avere rispetto a`, have respect to (glossary); `costretto`, constrained, kept apart from `necessitato`, necessitated (NM II.10); `rispetto`, respect (glossary); `danari`, `danaro`, money (D431). `Signore`, lord (glossary).

**`quella necessità fa virtù, come più volte abbiamo detto`.** "That necessity makes *virtù*, as we have said more times": `più volte`, more times (D373); *virtù* (D16); the cross-reference is Machiavelli's (I.1, among others).

**`senza disagio alcuno tu puoi dare a quello molti disagi`.** "Without any hardship [to yourself] you can give him many hardships": `disagio`, hardship, twice; the bracket marks whose hardship the first is. The second person runs from here to the end of the list, and the four `puoi` stay "you can"; the 1554 spells them `poi`.

**`per la notizia del paese`.** "Through the notice of the country": `notizia`, notice (D373), knowledge held of the ground.

**`ma non potere già tutte discostarle da casa`.** "But not being able to distance them all from home": the infinitive continues `per poterle`; `già` after a negative is not rendered (D423).

**`per avere il nimico in casa`, `per trovarsi`, `per poterle`, `per avere i rifugj`.** "Through having", "through finding", "through being able", "through having": causal `per` with the infinitive, one English preposition.

**`il supplemento non ha a venire discosto`.** "The supplement does not have to come from a distance": `avere a`, "have to" after a negative (D261). `supplemento`, supplement, the fresh troops sent to make up the army.

**`tanto che tu vieni ad arrischiare tutte le forze, e non tutta la fortuna, e discostandoti arrischi tutta la fortuna, e non tutte le forze`.** "So that you come to hazard all the forces, and not all the fortune, and, going to a distance, you hazard all the fortune, and not all the forces": a chiasmus whose second member holds only under `discostandoti`. It turns Machiavelli I.23's `tutta la fortuna tua, e non tutte le forze`; here there is no possessive, and none is supplied. The 1554 omits the gerund, so that the two members contradict each other (NM-DISC-II12-D002, D438). `arrischiare`, hazard.

**`parecchie giornate`.** "Several days' [march]": `giornata` of distance, the day's journey, decided by sense (D444); "battle" stays for the engagement (D49).

**`assai Terre`, `assai preparazioni`.** "Many towns", "many preparations": `assai` of number, "many" (glossary, I.7); `Terre`, towns, lower case in the English.

**`possanlo dipoi combattere più facilmente`.** "They may afterwards fight him more readily": `dipoi`, afterwards (D187); `facilmente`, readily (D27), as at `per poterle facilmente` and `rifarti facilmente`; `è facile` (.06) stays "it is easy". The purpose subjunctives `indebolisca`, `possanlo` both take "may". The 1554 omits `più facilmente` (NM-DISC-II12-D002, D438).

**`io credo che si abbia a fare questa distinzione`.** "I believe that this distinction is to be made": `credo`, I believe (glossary), Machiavelli's own judgment, marked; `avere a`, "be to" (D261).

**`come i Romani, e come l'hanno i Svizzeri`.** "As the Romans [had], and as the Swiss have it": the bracket supplies the verb the first member leaves to the second. At both `e come` the 1554 reads `o come` (NM-DISC-II12-D004, D438). `i Re di Francia`, "the kings of France", plural; the 1554 `il Re` (NM-DISC-II12-D005).

**`In questo caso`.** "In this case": the second, the disarmed country; the first is answered in .06.

**`si debbe tenere il nimico discosto a casa`.** "One must keep the enemy at a distance from home": `dovere`, must (D274).

**`sendo la tua virtù nel danaro e non negli uomini`.** "Your *virtù* being in money and not in men": *virtù* (D16); `danaro`, money (D431).

**`tu sei spacciato`.** "You are despatched": `spacciare`, despatch, finish off, here, at `erano spacciati` (.06) and `gli spacciarono` (.06), one verb. The 1554 reads `spacciata` (NM-DISC-II12-D004).

**`quando l'avevano assaltata`.** "When they had it assailed": `la` is the home (`la casa loro`); the construction, a participle agreed with its object, is kept.

**`I Fiorentini non avevano rimedio alcuno con Castruccio`.** "The Florentines had no remedy whatever against Castruccio": `rimedio`, remedy (glossary); Castruccio Castracani, lord of Lucca, who defeated the Florentines at Altopascio in 1325; Florence gave itself to Charles of Calabria, son of King Robert of Naples, in 1326, and Machiavelli names the king (the editor's identification; Machiavelli's `Re Roberto` is kept). `ebbero a darsi`, "had to give themselves" (D261, necessity).

**`il Duca di Milano`.** "The duke of Milan": not named; on the editor's reading the wars against Giangaleazzo Visconti in the 1390s, which Guicciardini's `nelle guerre fatte loro da' Visconti` (Guicciardini .01) seems to take up.

**`nelle guerre longinque, e tanta viltà nelle propinque`.** "In far-off wars, and so much baseness in near ones": `longinquo`, far-off (D444); `propinquo`, near (glossary), as `i rifugj propinqui` (.04). The 1554 has the plainer `lontane` and `uicine` (NM-DISC-II12-D003, D438). `viltà`, baseness, against *virtù*.

**`questi Corpi`.** "These bodies": the armed kingdoms, the capital kept as printed in the Italian only.

**`ad uno impeto`.** "To an onset": `impeto`, the attack of an assailant.

**`la passione e l'utile suo`.** "His passion and his advantage": `utile`, advantage (D283).

**`senza dubbio erano spacciati`.** "Without doubt they were despatched": the indicative in the apodosis of a counterfactual, as in the Italian, and so `né potevano`, "nor could they resist", with `che poterono`, "with which they could".

**`quelle tre rotte in Francia, ch'egli ebbero in Italia da Annibale`.** "Those three routs in France that they had in Italy from Hannibal": `rotte`, routs (D431); the Trebia, Trasimene and Cannae, by the editor's count. `Francia` is Machiavelli's France for Gaul, as `Francesi` (names.md).

**`diciotto centinaja di migliaja`.** "Eighteen hundred thousand": the figure is Machiavelli's, against the Gauls after the first Punic war (the war of 225 BC, by the editor's identification), and is not corrected.

**`I Cimbri ruppero uno esercito romano in la Magna`.** "The Cimbri routed a Roman army in Germany": `la Magna`, Germany (names.md); `uno esercito romano`, where the 1554 reads `Romana` (NM-DISC-II12-D004, D438, under D203(b)). The Romans who despatched them in Italy were Marius' army (101 BC, by the editor's identification); the bracketed subject marks the change.

**`dei residui degli eserciti`.** "The residues of the armies": `residuo`, residue, as `il residuo delle genti tue` (Machiavelli I.23). **`condurre`.** "Conduct": "lead" stays `menare`'s (D313).

**`raccozzare`.** "Muster": gather together in haste; `ragunare`, gather, kept apart (D374).

**`Conchiudo adunque di nuovo`.** "I conclude, then, anew": `adunque`, then (D74); `di nuovo`, anew (glossary).

**`i suoi Popoli armati e ordinati alla guerra`.** "His peoples armed and ordered for war": `Popoli`, peoples (D402); `ordinare`, order (glossary).

**`eleggesse`, `volesse`, `si andasse`, `aspettasse`.** The reported commands of .02 and .03 keep the bare subjunctive, "that he choose", "that they go", "that he await" (D348, D423).

**`aspetti`, `se la discosti`.** "Always await", "let him always distance it": subjunctives of counsel, the first under `Conchiudo ... che`, the second free-standing; neither takes `dovere`'s "should" (D348).

**`ciascuno nel suo grado`.** "Each in his degree": `grado`, degree (glossary).

The unit ends with a stop.
