# Discourses II.14

## NM-DISC.2.14.01

One sees many times how humility not only does not help but does harm, most of all using it with insolent men, who either from envy or from another cause have conceived hatred toward you. Of which our Historian bears witness in this cause of war between the Romans and the Latins. Because, the Samnites complaining to the Romans that the Latins had assailed them, the Romans did not wish to prohibit the Latins such a war, desiring not to irritate them; which not only did not irritate them, but made them become more spirited against them, and they uncovered themselves sooner as enemies. Of which the words used by the aforesaid Annius, Latin praetor, in the same council bear witness, where he says: *Tentastis patientiam negando militem: quis dubitat exarsisse eos? Pertulerunt tamen hunc dolorem. Exercitus nos parare adversus Samnites foederatos suos audierunt, nec moverunt se ab urbe. Unde haec illis tanta modestia, nisi a conscientia virium, et nostrarum, et suarum?* One knows, on that account, most clearly by this text how much the patience of the Romans increased the arrogance of the Latins.

## NM-DISC.2.14.02

And therefore a prince must never wish to lack his degree, and must never leave any thing by accord, wishing to leave it honourably, except when he can [hold] it, or he believes that he can hold it; because it is better almost always (the thing having been conducted to [such a] term that you cannot leave it in the said way) to let it be taken away from you with forces than with the fear of forces; because if you leave it with the fear, you do it to remove the war from yourself, and most times you do not remove it from yourself; because he to whom you will have conceded it with an uncovered baseness will not stand firm, but will wish to take away other things from you, and will be kindled more against you, esteeming you less; and on the other hand, in your favour, you will find the defenders colder, it seeming to them that you are either weak or base.

## NM-DISC.2.14.03

But if you at once, the longing of the adversary uncovered, prepare the forces, even though they are inferior to him, he begins to esteem you; esteem you more [do] the other princes round about; and to such a one comes the longing to aid you, [you] being in arms, who, [you] abandoning yourself, would never aid you.

## NM-DISC.2.14.04

This is understood when you have one enemy; but when you had more, to render some of the things that you possessed to one of them to regain him [for yourself], even though the war were already uncovered, and to dismember him from the other confederates your enemies, will always be a prudent course.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Ingannansi molte volte gli uomini, credendo con la umiltà vincere la superbia`: "Men deceive themselves many times, believing with humility to vanquish pride": `ingannarsi`, deceive oneself (D374, glossary), the verb of II.13's deceit turned on the deceiver; `molte volte`, many times, as `Vedesi molte volte` (.01); `umiltà`, humility, against `superbia`, pride (glossary), the pair of the rubric, which the chapter carries on as `pazienza` and `arroganza`, patience and arrogance (.01); `vincere`, vanquish (D313). The rubric's fronted verb is the ordinary order of the Italian reflexive, and the English is plain (D263). The 1554 heads the chapter with the same sentence (NM-DISC-II14-D005); Canestrini sets it over Guicciardini's reply (Guicciardini, heading).

**`Vedesi molte volte come la umiltà non solamente non giova ma nuoce`.** "One sees many times how humility not only does not help but does harm": `giovare`, help (D334); `nuocere`, do harm (D106). `come`, how, introducing what is seen.

**`massimamente usandola con gli uomini insolenti`.** "Most of all using it with insolent men": `massimamente`, most of all; the gerund is left without a subject, as in the Italian; `insolente`, insolent, with `insolenza` (glossary), the word Guicciardini takes up of the enemy (`insolente lo inimico`, Guicciardini .01).

**`hanno concetto odio teco`.** "Have conceived hatred toward you": `concepire`, conceive; `teco`, with you, rendered "toward you". The `ti` of the chapter's advice begins here, before any prince is named.

**`Di che ne fa fede lo Istorico nostro`.** "Of which our Historian bears witness": `fare fede`, bear witness (D464), here and at `Di che ne fanno fede` below; `Istorico`, Historian, Livy, capitalised as printed. The episode is Livy 8.1-2 (the editor's reference): in 341 BC the Samnites complained at Rome that the Latins and Campanians were making war on them, and the senate answered, of the Latins, that nothing in the treaty forbade them to make war on whom they wished. The motive, `desiderando non gli irritare`, is as Machiavelli gives it, not a word of Livy's that the note reports.

**`in questa cagione di guerra tra i Romani e i Latini`.** "In this cause of war between the Romans and the Latins": the war of II.13, whose cause there is the Latins' recognition of the Roman deceit (Machiavelli II.13); here the same war is given a second cause, Roman humility. `cagione`, cause (glossary).

**`dolendosi i Sanniti con i Romani`.** "The Samnites complaining to the Romans": `dolersi`, complain; the gerund's subject stated, as printed.

**`i Romani non vollono proibire ai Latini tal guerra, desiderando non gli irritare`.** "The Romans did not wish to prohibit the Latins such a war, desiring not to irritate them": `volere`, wish (D117); `proibire`, prohibit (glossary); `desiderare`, desire (glossary); `irritare`, irritate.

**`ma li fece diventare più animosi contro a loro`.** "But made them become more spirited against them": `animoso`, spirited, with `animosamente`, spiritedly (glossary); the first "them" is the Latins, the second the Romans, as in the Italian `li` and `loro`.

**`e si scopersono più presto inimici`.** "And they uncovered themselves sooner as enemies": `scoprirsi`, uncover oneself, of `scoprire`, uncover (D452), the root of the chapter's `scoperta` (.02, .03, .04); `più presto` without `che` is temporal here, "sooner", and D135's "rather ... than" stays for the comparison; `inimico`, enemy (D334).

**`le parole usate dal prefato Annio Pretore Latino nel medesimo Concilio`.** "The words used by the aforesaid Annius, Latin praetor, in the same council": `prefato`, aforesaid: Annius Setinus, named in Machiavelli II.13 (names.md); `Concilio`, council, here the same body as II.13's `Consiglio` (D452), a collapse now recorded (D464). The 1554 reads `Pretorio` (NM-DISC-II14-D001, D459).

**`Tentastis patientiam ... et nostrarum, et suarum?`** Livy 8.4.6-7, as Machiavelli quotes it, left in Latin as he leaves it: "You tried [their] patience by refusing soldiers: who doubts they were inflamed? Yet they bore this pain. They heard that we were preparing armies against the Samnites, their confederates, and did not move from the city. Whence this so great modesty in them, if not from a consciousness of strength, both ours and theirs?" (the editor's rendering). In the 1554 the quotation lacks Livy's `a` before `conscientia` and is followed by an Italian rendering, which the 1824 does not print (NM-DISC-II14-D002, D459). `patientia` is the chapter's `pazienza`; `modestia`, the Romans' restraint, Annius puts down to awareness of strength (`conscientia virium`), the Latins' as well as their own. `Tentastis` as the 1824 prints it.

**`Conoscesi pertanto chiarissimo per questo testo`.** "One knows, on that account, most clearly by this text": `conoscere`, know (D374); `pertanto`, on that account (D98), kept apart from `però`, therefore, which opens the next period; `chiarissimo`, most clearly, the superlative kept; `testo`, text, the passage of Livy.

**`quanto la pazienza dei Romani accrebbe l'arroganza de' Latini`.** "How much the patience of the Romans increased the arrogance of the Latins": `pazienza`, patience; `accrescere`, increase (D69); `arroganza`, arrogance. The rubric's humility and pride, in the case.

**`E però mai un Principe debbe volere mancare del grado suo`.** "And therefore a prince must never wish to lack his degree": `però`, therefore (D22); `dovere`, must (D274), here and in the next member; `mancare di` with a noun, lack (D312), here in the sense of falling short of, or letting go, the rank he holds; `grado`, degree (glossary), as II.13's `gradi grandi`. The 1554 doubles the negative, `mai ... non debbe`, with the same sense (NM-DISC-II14-D003, D459).

**`e non debbe mai lasciare alcuna cosa d'accordo, volendola lasciare onorevolmente`.** "And must never leave any thing by accord, wishing to leave it honourably": `lasciare`, leave, the chapter's verb of giving up, held through .02; `d'accordo`, by accord, by agreement rather than by force; `accordo`, accord (D452); `onorevolmente`, honourably. The gerund states the condition: to leave a thing honourably, leave it by accord only when the case below holds.

**`se non quando e' la può, o e' si crede che la possa tenere`.** "Except when he can [hold] it, or he believes that he can hold it": the first `la può` leaves `tenere` to the second member, and the English supplies it in brackets (D53); `tenere`, hold (D242). The construction is compressed; the sense is that the prince should leave a thing by accord only while he still could hold it, or thinks he could. `e' si crede` may also be impersonal, `e'` for `egli` as in `egli è meglio` just after: "or it is believed that he can hold it", the belief of others, whose esteem the chapter goes on to weigh; the English keeps the personal reading and records the other.

**`perché egli è meglio quasi sempre`.** "Because it is better almost always": `egli` the impersonal subject, not rendered.

**`(sendosi condotta la cosa in termine che tu non la possa lasciare nel modo detto)`.** "(The thing having been conducted to [such a] term that you cannot leave it in the said way)": `sendosi condotta`, a perfect gerund, the point already reached; `condursi`, of a thing, be conducted (D444, D452, D464); `termine`, term, a point reached: a third sense beside D325's `termini`, bearings, of conduct, and D401's terms of a comparison (D464); `nel modo detto`, in the said way, honourably, by accord. The parenthesis is printed and kept.

**`lasciarsela torre con le forze, che con la paura delle forze`.** "To let it be taken away from you with forces than with the fear of forces": `-se-`, from you, the chapter's person; `torre`, take away (D131); `forze`, forces (glossary); `paura`, fear (D313); `con la paura`, "with the fear", in the next member, points back to it. The 1554 omits the article before `paura` (NM-DISC-II14-D003).

**`lo fai per levarti la guerra, e il più delle volte non te la lievi`.** "You do it to remove the war from yourself, and most times you do not remove it from yourself": `levarsi` with an object, remove from oneself (D373), held in both members; `il più delle volte`, most times.

**`colui a chi tu arai con una viltà scoperta concesso quella`.** "He to whom you will have conceded it with an uncovered baseness": `viltà`, baseness (D444); `scoperto`, uncovered, the participle of `scoprire` (D452), the baseness shown openly; `concedere`, concede; `quella`, it, the thing (`la cosa`). The 1554 reads `quello` (NM-DISC-II14-D003, D459).

**`non starà saldo, ma ti vorrà torre delle altre cose`.** "Will not stand firm, but will wish to take away other things from you": `saldo`, firm; `delle altre cose`, other things, partitive.

**`e si accenderà più contro di te stimandoti meno`.** "And will be kindled more against you, esteeming you less": `accendersi`, be kindled, as Livy's `exarsisse`; `stimare`, esteem (D452), held at .03.

**`e dall'altra parte in tuo favore troverai i difensori più freddi`.** "And on the other hand, in your favour, you will find the defenders colder": `dall'altra parte`, on the other hand (D24); the fronted `in tuo favore` is kept where it stands, so that it may go with the defenders or with their coldness; `difensore`, defender; `freddo`, cold.

**`parendo loro che tu sia o debole o vile`.** "It seeming to them that you are either weak or base": `parere`, seem; `debole`, weak; `vile`, base, the adjective of `viltà` (D444, D464), the word Guicciardini takes up (`a fare vile te`, Guicciardini .01).

**`Ma se tu subito scoperta la voglia dello avversario prepari le forze`.** "But if you at once, the longing of the adversary uncovered, prepare the forces": `subito`, at once (D177), placed between `tu` and the absolute participle, so that it may go with either, and the English keeps it there; `le forze`, the forces, your own. `voglia`, longing (D117); `avversario`, adversary (D334), here, beside `inimico`, enemy (.04); `scoperta`, uncovered, as above.

**`ancora che elle siano inferiori a lui`.** "Even though they are inferior to him": `ancora che`, even though (D31 extended); `inferiore`, inferior. `elle`, the forces.

**`quello ti comincia a stimare; stimanti più gli altri Principi allo intorno`.** "He begins to esteem you; esteem you more [do] the other princes round about": the second member fronts its verb to answer the first (`stimare`, `stimanti`), a contrast in which the order does the work, so it is kept (D263), the auxiliary supplied (D53); `allo intorno`, round about, as II.13's `all'intorno`.

**`e a tale viene voglia di ajutarti, sendo in su l'arme, che abbandonandoti non ti ajuterebbe mai`.** "And to such a one comes the longing to aid you, [you] being in arms, who, [you] abandoning yourself, would never aid you": `aiutare`, aid (D348); `voglia`, longing, as above; `in su l'arme`, in arms, under arms. The two gerunds, `sendo` and `abbandonandoti`, have the same unexpressed subject, you, set against each other: armed, you are aided; given up, you would never be. English needs the subject supplied (D53). `abbandonandoti` could also be read "abandoning you", of the helper, which the sense of the period does not favour.

**`Questo s'intende quando tu abbia un inimico`.** "This is understood when you have one enemy": `intendere`, understand (D374); `un`, one, the number that the next member sets against `più`.

**`ma quando ne avessi più`.** "But when you had more": `avessi` may be second or, in Florentine, third person; the second is taken from `tu possedessi` in the next clause, and the bare subjunctive is not given "should" (D348). The 1554 reads `haveſſe` (NM-DISC-II14-D004, D459).

**`rendere delle cose, che tu possedessi, ad alcuno di loro per riguadagnarselo`.** "To render some of the things that you possessed to one of them to regain him [for yourself]": `rendere`, render, give back (D464); `possedere`, possess; `riguadagnare`, regain, of `guadagnare`, gain (glossary); `se`, for yourself, bracketed because the English needs the person. The 1554 reads `riſguardarſelo`, which barely construes (NM-DISC-II14-D004, D459).

**`ancora che fusse già scoperta la guerra`.** "Even though the war were already uncovered": the last of the chapter's four uses of `scoprire`; a war uncovered is a war declared, in the open. The 1554 adds `di` (NM-DISC-II14-D004).

**`e per smembrarlo dagli altri confederati tuoi inimici`.** "And to dismember him from the other confederates your enemies": `smembrare`, dismember, detach a member from the body of the league; `confederato`, confederate (D464), with Guicciardini's `confederazione` at II.13 (D452) and Livy's `foederatos`. Without a comma, as printed.

**`fia sempre partito prudente`.** "Will always be a prudent course": `partito`, course (D236); `prudente`, prudent (glossary); `fia`, will be, the future. The 1554 reads `ſia`, be, with the long s for the f (NM-DISC-II14-D004, D459). The chapter ends on the counsel for many enemies, which the Italian sets after the counsel for one; neither is subordinated to the other.
