# Discourses II.10

## NM-DISC.2.10.01

Because each one can begin a war at his pleasure, but not finish it, a prince must, before he takes up an enterprise, measure his forces, and govern himself according to them. But he must have such prudence that he does not deceive himself about his forces; and he will deceive himself every time that he measures them either by money, or by the site, or by the benevolence of men, lacking, on the other hand, arms of his own. For the things aforesaid do indeed increase your forces, but they do not give them to you, and by themselves they are nothing, and do not help in any thing without faithful arms. For much money is not enough for you without them, the strength of the country does not help you, and the faith and benevolence of men does not last, because these cannot be faithful to you, you not being able to defend them. Every mountain, every lake, every inaccessible place becomes [a] plain where strong defenders fail. Money, too, not only does not defend you, but makes you be plundered the sooner.

## NM-DISC.2.10.02

Nor can that common opinion be more false which says that money is the sinew of war. Which sentence is said by Quintus Curtius in the war that was between Antipater the Macedonian and the Spartan king; where he narrates that for defect of money the king of Sparta was necessitated to engage, and was routed; for, had he deferred the engagement a few days, the news of the death of Alexander would have come into Greece, whence he would have remained victor without fighting. But, money failing him, and doubting lest his army, for defect of it, should abandon him, he was constrained to try the fortune of the engagement. So that Quintus Curtius, for this cause, affirms money to be the sinew of war. Which sentence is cited every day and, by princes not so prudent as would be enough, followed. For, having founded themselves upon it, they believe that to have much treasure is enough for them to defend themselves, and they do not think that, if treasure were enough to win, Darius would have vanquished Alexander, the Greeks would have vanquished the Romans, in our times Duke Charles would have vanquished the Swiss, and, a few days ago, the pope and the Florentines together would not have had difficulty in vanquishing Francesco Maria, nephew of Pope Julius II, in the war of Urbino. But all the above-named were vanquished by those who esteem not money but good soldiers to be the sinew of war.

## NM-DISC.2.10.03

Among the other things that Croesus, king of Lydia, showed to Solon the Athenian was an innumerable treasure; and, [Croesus] asking what seemed to him of his power, Solon answered him that for that he did not judge him more powerful, because war was made with steel and not with gold, and that one might come who had more steel than he, and take it away from him. Beyond this, when, after the death of Alexander the Great, a multitude of the French passed into Greece, and then into Asia, and, the French sending orators to the king of Macedonia to treat of a certain accord, that king, to show his power and to frighten them, showed them much gold and silver; whence those French, who already held the peace as firm, broke it; so much desire grew in them to take that gold away from him. And so that king was despoiled through that thing which he had accumulated for his defence. The Venetians, a few years ago, having their treasury still full of treasure, lost all the state, without being able to be defended by it.

## NM-DISC.2.10.04

I say, on that account, that not gold, as the common opinion cries, is the sinew of war, but good soldiers; because gold is not sufficient to find good soldiers, but good soldiers are indeed sufficient to find gold. For the Romans, if they had wished to make war more with money than with steel, it would not have been enough to have all the treasure of the world, considering the great enterprises that they made and the difficulties that they had in them. But, making their wars with steel, they never suffered dearth of gold, because by those who feared them gold was carried even into the camps.

## NM-DISC.2.10.05

And if that Spartan king, through dearth of money, had to try the fortune of the engagement, there befell him, on account of money, that which has many times befallen through other causes; for it has been seen that, the victuals failing an army, and [the men] being necessitated either to die of hunger or to engage, the course of engaging is always taken, as being more honourable, and [the course] where fortune can in some way favour you. Again, it has befallen many times that a captain, seeing succour come to the army [that is] his enemy, it is fitting for him either to engage with it and try the fortune of the engagement, or, waiting until it grows in force, to have to fight in any case, with a thousand disadvantages of his own. Again, it has been seen, as befell Hasdrubal when in the Marches he was assaulted by Claudius Nero together with the other Roman consul, that a captain who is necessitated either to flee or to fight always chooses fighting, it seeming to him that in this course, even though most doubtful, he can win, and in that other he has to lose in any case. There are, then, many necessities that make a captain, outside his intention, take the course of engaging, and among which the dearth of money can sometimes be; nor for this must money be judged to be the sinew of war, more than the other things that induce men to like necessities.

## NM-DISC.2.10.06

Gold, then, to repeat it anew, is not the sinew of war, but good soldiers. Money is indeed necessary in the second place, but it is a necessity which good soldiers by themselves vanquish; because it is impossible that money should fail good soldiers, as [it is impossible] that money by itself should find good soldiers. Every history shows in a thousand places that this which we say is true. Notwithstanding that Pericles counselled the Athenians to make war with all the Peloponnese, showing that they could win that war with industry and with the force of money, and albeit in such a war the Athenians prospered sometimes, in the end they lost it, and the counsel and the good soldiers of Sparta were of more worth than the industry and the money of Athens. But Livy is a truer witness of this opinion than any other, where, discoursing, if Alexander the Great had come into Italy, whether he would have vanquished the Romans, he shows three things to be necessary in war, many soldiers and good, prudent captains, and good fortune: where, examining which, either the Romans or Alexander, prevailed in these things, he afterwards makes his conclusion without ever recalling money. The Campanians, when they were asked by the Sidicini to take up arms for them against the Samnites, must have measured their power by money, and not by soldiers; because, having taken the course of aiding them, after two routs they were constrained to make themselves tributaries of the Romans, if they wished to save themselves.

## Notes

**The rubric.** The 1824 prints CAPITOLO X and, in italic, `I danari non sono il nervo della guerra, secondo che è la comune opinione.` "Money is not the sinew of war, as is the common opinion": the rubric denies the opinion that it names, and the English keeps the Italian's order and its looseness; the chapter shows which way to read it (.02 `quella comune opinione che dice, che i danari sono il nervo della guerra`). The 1554 heads the chapter the same way (NM-DISC-II10-D007), and Canestrini sets the same words over Guicciardini's reply (D430).

**`danari`, `danaro`; `il nervo della guerra`.** "Money"; "the sinew of war". Machiavelli's plural `danari` and his singular `danaro` (.02, .06) are both "money", a recorded collapse; `oro`, gold, and `tesoro`, treasure, are kept apart from it, since the chapter moves among all three. `nervo` is singular, "sinew", not the proverbial English plural.

**`a sua posta`.** "At his pleasure": when he chooses.

**`ed ogni volta s'ingannerà quando le misuri o dai danari, o dal sito, o dalla benivolenza degli uomini`.** "And he will deceive himself every time that he measures them either by money, or by the site, or by the benevolence of men": `ingannarsi`, deceive oneself (glossary); `sito`, site (glossary); `benivolenza`, benevolence. The three false measures are the three things taken in turn in the next two periods: money, the strength of the country, the faith and benevolence of men. The 1554 runs the clauses together (`non s'ingannera, quando`) and has no money in the list, the measure the chapter is about (NM-DISC-II10-D001, D428).

**`mancando dall'altra parte d'armi proprie`.** "Lacking, on the other hand, arms of his own": `mancare di`, lack (D312); `dall'altra parte`, on the other hand (D24). `armi proprie` is the term the chapter turns on, held as "arms of one's own"; Guicciardini's `soldati proprii` is "soldiers of his own" (Guicciardini .01).

**`le cose predette ti accrescono bene le forze, ma le non te le danno`.** "The things aforesaid do indeed increase your forces, but they do not give them to you": `accrescere`, increase (D69); `forze`, forces (glossary). The second person enters here and runs to `ti fanno predare`; the English keeps it. The doubled `le` is the Florentine subject pronoun, not rendered twice.

**`non ti giova la fortezza del paese`.** "The strength of the country does not help you": `fortezza` here is neither the virtue, fortitude (FG I.10), nor a stronghold, fortress (D312), but what mountains, lakes and passes give a country, as the next period shows. "Strength", a third sense of the word decided by sense, as `fazione` (D432).

**`diventa piano, dove i forti difensori mancano`.** "Becomes [a] plain where strong defenders fail": `piano`, flat, open ground, the noun supplied so that "plain" is not read as "evident"; `mancare`, fail (D206).

**`ma ti fanno predare più presto`.** "But makes you be plundered the sooner": `predare`, to take as prey; `più presto` without a following `che` is "sooner", not D135's "rather than".

**`La quale sentenza è detta da Quinto Curzio nella guerra`.** "Which sentence is said by Quintus Curtius in the war": `sentenza`, sentence, an opinion pronounced (glossary); the 1554 reads `e dato` (NM-DISC-II10-D005, D428). On the editor's identification, the Spartan king is Agis III, defeated by Antipater in 331 BC, whose war Quintus Curtius Rufus tells; Alexander died in 323, and the chronology of the news is Machiavelli's, not corrected. The editor notes that the phrase occurs in Cicero's Fifth Philippic, *nervos belli, pecuniam*; the attribution to Curtius is Machiavelli's.

**`che se ei differiva la zuffa pochi giorni, veniva la nuova`.** "For, had he deferred the engagement a few days, there would have come ... the news ... would have come": the imperfects carry the unreal condition, as Italian allows and English does not; the clause is exposition and goes into plain order (D263); `differire`, defer (D334); `zuffa`, engagement (D49).

**`dubitando che lo esercito suo per difetto di quelli non lo abbandonasse`.** "Doubting lest his army, for defect of it, should abandon him": `dubitare che ... non`, apprehension (D205); with `non` and a feared outcome, "doubt lest", as in the proem to this book (`dubitando non incorrere`; D431); `difetto`, defect (glossary).

**`da' Principi non tanto prudenti che basti, seguitata`.** "By princes not so prudent as would be enough, followed": the participle held to the end, as printed; `bastare`, be enough (D205); `seguitare`, follow, the verb Guicciardini's reply opens with (`chi l'ha poi seguitata`). `allegare`, cite, in its old sense of adducing an authority, not "allege" (D431).

**`Dario ... il Duca Carlo ... il Papa e i Fiorentini ... Francesco Maria`.** On the editor's identifications: Darius III, beaten by Alexander; Charles the Bold, duke of Burgundy, beaten by the Swiss in 1476-77; Pope Leo X and Florence against Francesco Maria della Rovere, duke of Urbino, nephew of Julius II, in the war of 1517. `pochi giorni sono`, "a few days ago", suggests to the editor that the chapter was written close to that war.

**`vincere`.** "Vanquish" of an enemy, and of the necessity in .06; "win" of a war (.06 `vincere quella guerra`) and without an object (.02, .05), as D313 (D431).

**`fu un tesoro innumerabile; e domandando quel che gli pareva della potenza sua`.** "Was an innumerable treasure; and, [Croesus] asking what seemed to him of his power": the gerund has no stated subject; the asker is Croesus, and the bracket names him, since an English participle would attach to Solon.

**`perché la guerra si faceva col ferro e non con l'oro`.** "Because war was made with steel and not with gold": `ferro` is "the steel" in the glossary (D402, NM I.58, the sword as remedy); here and at .04 it is the metal of arms counted against the metal of money, and the article is dropped (D433).

**`una moltitudine di Francesi`.** "A multitude of the French": Machiavelli's name for the Gauls, kept and noted (names.md, as NM I.7); on the editor's identification, the invasion of Macedonia and Greece in 280-279 BC, and the crossing into Asia. The king of Macedonia is not named.

**`I Veneziani pochi anni sono`.** "The Venetians, a few years ago": on the editor's identification, the defeat at Agnadello in 1509; `tutto lo Stato`, "all the state", is Machiavelli's and is not narrowed. `erario`, treasury, the public chest; `tesoro`, treasure.

**`Dico pertanto non l'oro, come grida la comune opinione, essere il nervo della guerra, ma i buoni soldati`.** "I say, on that account, that not gold, as the common opinion cries, is the sinew of war, but good soldiers": `pertanto`, on that account (D98); `gridare`, cry, as the opinion is said aloud. `sufficiente`, sufficient, kept apart from `bastare`, be enough (D205): the chiasmus with gold and good soldiers is held, and it is the sentence Guicciardini turns round (Guicciardini .01).

**`Ai Romani, s'egli avessero voluto fare la guerra più coi danari che con il ferro`.** "For the Romans, if they had wished to make war more with money than with steel": `egli`, plural, as printed. The 1554 reads `ſe non haueſſero uoluto`, which reverses the condition; the next period answers the 1824's (NM-DISC-II10-D002, D428). `considerato le grandi imprese`, "considering the great enterprises", the participle unagreed before its object; the 1554 `conſiderate` (NM-DISC-II10-D005, D428).

**`E se quel Re spartano per carestia di danari ebbe a tentare la fortuna della zuffa`.** "And if that Spartan king, through dearth of money, had to try the fortune of the engagement": `carestia`, dearth, the same word as `carestia dell'oro` in .04; `avere a`, necessity, "have to" (D261); `intervenire`, befall (D298), three times in .05.

**`si piglia il partito sempre d'azzuffarsi, per essere più onorevole, e dove la fortuna ti può in qualche modo favorire`.** "The course of engaging is always taken, as being more honourable, and [the course] where fortune can in some way favour you": `partito`, course (D236), three times in the paragraph; `pigliare`, take (glossary). The second reason is a place, `dove`, and the bracket supplies its noun.

**`che veggendo un Capitano al suo esercito nimico venire soccorso, gli conviene o azzuffarsi con quello`.** "That a captain, seeing succour come to the army [that is] his enemy, it is fitting for him either to engage with it": the sentence changes construction after its subject, and the English keeps the break; `in ogni modo`, "in any case", here and below (D431); `convenire`, impersonal, to be fitting (D85); `ingrossare`, grow in force, with `grosso` of an army (D238).

**`come intervenne ad Asdrubale quando nella Marca fu assaltato da Claudio Nerone, insieme con l'altro Consolo romano`.** On the editor's identification, Hasdrubal, Hannibal's brother, at the Metaurus in 207 BC, against the consuls Gaius Claudius Nero and Marcus Livius Salinator; the second is not named. `la Marca`, the Marches.

**`che un Capitano che è necessitato o a fuggirsi o a combattere, come sempre elegge il combattere`.** "That a captain who is necessitated either to flee or to fight always chooses fighting": `come` after `che` repeats the conjunction and is not rendered; `Capitano`, captain (glossary); `eleggere`, choose (glossary); `dubbiosissimo`, most doubtful.

**`fuori della sua intenzione`.** "Outside his intention": against his purpose. `necessitadi`, necessities, one root held with `necessitare`, necessitate, `necessità`, necessity, and `necessario`, necessary, through .05 and .06.

**`Non è adunque, replicandolo di nuovo, l'oro il nervo della guerra, ma i buoni soldati`.** "Gold, then, to repeat it anew, is not the sinew of war, but good soldiers": the restatement of .04, in plain order (D263), the gerund given as an infinitive so that it does not attach to "gold"; `adunque`, then (D74); `replicare`, repeat (D423); `di nuovo`, anew (glossary). The 1554 adds `e` before `l'oro` (NM-DISC-II10-D006, D428).

**`ma è una necessità, che i soldati buoni per sé medesimi la vincono`.** "But it is a necessity that good soldiers by themselves vanquish": `la` is the necessity, and the relative keeps the claim from reading "it is necessary that good soldiers win"; `vincere` of a difficulty (D313).

**`come che i danari per loro medesimi trovino i buoni soldati`.** "As [it is] that money by itself should find good soldiers": `come che` compares the second impossibility with the first, and the bracket supplies what the Italian carries over.

**`Mostra, questo che noi diciamo essere vero, ogni istoria in mille luoghi`.** "Every history shows in a thousand places that this which we say is true": exposition, in plain order (D263); `istoria`, history (glossary).

**`mostrando che e' potevano vincere quella guerra con la industria e con la forza del danaro`.** "Showing that they could win that war with industry and with the force of money": Pericles' counsel, on the editor's identification Thucydides 1.140-144. The 1554 reads `& non con la forZa del danaio`, which makes Pericles advise against money; the close of the period, `che la industria ed il danaro di Atene`, answers the 1824's reading (NM-DISC-II10-D003, D428). `industria`, industry, skill and diligence, not manufacture. `benché`, albeit (D31); `tale guerra`, "such a war".

**`Ma Tito Livio è di questa opinione più vero testimone che alcun altro`.** "But Livy is a truer witness of this opinion than any other": on the editor's identification, the digression on Alexander in Livy 9.17-19; `discorrere`, discourse (D01); `testimone`, witness, as NM I.60 and the proem's `testimonj`; `ricordare`, recall, mention. The three things are Livy's (9.17); the editor has not checked whether Livy names money there.

**`Doverono i Campani ... misurare la potenza loro dai danari, e non dai soldati`.** "The Campanians ... must have measured their power by money, and not by soldiers": `dovere`, must (D274), here "must have", of what Machiavelli infers they did (D431). On the editor's identification, Livy 7.29-31: the Campanians took up the Sidicini's cause against the Samnites, were beaten twice, and gave themselves into Roman power. The 1554 names them `Capouani`, by their city, Capua (NM-DISC-II10-D004, D428). `tributarj`, tributaries; `salvarsi`, save oneself (glossary). The unit ends with a stop.
