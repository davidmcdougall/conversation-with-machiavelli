# Discourses I.24

*Well-ordered republics constitute rewards and penalties for their citizens, nor do they ever compensate the one with the other.*

## NM-DISC.1.24.01

The merits of Horatius had been very great, he having with his *virtù* overcome the Curiatii. His fault had been atrocious, he having slain his sister. Nonetheless such a homicide so displeased the Romans that they brought him to dispute for his life, notwithstanding that his merits were so great and so fresh. Which thing, to whoever should consider it superficially, would seem an example of popular ingratitude.

## NM-DISC.1.24.02

Nonetheless whoever examines it better, and with better consideration searches out what the orders of republics must be, will blame that people rather for having absolved him than for having wished to condemn him; and the reason is this, that no well-ordered republic ever cancelled the demerits with the merits of its citizens, but, having ordered the rewards for a good work and the penalties for a bad one, and having rewarded one for having worked well, if that same one works ill afterwards, it chastises him without having any regard to his good works.

## NM-DISC.1.24.03

And when these orders are well observed, a city lives free much time; otherwise it will always fall to ruin soon. For if, to a citizen who has done some outstanding work for the city, there is added, beyond the reputation which that thing brings him, an audacity and confidence of being able, without fearing penalty, to do some work not good, he will become in a short time so insolent that all civility will dissolve. It is indeed necessary, wishing that the penalty be feared for wicked works, to observe the rewards for good ones, as one sees that Rome did. And albeit a republic be poor, and be able to give little, it must not abstain from that little; for always every small gift, given to someone in recompense of good, even though great, will be esteemed by him who receives it honourable and very great.

## NM-DISC.1.24.04

The history of Horatius Cocles is very well known, and that of Mucius Scaevola: how the one withstood the enemies upon a bridge until it should be cut; the other burned his hand, having erred, wishing to kill Porsena, king of the Tuscans. To these, for these two works so outstanding, two *staiora* of land were donated by the public to each. The history of Manlius Capitolinus is also known. To him, for having saved the Capitol from the Gauls who were encamped there, there was given by those who were besieged within together with him a small measure of meal. Which reward, according to the fortune that then ran in Rome, was great, and of such quality that, Manlius being moved later either by envy or by his bad nature to make sedition arise in Rome, and seeking to gain the people for himself, he was, without any respect to his merits, thrown headlong from that Capitol which he before, with so much glory of his own, had saved.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Le Repubbliche bene ordinate constituiscono premj e pene a' loro cittadini, né compensano mai l'uno con l'altro`. "Well-ordered republics constitute rewards and penalties for their citizens, nor do they ever compensate the one with the other": `constituire`, constitute (glossary); `premj` / `pene`, rewards / penalties (glossary, I.3). `Compensare`, to set one against the other so that they cancel, is the word Guicciardini takes up three times; the body says `cancellò` (.02), "cancelled".

**`i meriti di Orazio`.** "The merits of Horatius": Publius Horatius, the surviving Roman champion of the combat Machiavelli discussed in I.22 and I.23 (Livy 1.24-26). Returning in triumph, he killed his sister, who was mourning one of the Curiatii, her betrothed. Livy tells that he was condemned by the duumvirs, appealed to the people, and, after his father's pleas, was absolved more in admiration of his *virtus* than by the justice of the case (1.26).

**`avendo con la sua virtù vinti i Curiazj`.** "He having with his *virtù* overcome the Curiatii": *virtù* in Italian (D16). The two gerunds, `avendo ... vinti` and `avendo morto`, are kept parallel: the merit and the fault in the same form.

**`il fallo suo atroce`.** "His fault ... atrocious": `fallo`, fault; Guicciardini's reply has the verb, of the sister, under a condition: `se lei aveva fallato`.

**`avendo morto la sorella`.** "He having slain his sister": `morto` transitive, slain, kept apart from `ammazzare`, kill (D262). The possessive "his" for the article is English idiom, not a supply.

**`omicidio`.** "Homicide", as D169. Guicciardini uses the same word twice.

**`lo condussero a disputare della vita`.** "They brought him to dispute for his life": he was put on trial for it. `Condurre`, bring or lead.

**`ingratitudine popolare`.** "Popular ingratitude": the ingratitude of a people. Machiavelli treats it at length in I.28-30; the phrase anticipates that discussion.

**`Nondimeno` ... `Nondimeno`.** "Nonetheless" twice in five lines (.01, .02), as the glossary: the first turns from the merits to the trial, the second from appearance to examination. The English repeats.

**`gli ordini delle Repubbliche`.** "The orders of republics": `ordine`, order (glossary), and `ordinata`, `ordinati` in the same period: the well-ordered republic has "ordered" rewards and penalties.

**`più tosto per averlo assoluto, che per averlo voluto condannare`.** "Rather for having absolved him than for having wished to condemn him": the chapter's reversal. The people's fault, if any, was to let the merit cancel the fault.

**`non mai cancellò i demeriti con li meriti`.** "Ever cancelled the demerits with the merits": the double negative `nessuna ... non mai` is Italian idiom and reads as one negative in English.

**`opera`, `operato`, `opera`, `opere`.** "Work", "worked", "works": `operare`, work (D83), so that "having worked well" and "a good work" stay in one root, as in the Italian.

**`lo gastiga`.** "It chastises him": the 1554 edition's `castiga` is the same verb.

**`rovinerà presto`.** "It will fall to ruin soon": `rovinare` intransitive; `rovina`, ruin (glossary). `molto tempo`, "much time", in the sentence before, kept apart from `lungo tempo`, "a long time" (D262). The 1554 edition reads `tosto`, the same.

**`egregia opera`, `opere tanto egregie`.** "Outstanding work", "works so outstanding": English "egregious" now means the opposite.

**`audacia e confidenza`.** "Audacity and confidence": confidence in the older sense of assurance, here presumption.

**`si risolverà ogni civiltà`.** "All civility will dissolve": `civiltà`, civility, civil life under laws (glossary, I.2); `risolvere`, dissolve.

**`osservare i premj`.** "To observe the rewards": to keep them, as orders are "observed" in the sentence before.

**`come si vede che fece Roma`.** "As one sees that Rome did". The 1554 edition reads `si uide`, "as was seen" (NM-DISC-I24-D003).

**`E benché una Repubblica sia povera`.** "And albeit a republic be poor": `benché`, albeit (D31).

**`debbe di quel poco non astenersi`.** "It must not abstain from that little": `debbe`, must (D120). The 1554 edition prints `debbe di di quel poco astenersi`, without `non`, [*sic*], which says the opposite (NM-DISC-I24-D001).

**`per ricompensa di bene, ancora che grande`.** "In recompense of good, even though great": `ancora che`, even though (glossary). `grande` most naturally attaches to `bene`: the good done may be great and the gift small, as .04 then shows. Both the 1824 and the 1554 editions print `grande`, and both cold readers read it so; the English, like the Italian, leaves the attachment to the reader. The 1554 reads `ricompenso`, the masculine form of the noun; the reading was put to David under D203(b) and `ricompensa` stands (D248; NM-DISC-I24-D002).

**`onorevole e grandissimo`.** "Honourable and very great": the elative answers "very great" of Horatius' merits (.01).

**`Orazio Cocle`.** Horatius Cocles held the bridge over the Tiber against Porsena's army until the Romans cut it behind him (Livy 2.10). Not the Horatius of .01.

**`Muzio Scevola`.** Gaius Mucius Scaevola, who entered Porsena's camp to kill him, killed his secretary instead, and held his right hand in the fire before the king (Livy 2.12-13). `avendo errato`, "having erred": he mistook the man.

**`Porsena Re delli Toscani`.** "Porsena, king of the Tuscans": Lars Porsena of Clusium, king of the Etruscans; "Tuscans" for Machiavelli's `Toscani`, as he names them.

**`tantoché si tagliasse`.** "Until it should be cut": `tantoché`, so long that; the subjunctive keeps the prospect.

**`due stajora di terra`.** "Two *staiora* of land": the `staioro` is a Tuscan measure of land, the area sown with a `staio` of seed. Livy gives Cocles as much land as he could plough round in a day, and Scaevola land across the Tiber (2.10, 2.13); Livy gives no measure; the two *staiora* each are Machiavelli's.

**`fu donato dal Pubblico`.** "Were donated by the public": `donare`, the verb of `dono`, "gift" (.03), kept apart from `dato`, "given", of Manlius' meal; `Pubblico`, the commonwealth. Guicciardini's `dal publico` is the same word.

**`Manlio Capitolino`.** Marcus Manlius Capitolinus, who saved the Capitol from the Gauls (390 BC on the traditional reckoning), roused by the geese; the besieged gave him each a half-pound of meal and a little wine (Livy 5.47). He was later condemned for aiming at kingship and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (Livy 6.20). Machiavelli told his calumnies in I.8. The 1554 edition prints `Mallio` (NM-DISC-I24-D005).

**`che vi erano a campo`.** "Who were encamped there": besieging the Capitol.

**`secondo la fortuna che allora correva in Roma`.** "According to the fortune that then ran in Rome": in the straits of the siege, a measure of meal was much. `Correre`, run: the simple verb of the *currere* family (D01).

**`e di qualità che mosso poi Manlio`.** "And of such quality that, Manlius being moved later": the consecutive `che` opens a period that changes subject; Machiavelli's point is that the reward did not buy him exemption.

**`senza rispetto alcuno de' suoi meriti`.** "Without any respect to his merits": `rispetto` with the glossary's "respect to"; the rule's `senza avere riguardo alcuno` (.02), "without having any regard", is executed in the same shape, two words as in the Italian.

**`gittato precipite`.** "Thrown headlong": `precipite`, a Latinism.

**`ch'egli prima con tanta sua gloria aveva salvato`.** "Which he before, with so much glory of his own, had saved": the chapter ends on the Capitol, saved and then the place of his death, the merit and the penalty in one place, not compensated. Livy makes the same observation of the place (6.20.12); Machiavelli's `da quello Campidoglio` compresses the Tarpeian rock into the Capitol.
