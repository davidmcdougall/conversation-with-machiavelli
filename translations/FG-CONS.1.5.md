# Consideration on chapter V of Book I

*Where the guard of liberty is more securely set, whether in the people or in
the great, and which have greater cause to make tumult, whoever wishes to
acquire, or whoever wishes to maintain*

## FG-CONS.1.5.01

I do not understand the title of the question, that is, what it means, the
setting of the guard of liberty either in the people or in the great, for it is
one thing to say in whom the government is to be, either in the great or in the
plebs, and to this the example of Venice serves, for it is in the nobles in
such a way that the whole plebs is excluded from it, it is another thing to say,
everyone partaking of the government, in whom a particular authority for the
defence of liberty is to be, either in a magistracy of plebeian men or of noble
men; and to this the example of Rome can serve, where, both the nobles and the
plebs partaking, the magistracy of the tribunes, which seemed to have a
particular guard of liberty, was in the plebeians. Albeit, to speak better, in
Rome the guard of liberty was not less in the patricians than in the
plebeians, for both the consuls and the dictators had care of it and authority
to defend liberty, as is seen both in Spurius Maelius and in Manlius
Capitolinus, of whom, for laying snares for liberty, the one was killed, the
other put in prison by the dictators; and in the latter times the sedition of
the Gracchi, and the conspiracy of Catiline, was oppressed by the consuls. The
authority of accusing, too, was common alike to the patricians and to the
plebeians, and the other magistrates as well as the tribunes could call a man to
judgment, [the tribunes] who were not created to defend liberty against whoever might wish
to oppress the whole republic, but only for the defence of the plebs against
whoever wished to oppress it, and although the tribunes more often called the
citizens to judgment, they did so because, being a plebeian magistracy, they
had more credit with the plebs, and it seemed in a certain way that this was
properly their office. But as to the title of the question, I shall always
praise, more than all other governments, a mixed government as above, and in
a like government I shall wish that the guard of liberty against whoever might
wish to oppress the republic belong to all, fleeing always, as far as one can,
the distinction between nobles and plebeians, and of necessity a mixed
government is tempered in such a way that, in favour of liberty, the one order
is guard of the other.

## FG-CONS.1.5.02

But if I were necessitated to put in a city either a government merely of
nobles or a government of plebs, I shall believe it less error to make it of
nobles, for, there being more prudence in it, and its having more quality,
one will be able to hope more that they may put themselves into some reasonable
form than in a plebs, which, being full of ignorance and of confusion and of
many ill qualities, one cannot hope will do other than precipitate and shatter
everything. Nor shall I proceed with that distinction: either you wish to make
a republic that acquires, or one that conserves, for the government of the
plebs is neither for acquiring nor for conserving, and the government of Rome
was mixed, not plebeian. And this conclusion is according to the sentence of
all those who have written of republics, who set the government of the
optimates before that of the multitude.

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply,
without the 1824 *Discorsi*'s capitals, with `securamente` for `sicuramente`,
with a comma for the 1824 semicolon after `grandi` and none after `popolo`, and
without a full stop. The English follows his punctuation.

**The paragraphs.** Canestrini prints this reply in two paragraphs, and they
are kept: the question taken apart and answered, then the forced choice.

**`Io non intendo`.** "I do not understand": the verb of FG I.3 `intese`
(D92). He does not say the question is wrong; he says he cannot tell which of
two questions it is.

**`il porre la guardia`.** "The setting of the guard": he quotes the rubric's
verb, `si ponga`.

**`altro è a dire ..., altro è dire`.** "It is one thing to say ..., it is
another thing to say": the distinction between who governs and who keeps a
particular charge of liberty, held in one period. The second drops the `a` of
the first; English cannot show it.

**`chi ha a essere`.** "In whom ... is to be": `avere a` is "be to" (D61).

**`participando`.** "Partaking", as `partecipare di` in I.2: everyone has a
share in the government.

**`particulare`.** "Particular": a charge belonging to one magistracy (D93).

**`Benché`.** "Albeit" (D31), here opening a period: he corrects what he has
just said of the tribunes.

**`e e' consuli e e' dittatori`.** "Both the consuls and the dictators": the
first `e` is "both", the second the plural article (D35).

**`n'avevano cura`.** "Had care of it": of liberty. The 1933 edition reads
`v'avevano`.

**Spurius Maelius, Manlius Capitolinus.** Maelius, a rich plebeian who sold
grain cheaply in a famine, was accused of aiming at kingship and killed in
439 BC by Servilius Ahala, master of the horse to the dictator Cincinnatus.
Marcus Manlius, who had saved the Capitol from the Gauls, was imprisoned by
the dictator Cornelius Cossus in 385 BC, and later condemned and thrown from
the Tarpeian rock. Machiavelli names both in other chapters.

**`insidiare alla libertà`.** "Laying snares for liberty": plotting against
it.

**The Gracchi, Catiline.** Tiberius Gracchus was killed in 133 BC by a crowd of
senators led by a private man, Scipio Nasica; his brother Gaius in 121 BC under
the senate's decree, with the consul Opimius acting. Catiline's conspiracy was
put down in 63 BC by the consul Cicero. Guicciardini's "by the consuls" holds
best for the last.

**`fu oppressa`.** "Was oppressed": put down, crushed, in the older sense of
the word. The paragraph has the verb three more times, for whoever would
oppress the republic (twice) and for those who would oppress the plebs; the
English keeps one verb throughout, as the Italian does.

**`chi volesse ... chi volessi opprimere`.** "Whoever might wish to oppress",
both times: the tribunes were not made against such a man, and the guard of
all is.

**`promiscua`.** "Common alike": held by both orders.

**`così potevano chiamare ... gli altri magistrati come e' tribuni, i quali`.**
`Così ... come` is "as well as". The English repeats "[the tribunes]" so that
`i quali`, "who", keeps its antecedent.

**`chiamare ... in giudicio`.** "Call ... to judgment": to arraign before the
people.

**`plebeio`.** "Plebeian": both Canestrini and the 1933 edition print the
form in -eio.

**`credito`.** "Credit", as in I.12.

**`ufficio`.** "Office" (D56).

**`loderò ... vorrò`.** "I shall praise ... I shall wish": future, as in the
Italian. In .02 `crederò`, "I shall believe": the act of assent (`credo`) put
into the future, for a case he hopes not to meet.

**`governo misto come di sopra`.** "A mixed government as above": FG I.2,
where he grants the mixture and designs one on the Venetian model.

**`fuggendo`.** "Fleeing": avoiding the distinction of orders that, in I.4, he
made the cause of Rome's disunions.

**`l'uno ordine è guardia dell'altro`.** "The one order is guard of the
other": his answer to the title. No one order keeps liberty; each keeps the
other.

**`uno governo simile`.** "A like government": `simile` is "like" (glossary).

**`essendovi più prudenza`.** "There being more prudence in it": in a
government of nobles.

**`necessitato`.** "Necessitated": compelled, with `per necessità`, "of
necessity", at the end of .01.

**`qualità`.** "Quality": the nobles have "more quality", the plebs "many ill
qualities". Machiavelli's "which quality of men" (.04) uses the same word.

**`male qualità`.** "Ill qualities": `male`, adjective or noun, is "ill" in
Guicciardini (FG I.2).

**`precipiti e conquassi`.** "Precipitate and shatter": throw headlong and
break apart.

**`o tu vuoi fare una republica che acquisti, o una che conservi`.** His
restatement of Machiavelli's verdict (`o tu ragioni di una Repubblica, che
voglia fare uno Imperio, ... o d'una che le basti mantenersi`) in the words of
Machiavelli's second question: "acquire" is from the rubric and .04–.05, and
"maintain" becomes "conserve". "You are reasoning of" becomes "you wish to
make". The English keeps his words, not Machiavelli's.

**`non è né per acquistare, né per conservare`.** "Is neither for acquiring nor
for conserving": not made for either. `Essere per` with an infinitive is "be
about to" where it looks forward (D45, `era per opprimere`); here it states
what a thing is fit for, and "about to" would misread it (D116).

**`la sentenza`.** "The sentence": an opinion pronounced, in the older sense.
`Opinione` is "opinion" (D02) and `giudicio` "judgment"; this is the third
word.

**`ottimati`.** "Optimates" (D76).

**`prepongono`.** "Set ... before": prefer, placing first.
