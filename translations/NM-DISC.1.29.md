# Discourses I.29

*Which is more ungrateful, either a people or a prince.*

## NM-DISC.1.29.01

It seems to me to the purpose of the above-written matter to discourse which uses this ingratitude with greater examples, either a people or a prince. And to dispute this part better I say that this vice of ingratitude arises either from avarice or from suspicion.

## NM-DISC.1.29.02

For when either a people or a prince has sent out a captain of his on an important expedition, where that captain, winning it, has acquired much glory from it, that prince or that people is bound in return to reward him; and if in place of a reward either he dishonours him or he offends him, moved by avarice, not wishing, held back by this cupidity, to satisfy him, he makes an error that has no excuse, indeed draws after him an eternal infamy. Yet many princes are found who sin in it, and Cornelius Tacitus tells the cause with this sentence: *Proclivius est injuriae, quam beneficio vicem exsolvere, quia gratia oneri, ultio in quaestu habetur.* But when he does not reward him, or, to say better, offends him, moved not by avarice but by suspicion, then both the people and the prince merit some excuse.

## NM-DISC.1.29.03

And of these ingratitudes used for such a cause many are read; for that captain who has virtuously acquired an empire for his lord, overcoming the enemies, and filling himself with glory and his soldiers with riches, of necessity, both with his own soldiers and with the enemies and with the subjects proper to that prince, acquires so much reputation that that victory cannot taste good to that lord who has sent him. And because the nature of men is ambitious and suspicious, and does not know how to set a measure to any fortune of its own, it is impossible that that suspicion which arises at once in the prince after the victory of that captain of his should not be increased by that same man, through some way or bearing of his used insolently. So that the prince cannot think of anything other than securing himself of him; and to do this he thinks either of having him die, or of taking away from him the reputation which he has gained for himself in his army and in his peoples, and, with every industry, of showing that that victory arose not through his *virtù* but through fortune, or through the cowardice of the enemies, or through the prudence of the other captains who were with him in such an action.

## NM-DISC.1.29.04

After Vespasian, being in Judaea, was declared emperor by his army, Antonius Primus, who was with another army in Illyria, took his part, and came thence into Italy against Vitellius, who was reigning at Rome, and most virtuously broke two Vitellian armies and occupied Rome; so that Mucianus, sent by Vespasian, found, through the *virtù* of Antonius, the whole acquired and every difficulty vanquished. The reward that Antonius carried away from it was that Mucianus at once took away from him the obedience of the army, and little by little brought him, in Rome, to [being] without any authority; so that Antonius went to find Vespasian, who was still in Asia, by whom he was received in such a way that, in a short time brought to no degree, he died almost in despair. And the histories are full of these examples.

## NM-DISC.1.29.05

In our times, each one who lives at present knows with how much industry and *virtù* Consalvo Ferrante, soldiering in the Kingdom of Naples against the French for Ferdinand, king of Aragon, conquered and won that kingdom, and how, for the reward of victory, he carried away from it that Ferdinand departed from Aragon and, having come to Naples, first removed from him the obedience of the men-at-arms, and afterwards took away from him the fortresses, and thereupon led him away with him into Spain, where after a little time he died unhonoured. So natural, then, is this suspicion in princes, that they cannot defend themselves from it, and it is impossible that they should use gratitude to those who, with victory, have made great acquisitions under their ensigns.

## NM-DISC.1.29.06

And from what a prince does not defend himself, it is no miracle, nor a thing worthy of greater consideration, if a people does not defend itself from it. For a city that lives free having two ends, the one to acquire, the other to maintain itself free, it is fitting that in the one thing and in the other it should err through too much love. As to the errors in acquiring, it will be said of them in its place. As to the errors for maintaining itself free, they are, among the others, these: to offend those citizens whom it ought to reward, to have suspicion of those in whom one ought to confide. And albeit these ways, in a republic come to corruption, are the cause of great ills, and many times it rather comes to tyranny, as befell at Rome with Caesar, who by force took for himself what ingratitude denied him; nonetheless, in a republic not corrupted, they are the cause of great goods, and make it live the more free for it, men maintaining themselves better and less ambitious through fear of punishment.

## NM-DISC.1.29.07

It is true that, among all the peoples that ever had empire, for the causes discoursed above, Rome was the least ungrateful. For of its ingratitude it can be said that there is no other example than that of Scipio; for Coriolanus and Camillus were made exiles for an injury that the one and the other had done to the plebs. But the one was not pardoned, for having always reserved against the people a hostile spirit; the other was not only recalled, but for the whole time of his life adored as a prince.

## NM-DISC.1.29.08

But the ingratitude used towards Scipio arose from a suspicion that the citizens began to have of him, which had not been had of the others, which arose from the greatness of the enemy that Scipio had vanquished, from the reputation that the victory of so long and dangerous a war had given him, from its celerity, from the favours that the youth, the prudence and his other memorable *virtù* acquired for him. Which things were so many that, not to speak of others, the magistrates of Rome feared his authority; which thing displeased wise men, as a thing unaccustomed in Rome. And his living seemed so extraordinary that Cato Priscus, deemed holy, was the first to act against him, and to say that a city could not be called free where there was a citizen who should be feared by the magistrates. So that if the people of Rome followed the opinion of Cato in this case, it merits that excuse which I have said above those peoples and those princes merit who are ungrateful through suspicion.

## NM-DISC.1.29.09

Concluding, then, this discourse, I say that, this vice of ingratitude being used either through avarice or through suspicion, it will be seen how peoples never used it through avarice, and through suspicion much less than princes, having less cause to suspect, as will be said below.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Quale sia più ingrato, o un Popolo, o un Principe`. "Which is more ungrateful, either a people or a prince": `o ... o`, "either ... or", as in the statements of I.5's question (D120). The question was promised at the close of I.28; Guicciardini's reply is headed by the same words in Canestrini's setting.

**`Egli mi pare a proposito della soprascritta materia`.** "It seems to me to the purpose of the above-written matter": `soprascritto`, above-written (glossary, I.16), the matter of I.28; `egli`, the pleonastic subject, not rendered.

**`da discorrere`.** "To discourse": `discorrere`, discourse (D01), the first of the chapter's *currere* words; it returns at `per le cagioni di sopra discorse` (.07) and in `questo discorso` (.09). The 1554 edition reads `di discorrere`.

**`quale usi con maggiori esempj questa ingratitudine`.** "Which uses this ingratitude with greater examples": `usare`, use, the chapter's verb for ingratitude (`ingratitudini usate`, `usino gratitudine`, `l'ingratitudine usata`, `usandosi`, `l'usarono`), kept as one English verb. `maggiori`, greater: more and weightier examples.

**`E per disputare meglio questa parte dico: come questo vizio della ingratitudine nasce`.** "And to dispute this part better I say that this vice of ingratitude arises": `disputare`, dispute, to argue a question; `come` after `dico`, that, introducing the claim; `nascere`, arise (D04).

**`dalla avarizia, o dal sospetto`.** "From avarice or from suspicion": the chapter's two causes, held as "avarice" and "suspicion" throughout and into Guicciardini's reply. `sospetto`, suspicion (glossary, I.28).

**`ha mandato fuori un suo Capitano in una espedizione importante`.** "Has sent out a captain of his on an important expedition": `Capitano`, captain, the commander of an army.

**`vincendola, ne abbia acquistata assai gloria`.** "Winning it, has acquired much glory from it": `assai`, much: of degree, where D254's "very" cannot take a noun; a scoped exception, recorded in the glossary (D312), and held at `assai manco`, "much less" (.09). `gloria`, glory (glossary). The subjunctive marks the case as general; English cannot mark it without a modal that would hedge it. The 1554 edition reads `uincendo`, without the object, and `acquistato`, unagreed; escalated as a word at a letter the scans break and kept (D304; NM-DISC-I29-D003).

**`è tenuto allo incontro a premiarlo`.** "Is bound in return to reward him": `allo incontro`, in return, of what is owed back; kept apart from `al rincontro`, "on the other side" (D24), which is adversative; `premiare`, `premio`, reward (glossary).

**`o ei lo disonora, o ei l'offende`.** "Either he dishonours him or he offends him": `ei`, he, the people or the prince taken as one agent, repeated as in the Italian. `offendere`, offend, in the older sense of injure, as at NM I.28; Guicciardini repeats the pair, `non gli premiando ... o offendendogli` (Guicciardini .01).

**`non volendo ritenuto da questa cupidità satisfargli`.** "Not wishing, held back by this cupidity, to satisfy him": `volere`, wish (D117); `ritenuto`, held back, restrained: a scoped exception to D272's "retain", which would read as keeping (D312); the participle stands between the verb and its infinitive as in the Italian; `cupidità`, cupidity, the avarice named again.

**`fa uno errore che non ha scusa, anzi si tira dietro una infamia eterna`.** "He makes an error that has no excuse, indeed draws after him an eternal infamy": `scusa`, excuse (D173), the chapter's measure, which it grants to suspicion in .02 and to Rome in .08. `anzi` raises the charge, "indeed" (D147). `infamia`, infamy (glossary).

**`Pure si trovano molti Principi che ci peccano`.** "Yet many princes are found who sin in it": `pure`, yet (D191), conceding the rule's frequent breach; `ci`, in it, the avarice.

**`Cornelio Tacito dice con questa sentenza la cagione`.** "Cornelius Tacitus tells the cause with this sentence": `sentenza`, sentence, an opinion pronounced (glossary). Tacitus, *Histories* 4.3, on the victors after Vitellius' fall, who punished Capua and gave Tarracina, which had suffered for their cause, no relief; Tacitus begins *Tanto proclivius*, "so much readier", and Machiavelli drops *tanto*.

**`Proclivius est injuriae, quam beneficio vicem exsolvere, quia gratia oneri, ultio in quaestu habetur`.** Left in Latin, as the witness prints it (D14): "It is readier to repay an injury than a benefit, because gratitude is held a burden, revenge a gain." The 1554 edition misprints the Latin and adds an Italian rendering, which the 1824 does not print (NM-DISC-I29-D007).

**`o a dir meglio l'offende`.** "Or, to say better, offends him": the correction raises not rewarding to offending; it is a correction, not a second case.

**`allora merita e il Popolo e il Principe qualche scusa`.** "Then both the people and the prince merit some excuse": the singular verb with a double subject kept in sense; "some excuse", not "an excuse", against the error "that has no excuse".

**`E di queste ingratitudini usate per tal cagione se ne leggono assai`.** "And of these ingratitudes used for such a cause many are read": `assai`, of number, many (glossary, I.7); the plural `ingratitudini` kept.

**`ha acquistato uno Imperio al suo Signore`.** "Has acquired an empire for his lord": `superare`, overcome, of the enemies; `vincere` of an enemy or a difficulty is "vanquish" (.04, .08), of a thing won "win" (.02, .05). `Imperio`, empire, dominion over others (D124). `Signore`, lord, kept apart from `Principe`, prince, which the same period also uses for him.

**`riempiendo sé di gloria, e gli suoi soldati di ricchezze`.** "Filling himself with glory and his soldiers with riches": one participle with two objects, kept.

**`di necessità e con i soldati suoi, e con i nimici, e con i sudditi proprj di quel Principe acquista tanta riputazione`.** "Of necessity, both with his own soldiers and with the enemies and with the subjects proper to that prince, acquires so much reputation": the catalogue kept before the verb, as in the Italian; `riputazione`, reputation; `di necessità`, of necessity, Machiavelli's claim that the general's standing grows whatever he intends. Whose army and whose peoples are meant at `nel suo esercito, e nei suoi Popoli` below, the captain's or the prince's, the Italian leaves open, and so does the English.

**`che quella vittoria non può sapere di buono a quel Signore`.** "That that victory cannot taste good to that lord": `sapere di`, taste of; the idiom says the victory turns sour to the man it was won for.

**`la natura degli uomini è ambiziosa e sospettosa, e non sa porre modo a nissuna sua fortuna`.** "The nature of men is ambitious and suspicious, and does not know how to set a measure to any fortune of its own": `porre modo` is an idiom, to set a limit or measure, and "way", the glossary's word for `modo`, cannot take it (D311). `sospettosa`, suspicious (glossary). The 1554 edition reads `sospetta`.

**`è impossibile che quel sospetto ... non sia da quel medesimo accresciuto`.** "It is impossible that the suspicion ... should not be increased by that same man": `accrescere`, increase (D69); `quel medesimo`, that same man, the captain, whose insolence feeds the suspicion the victory began. `subito`, at once (D177).

**`per qualche suo modo o termine usato insolentemente`.** "Through some way or bearing of his used insolently": `modo`, way (glossary); `termine`, bearing, his manner of conduct; `insolentemente`, insolently, with `insolenza` (glossary).

**`Talché il Principe non può pensare ad altro che assicurarsene`.** "So that the prince cannot think of anything other than securing himself of him": `talché`, so that (glossary); `assicurarsi di`, secure oneself of (D219).

**`pensa o di farlo morire`.** "He thinks either of having him die": the causative kept; Machiavelli does not say "kill" here.

**`o di torgli la riputazione, che egli si ha guadagnato`.** "Or of taking away from him the reputation which he has gained for himself": `torre`, take away (D131), as `togliere` throughout the chapter; `guadagnare`, gain (glossary). The 1554 edition reads `o ne suoi popoli`, "or among his peoples", for `e nei suoi Popoli`; escalated as a word at a letter the scans break and kept (D304; NM-DISC-I29-D004).

**`e con ogn'industria mostrare`.** "And, with every industry, of showing": `mostrare` is the second infinitive after `di torgli`, and `con ogn'industria` its manner; `industria`, industry, diligence and skill, the word Machiavelli gives Consalvo (.05).

**`per viltà dei nimici`.** "Through the cowardice of the enemies": `viltà` of fighting men is cowardice. The 1554 edition has `per la uilta`.

**`in tale fazione`.** "In such an action": `fazione` here is a military operation, the campaign the captains shared. The glossary's "faction" (FG I.10) is a party in the state; the sense split is ruled provisionally and held for review at the next occurrence of the word (D317): the ruling rests on context, `tale` pointing back to the victory and `sono stati seco in` meaning taking part with him.

**`Poi che Vespasiano sendo in Giudea fu dichiarato dal suo esercito Imperadore`.** "After Vespasian, being in Judaea, was declared emperor by his army": `Imperadore`, emperor (glossary). Vespasian was hailed in July 69 while commanding the war in Judaea; the story that follows is Tacitus' (*Histories* 2-4).

**`Antonio Primo, che si trovava con un altro esercito in Illiria`.** "Antonius Primus, who was with another army in Illyria": Marcus Antonius Primus, legate of the Seventh Legion in Pannonia, part of the Illyrian command, who led the Danube legions into Italy for Vespasian in the autumn of 69.

**`prese le parti sue, e ne venne in Italia`.** "Took his part, and came thence into Italy": Vespasian's part; `ne`, thence.

**`e virtuosissimamente ruppe due eserciti Vitelliani`.** "And most virtuously broke two Vitellian armies": `virtuosissimamente`, the superlative of `virtuosamente` (.03), the adverb of *virtù*, which the English keeps in the virtue family (glossary `virtuoso`). Both readers doubted a mark after `e`, which as `e'` would be the pronoun "he"; read as the conjunction, with the 1554 `et` (D303; NM-DISC-I29-D001). Antonius won the second battle of Bedriacum, near Cremona, and took Rome in December 69.

**`talché Muziano mandato da Vespasiano, trovò ... acquistato il tutto`.** "So that Mucianus, sent by Vespasian, found ... the whole acquired": Gaius Licinius Mucianus, governor of Syria, Vespasian's chief partisan, who reached Rome after its fall and took control of the government.

**`gli tolse subito la ubbidienza dello esercito`.** "At once took away from him the obedience of the army": `ubbidienza`, obedience, the command he held by the soldiers' obedience; the same phrase returns of Consalvo, `gli levò la ubbidienza delle genti d'arme` (.05), with `levare`, remove (D131), for `togliere`, take away.

**`lo ridusse in Roma senza alcuna autorità`.** "Brought him, in Rome, to [being] without any authority": `ridurre`, bring (to a state), twice in the paragraph, the second time `ridotto in nessun grado`, "brought to no degree"; `autorità`, authority (glossary).

**`il quale era ancora in Asia`.** "Who was still in Asia": Vespasian was at Alexandria in Egypt; Machiavelli's "Asia" is his. Tacitus (*Histories* 4.80) has Antonius go to Vespasian and be received less warmly than he hoped; he says nothing of a death in despair, and Antonius in fact lived into old age.

**`in breve tempo ridotto in nessun grado, quasi disperato morì`.** "In a short time brought to no degree, he died almost in despair": `grado`, degree (glossary), here standing, rank. The 1554 edition reads `breuissimo tempo`, "a very short time".

**`Consalvo Ferrante`.** Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba, the Great Captain, who conquered the Kingdom of Naples from the French for Ferdinand of Aragon in 1501-04. Machiavelli's form of the name is kept; Guicciardini uses it too, and gives `Consalvo Fernando` at FG I.23.

**`militando nel Regno di Napoli contro ai Francesi per Ferrando re di Ragona`.** "Soldiering in the Kingdom of Naples against the French for Ferdinand, king of Aragon": `militare` here is to serve in war, not D239's "militate". `ciascuno`, each (D161), "each one". Ferdinand II of Aragon came to Naples in 1506, left in 1507 taking Gonzalo with him, and kept him from office; Gonzalo died at Granada in 1515.

**`conquistasse e vincesse quel regno`.** "Conquered and won that kingdom": two verbs, kept.

**`in prima ... e dipoi ... ed appresso`.** "First ... and afterwards ... and thereupon": three steps; `dipoi`, afterwards (D187); `appresso`, thereupon. `levare`, remove; `togliere`, take away; `menare`, lead (D131).

**`dipoi gli tolse le Fortezze`.** "Afterwards took away from him the fortresses": strongholds. The glossary's `fortezza`, "fortitude" (FG I.10), is the virtue; the sense split is recorded (D312).

**`le genti d'arme`.** "The men-at-arms": the heavy cavalry, the established English term for the compound; `genti` alone is "troops" (glossary, I.23).

**`ne lo menò seco in Spagna, dove poco tempo dopo inonorato morì`.** "Led him away with him into Spain, where after a little time he died unhonoured": `inonorato`, unhonoured, against the honour Guicciardini will say Gonzalo had (Guicciardini .04). The 1554 edition reads `poco tempo poi`.

**`È tanto dunque naturale questo sospetto nei Principi`.** "So natural, then, is this suspicion in princes": the conclusion lands on the predicate, and the English keeps the Italian's order (D263, a landing). `dunque`, then, as `adunque` (D74).

**`ch'egli usino gratitudine`.** "That they should use gratitude": `egli`, they, the princes; `usare`, use, the chapter's verb, now of gratitude.

**`sotto le insegne loro`.** "Under their ensigns": the princes' standards.

**`E da quello che non si difende un Principe, non è miracolo`.** "And from what a prince does not defend himself, it is no miracle": the relative comes first, as in the Italian, so that the people's case follows from the prince's; `miracolo`, miracle. The closing `se ne difende` keeps its `ne`, "defend itself from it", the same suspicion.

**`avendo una Città, che vive libera duoi fini`.** "A city that lives free having two ends": `vivere libero`, free living (glossary). The witness prints no comma after `libera`; the English needs none.

**`conviene che nell'una cosa e nell'altra per troppo amore erri`.** "It is fitting that in the one thing and in the other it should err through too much love": `conviene`, it is fitting (D85), of what follows necessarily, not of what is proper; the error is an excess of love, for empire and for liberty.

**`Quanto agli errori nello acquistare, se ne dirà, nel luogo suo`.** "As to the errors in acquiring, it will be said of them in its place": Machiavelli's promise points to Book II. The next sentence turns `nello acquistare` into `per mantenersi libera`, "for maintaining itself free": the errors are made for liberty, through too much love of it.

**`di offendere quei cittadini che la dovrebbe premiare`.** "To offend those citizens whom it ought to reward": `la`, it, the city, as subject; the 1824 and the 1554 both print the singular verb.

**`avere sospetto di quelli in cui si dovrebbe confidare`.** "To have suspicion of those in whom one ought to confide": `si dovrebbe` is impersonal, against `la dovrebbe` before; `confidare`, confide, to trust; Guicciardini answers the whole sentence (Guicciardini .06).

**`E benché questi modi in una Repubblica venuta alla corruzione siano cagione di grandi mali`.** "And albeit these ways, in a republic come to corruption, are the cause of great ills": `benché`, albeit (D31); `modi`, ways (glossary); `male`, ill (glossary); `corruzione`, corruption, with `corrotto` (glossary).

**`e che molte volte piuttosto la viene alla tirannide`.** "And many times it rather comes to tyranny": `la`, it, the republic; `piuttosto`, rather, in the sense of the sooner; `tirannide`, tyranny (glossary). The `che` continues under `benché`.

**`come intervenne a Roma di Cesare`.** "As befell at Rome with Caesar": `intervenire`, befall (D298); `Cesare`, Caesar (names).

**`che per forza si tolse quello che la ingratitudine gli negava`.** "Who by force took for himself what ingratitude denied him": `torsi`, take for oneself.

**`fanno che la ne vive libera più`.** "Make it live the more free for it": `ne`, for it, by these ways. `più` after the verb could also be of time, "the longer"; the Italian allows both.

**`mantenendosi per paura di punizione gli uomini migliori e meno ambiziosi`.** "Men maintaining themselves better and less ambitious through fear of punishment": `mantenere`, maintain (D220).

**`Vero è che infra tutti i Popoli che mai ebbero Imperio`.** "It is true that, among all the peoples that ever had empire": plain English order (D263, exposition). `Imperio`, empire (D124).

**`per le cagioni di sopra discorse`.** "For the causes discoursed above": `discorrere`, discourse (D01), the causes argued in I.28.

**`perché Coriolano e Camillo furono fatti esuli per ingiuria, che l'uno e l'altro aveva fatto alla Plebe`.** "For Coriolanus and Camillus were made exiles for an injury that the one and the other had done to the plebs": `ingiuria`, injury (glossary). Gnaeus Marcius Coriolanus went into exile in 491 BC; Marcus Furius Camillus in 391, accused over the spoils of Veii. The 1554 edition reads `banditi`, "banished", and `fatta`, agreeing with `ingiuria`; the 1824 `fatto` is the unagreed participle that `avere` allows, escalated and kept (D304; NM-DISC-I29-D005).

**`Ma all'uno non fu perdonato ... l'altro non solamente fu richiamato`.** "But the one was not pardoned ... the other was not only recalled": Coriolanus led the Volsci against Rome; Camillus, recalled, saved it from the Gauls (Livy 2.35-40; 5.32, 5.46).

**`per aversi sempre riserbato contro al Popolo l'animo nimico`.** "For having always kept against the people a hostile spirit": `animo`, spirit (glossary); `nimico`, as an adjective, hostile; `riserbare`, reserve.

**`per tutto il tempo della sua vita adorato come Principe`.** "For the whole time of his life adored as a prince": `adorare`, adore; `Principe`, prince.

**`che degli altri non si era avuto`.** "Which had not been had of the others": the passive with `si` kept, so that "had" answers "began to have".

**`dalla grandezza del nimico che Scipione aveva vinto`.** "From the greatness of the enemy that Scipio had vanquished": Hannibal, at Zama in 202 BC; `vincere` of an enemy, vanquish, as at .04.

**`la vittoria di sì lunga e pericolosa guerra`.** "The victory in so long and dangerous a war": the Second Punic War, 218-201 BC. `pericoloso`, dangerous, with `pericolo`, danger (glossary).

**`dai favori che la gioventù, la prudenza, e le altre sue memorabili virtù gli acquistavano`.** "From the favours that the youth, the prudence and his other memorable *virtù* acquired for him": the articles kept; `virtù` left in Italian (D16); the plural is unmarked in the Italian and in the English. The 1554 edition reads `uirtuti`.

**`Le quali cose furono tante, che, non che altro`.** "Which things were so many that, not to speak of others": the relative link kept; `non che altro`, to say nothing of others: even the magistrates.

**`la qual cosa spiaceva agli uomini savj, come cosa inconsueta in Roma`.** "Which thing displeased wise men, as a thing unaccustomed in Rome": `savio`, wise. Guicciardini answers it nearly word for word, `mai non dispiacquono agli uomini savii e' progressi suoi` (Guicciardini .05). The 1554 edition reads `non usata`, the same sense.

**`E parve tanto straordinario il vivere suo`.** "And his living seemed so extraordinary": `vivere` as a noun, living (glossary); `straordinario`, extraordinary (glossary). Both readers saw a raised mark by `E`, which as `E'` would be the impersonal "it"; read as the conjunction, with the 1554 `Et` (D303; NM-DISC-I29-D002).

**`Catone Prisco, riputato santo`.** "Cato Priscus, deemed holy": Marcus Porcius Cato, the Censor; Plutarch (*Cato Maior* 1) gives Priscus as his first cognomen; `riputare`, deem (D259). Livy (38.50-54) tells of the attacks on the Scipios in 187-184 BC, and names Cato among those who pressed them.

**`fu il primo a fargli contra`.** "Was the first to act against him": `fare contra`, act against.

**`che una Città non si poteva chiamare libera, dove era un cittadino che fusse temuto dai Magistrati`.** "That a city could not be called free where there was a citizen who should be feared by the magistrates": the subjunctive `fusse`, "should be", marks the case as general.

**`merita quella scusa che di sopra ho detto meritare quelli Popoli e quelli Principi, che per sospetto sono ingrati`.** "It merits that excuse which I have said above those peoples and those princes merit who are ungrateful through suspicion": the excuse of .02, granted now to Rome; the accusative with infinitive becomes a relative.

**`Conchiudendo adunque questo discorso, dico`.** "Concluding, then, this discourse, I say": `adunque`, then (D74); `discorso`, discourse, the *currere* noun.

**`usandosi questo vizio della ingratitudine o per avarizia o per sospetto`.** "This vice of ingratitude being used either through avarice or through suspicion": the absolute construction kept.

**`i Popoli non mai per l'avarizia l'usarono, e per sospetto assai manco che i Principi`.** "Peoples never used it through avarice, and through suspicion much less than princes": `assai manco`, much less (D312); the conclusion stated as what will be seen, not proved here.

**`avendo meno cagione di sospettare`.** "Having less cause to suspect": the phrase of I.28 .01, held word for word (glossary `sospettare`, D300).

**`come di sotto si dirà`.** "As will be said below": the 1554 edition reads `tosto`, "as will soon be said"; kept (D305; NM-DISC-I29-D006). Guicciardini's reply uses the same phrase, `come di sotto si dirà`, of Rome's errors (Guicciardini .02).
