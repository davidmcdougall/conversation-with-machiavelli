# Discourses I.8

*As much as accusations are useful to republics, so much are calumnies pernicious.*

## NM-DISC.1.8.01

Notwithstanding that the *virtù* of Furius Camillus, after he had freed Rome
from the oppression of the French, had made all the Roman citizens, without
its seeming to them that they took from themselves reputation or degree, yield to that man,
nonetheless Manlius Capitolinus could not bear that so much honour and so much
glory should be attributed to that man; it seeming to him that, as to the safety
of Rome, through having saved the Capitol, he had merited as much as
Camillus; and as to the other praises of war, he was not inferior to him. So
that, laden with envy, not being able to quiet himself because of the glory
of that man, and seeing that he could not sow discord among the Fathers, he
turned to the plebs, sowing various sinister opinions among it. And among the
other things that he said, was how the treasure which had been gathered
together to give to the French, and then not given to them, had been usurped
by private citizens; and if it were had back, it could be converted to public
utility, lightening the plebs of the tributes, or of some private debt. These
words could do much with the plebs, so that he began to have concourse, and to
make many tumults at his pleasure in the city: which thing displeasing the
senate, and it seeming to it of moment and dangerous, it created a dictator,
that he might take cognizance of this case and rein in the impetus of Manlius.
Whereupon the dictator at once had him summoned, and they betook themselves in
public to meet one another, the dictator in the midst of the
nobles, and Manlius in the midst of the plebs. Manlius was asked to say with
whom this treasure was that was spoken of, for the senate was as desirous to
understand it as the plebs; to which Manlius was not answering particularly, but,
going about evading, was saying that it was not necessary to tell them what they
themselves knew, so that the dictator had him put in prison.

## NM-DISC.1.8.02

It is to be noted, by this text, how detestable calumnies are, in free cities
and in every other way of living, and how, to repress them, one must spare no
order that may serve the purpose. Nor can there be a better order to take them
away than to open many places to accusations, for as much as accusations
benefit republics, so much do calumnies do harm: and on the other hand there
is this difference, that calumnies have no need of witnesses, nor of any other
particular corroboration to prove them, in such a way that each can be
calumniated by each; but cannot, however, be accused, accusations having need of
true corroborations, and of circumstances, that show the truth of the
accusation. Men are accused to the magistracies, to the peoples, to the
councils; they are calumniated about the squares and about the loggias.
This calumny is used more where accusation is used less, and where cities are
less ordered to receive them. Therefore an orderer of a republic must order
that in it any citizen can be accused without any fear or without any
suspicion; and, this done and well observed, he must punish the calumniators
sharply; who cannot complain when they are punished, having the places open
to hear the accusations of him whom he might have calumniated about the
loggias. And where this part is not well ordered, great disorders always
follow; for calumnies irritate and do not chastise the citizens; and the
irritated think of availing themselves, hating rather than fearing the things
that are said against them.

## NM-DISC.1.8.03

This part, as has been said, was well ordered in Rome, and has always been
ill ordered in our city of Florence. And as at Rome this order did much good,
at Florence this disorder did much ill. And whoever reads the histories of
this city will see how many calumnies have been given in every time to its
citizens who have been employed in its important things. Of one they said
that he had robbed money from the Commune; of another, that he had not won an
enterprise, through having been corrupted; and that that other, through his
ambition, had done such and such an inconvenience. From which there arose
that from every part hatred rose, whence one came to division, from division
to sects, from sects to ruin. For if there had been in Florence an order for
accusing the citizens and punishing the calumniators, there would not have
followed the infinite scandals that have followed; for those citizens, whether
condemned or acquitted, would not have been able to do harm to the city, and they
would have been accused much less than they were calumniated, it not being possible,
as I have said, to accuse each as one can calumniate each. And among the other
things of which some citizen has availed himself to come to his greatness have
been these calumnies, which, coming against powerful citizens who were
opposing his appetite, did much for him; for, taking the part of the people,
and confirming it in the ill opinion that it had of them, he made it his
friend.

## NM-DISC.1.8.04

And albeit many examples of it could be adduced, I wish to be content with
one alone. The Florentine army was in the field before Lucca, commanded by
messer Giovanni Guicciardini, its commissary. Either his bad governing or his
bad fortune willed that the taking of that city by storm did not follow. Yet,
however the case stood, messer Giovanni was blamed for it, it being said that
he had been corrupted by the Lucchese; which calumny, being favoured by his
enemies, brought messer Giovanni almost to the last desperation. And albeit,
to justify himself, he wished to put himself in the hands of the
Captain, nonetheless he could never justify himself, there not being ways in
that republic by which to be able to do it. From which there arose much
indignation among the friends of messer Giovanni, who were the greater part of
the great men, and among those who desired to make novelty in Florence. Which
thing, both for these and for other like causes, grew so much that the ruin of
that republic followed from it.

## NM-DISC.1.8.05

Manlius Capitolinus was, then, a calumniator, and not an accuser; and the
Romans showed in this very case how calumniators must be punished. For
one must make them become accusers, and, when the accusation is corroborated
true, either reward them or not punish them; but when it is not corroborated
true, punish them as Manlius was punished.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Quanto ... tanto` is "as much as ... so much": a proportion.
The same figure returns in .02, `quanto le accuse giovano ..., tanto le
calunnie nuocono`, and I.7 ended on it (`come queste accuse sono utili ...,
così sono inutili e dannose le calunnie`).

**`calunnie`, `calunniare`, `calunniatori`.** "Calumnies", "calumniate",
"calumniators": the private charge, made without proof and without a court,
set against `accuse`, `accusare`, `accusatori`, "accusations", "accuse",
"accusers", the public charge before a court. One root each, held.

**`la virtù di Furio Camillo`.** "The *virtù* of Furius Camillus" (D16): the
capacity that made the citizens yield to him. Camillus, dictator, recovered
Rome after the Gallic sack of about 390 BC (Livy 5.49).

**`dei Francesi`.** "Of the French": Machiavelli's name for the Gauls, as in
I.7. The 1554 edition has him free Rome "from the siege and from the
oppression" (NM-DISC-I8-D001).

**`avesse fatto che ... cedevano a quello`.** "Had made all the Roman citizens
... yield to that man": the indicative `cedevano` states the yielding as fact.
`Quello` is Camillus throughout the period, "that man", so that the English
"him" cannot be taken for Manlius.

**`torsi reputazione o grado`.** "Took from themselves reputation or degree":
yielding did not feel like self-deprivation.

**`reputazione o grado`.** Glossary, `grado`, "degree".

**`Manlio Capitolino`.** Marcus Manlius Capitolinus, who held the Capitol
against the Gauls. Livy 6.11–20, for 385–384 BC: he took up the cause of
debtors, accused the senators of keeping back the Gallic gold, and was
imprisoned by the dictator Aulus Cornelius Cossus. Guicciardini named him in
his replies to I.5 and I.7.

**`belliche laudi`.** "Praises of war" (glossary, `laude`).

**`i Padri`.** "The Fathers": the senators, `patres`.

**`opinioni sinistre`.** "Sinister opinions": `opinione` (D02).

**`il tesoro`.** "The treasure": the gold collected to ransom Rome from the
Gauls, which Camillus' arrival made unnecessary.

**`dai tributi, o da qualche privato debito`.** "Of the tributes, or of some
private debt".

**`poterono assai`.** "Could do much": `potere` absolute, as the people of
I.7 "could" (D78 for the noun).

**`tumulti assai`.** "Many tumults" (glossary, `assai` of number).

**`cominciò avere concorso`.** "He began to have concourse": a crowd
running together to him. `Concorso` is `concorrere`'s noun, and the *currere*
family is held in it (D01, D63).

**`a sua posta`.** "At his pleasure".

**`di momento`.** "Of moment": of weight. Guicciardini's reply ends on the
same phrase (`di più momento`).

**`perché e' riconoscesse questo caso`.** "That he might take cognizance of
this case": examine it as a judge. "Recognise" now means identify.

**`frenasse l'impeto`.** "Rein in the impetus": the bridle, as `a freno`
(D88).

**`condussonsi ... all'incontro l'uno dell'altro`.** "They betook themselves
... to meet one another": `condursi`, reflexive.

**`andando fuggendo`.** "Going about evading": the progressive `andare` with
the gerund.

**`non rispondeva ... diceva`.** "Was not answering ... was saying": the
imperfects are durative; he went on evading until the dictator jailed him.

**`quello che e' si sapevano`.** "What they themselves knew": `si` is an
ethic dative, not a reflexive object.

**`detestabili`.** "Detestable". The 1554 edition reads `dannose & pessime`,
"damaging and very bad" (NM-DISC-I8-D003). Guicciardini's reply opens on the
1824 word: `le calunnie sono detestabili`.

**`si debbe non perdonare a ordine alcuno`.** "One must spare no order":
`perdonare a`, to spare, leave unused. `Debbe` is "must", as in I.4 and I.5
(D120).

**`torle via`.** "Take them away" (D131).

**`giovano` / `nuocono`.** "Benefit" / "do harm" (D106).

**`dall'altra parte`.** "On the other hand" (D24).

**`riscontro`, `riscontri`, `si riscontri vera`.** "Corroboration",
"corroborations", "is corroborated true": a matching of the charge against
other evidence. One root, held to the last sentence, where the reward or the
penalty turns on it.

**`ciascuno da ciascuno`.** "Each ... by each": everyone can be calumniated
by everyone, but not everyone accused. `Già` is "however". The same
`ciascuno` closes .03: "to accuse each as one can calumniate each". The 1554 edition has
neither this nor `più` in "is used more" (NM-DISC-I8-D004).

**`ai Magistrati, ai Popoli, ai Consigli`.** "To the magistracies, to the
peoples, to the councils" (D93): I.7's three courts, `al Popolo, o a
qualunque Magistrato o Consiglio`, now in the plural.

**`per le piazze, e per le logge`.** "About the squares and about the
loggias": the open places where Florentines met and talked.

**`di colui che gli avesse ... calunniato`.** "Of him whom he might have
calumniated": `gli` is the subject (`egli`). The sentence slides from the
calumniators, plural, to one calumniator, and the English follows it.

**`a riceverle`.** "To receive them": the pronoun is plural, though the
nearest noun, `l'accusa`, is singular; it takes accusations in general.

**`Però`.** "Therefore" (D22).

**`uno ordinatore`.** "An orderer" (glossary).

**`senz'alcuna paura, o senz'alcun sospetto`.** "Without any fear or without
any suspicion": of the accuser, who need fear nothing in accusing.

**`acremente`.** "Sharply". The 1554 edition reads `aspramente`, "harshly"
(NM-DISC-I8-D005).

**`irritano, e non gastigano`.** "Irritate and do not chastise": the calumny
wounds without punishing. `Gastigare` as in I.7.

**`pensano di valersi`.** "Think of availing themselves": of their own force,
to get their own back. The verb returns of the citizen who "availed himself"
of calumnies (.03), and of I.7's `valersi di forze esterne`.

**`odiando più presto che temendo`.** "Hating rather than fearing" (D135).

**`le Istorie`.** "The histories": the chronicles of Florence. Machiavelli's
own *Istorie fiorentine* were later.

**`non aveva vinto una impresa, per essere stato corrotto`.** The comma is
kept, so that the corruption is the alleged cause of the failure and not a
manner of winning.

**`il tale e tale inconveniente`.** "Such and such an inconvenience": `fare`
is "do" here, as "did much good" just before.

**`la mala opinione`.** "The ill opinion": `male` is "ill" (glossary), and
"bad" is kept for `cattivo` (D03), which follows in .04.

**`il Comune`.** "The Commune": the commonwealth of Florence as owner of
public money.

**`inconveniente`.** "Inconvenience" (D133): a harm done.

**`da ogni parte ne surgeva odio, d'onde si veniva alla divisione, dalla
divisione alle Sette, dalle Sette alla rovina`.** "From every part hatred
rose, whence one came to division, from division to sects, from sects to
ruin": the chain of I.7 .04 (offence, fear, defence, partisans, parties,
ruin), shortened. `Setta` is "sect", a faction, as in I.7.

**`non seguivano`.** "There would not have followed": the imperfect in a
contrary-to-fact condition (D47, D130).

**`o condannati o assoluti`.** "Whether condemned or acquitted".

**`si è valuto alcuno cittadino`.** "Some citizen has availed himself": unnamed.
Guicciardini's reply names him.

**`la parte del Popolo`.** "The part of the people": its side. The 1554
edition reads `del gran popolo` and has no relative `che` before
`all'appetito` (NM-DISC-I8-D006).

**`se lo fece amico`.** "He made it his friend": the people.

**`a campo a Lucca`.** "In the field before Lucca": besieging it. The war of
1429–1433, in which Florence failed to take Lucca.

**`messer Giovanni Guicciardini`.** Florentine commissary with the army
before Lucca in 1430, and a member of Guicciardini's own family; `messer`
marks a knight or doctor. The 1554 edition misprints the name and
calls him `buon commissario` (NM-DISC-I8-D007).

**`ei si volesse mettere`.** "He wished to put himself": `volere` is "wish"
(D117).

**`i cattivi suoi governi`.** "His bad governing": the management of the
siege. `Governo` is "government" (D64); the plural is of acts, not of
states, and "his bad governments" would read as regimes (D158).

**`la espugnazione`.** "The taking by storm".

**`corrotto da' Lucchesi`.** "Corrupted by the Lucchese": bribed.

**`del Capitano`.** "The Captain": presumably one of the foreign magistrates
with criminal jurisdiction in Florence, such as the Captain of the People. Guicciardini says he went
voluntarily to prison.

**`sdegno`.** "Indignation", as I.7.

**`gli uomini grandi`.** "The great men" (D118).

**`fare novità`.** "Make novelty": change the state, as I.7 `quale novità`.

**`ne seguì la rovina di quella Repubblica`.** "The ruin of that republic
followed from it": the quarrels that ended in the change of regime of
1433–1434, which Machiavelli writes as that republic's end.

**`in questo caso appunto`.** "In this very case": `appunto` goes with the
case.

**`fargli diventare accusatori`.** "Make them become accusers": the remedy is
to turn the calumniator into an accuser, so that his charge goes to a court.

**`la non si riscontri vera`.** "It is not corroborated true": `la` is the
accusation.

**`come fu punito Manlio`.** "As Manlius was punished": the text has shown
only the prison of .01. In 384 BC, accused before the people by tribunes, he
was condemned and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (Livy 6.20), but for aiming at
kingship, and Machiavelli does not say which punishment he means. Machiavelli calls him calumniator
and not accuser: he made the charge in the crowd, and would not bring it to
proof.
