# Consideration on chapter XII

*Of how much importance it is to take account of religion, and how Italy, for
having been wanting in it through the Roman Church, is ruined.*

## FG-CONS.1.12.01

One cannot speak so much ill of the Roman court that it does not deserve that
more be said, for it is an infamy, an example of all the disgraces and
opprobriums of the world. And I believe too that it is true that the greatness
of the Church, that is the authority which religion has given it, has been the
cause that Italy has not fallen into a monarchy; for on the one hand it has had
such credit that it has been able to make itself head, and to summon, when it
was needed, external princes against whoever was for oppressing Italy; on the
other, being stripped of arms of its own, it has not had such forces as to have
been able to establish temporal dominion other than what has voluntarily been
given it by others.

## FG-CONS.1.12.02

But I do not know whether the not coming into a monarchy has been the happiness
or the unhappiness of this province, for if under a republic this could have
been glorious to the name of Italy and happiness to that city that should
dominate, it was calamity to all the others, for, oppressed by the shadow of
that one, they had no capacity of attaining to any greatness, it being the
custom of republics not to impart the fruits of its liberty and rule to any
others than its own citizens.

## FG-CONS.1.12.03

And although Italy, divided into many dominions, has at various times suffered
many calamities which perhaps under a single dominion it would not have
suffered, albeit the inundations of the barbarians were rather in the time of
the Roman rule than otherwise; nonetheless in all these times it has had, on the
other side, so many flourishing cities as it would not have had under a
republic; so that I reckon a monarchy would have been more unhappy for it than
happy. This reason has no force in a kingdom, which is more common to all its
subjects; and therefore we see France and many other provinces live happy under
one king; yet, whether by some fate of Italy, or by the complexion of the men,
tempered in such a way that they have wit and force, this province has never
been easy to reduce under one rule, even when the Church was not there; on the
contrary, it has always by nature desired liberty, nor do I believe there is
memory of any other rule that has possessed it whole, than that of the Romans,
who subjugated it with great *virtù* and great violence; and as the republic was
extinguished and the *virtù* of the emperors failed, they readily lost the rule
of Italy. Therefore if the Roman Church has opposed itself to monarchies, I do
not readily concur that this has been the unhappiness of this province, since it
has preserved it in that way of living which is more according to its most
ancient custom and inclination.

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini prints a rubric over the Considerazione, in his own
wording — lower-case `religione`, a comma after `la Italia`, lower-case
`romana` — rather than reprinting the 1824 *Discorsi* heading. The established
text omitted it until 2026-09-18 (FG-CONS-I12-C001).

**`virtù`, twice.** Left untranslated, as in the facing chapter. Guicciardini
uses it of the Romans' conquering capacity and then of what failed in the
emperors — neither moral virtue nor simply force, and the same word must carry
both.

**`facilmente`, twice, eleven words apart.** `perderono facilmente` and `non
concorro facilmente`. The English holds one word for both — "readily" — because
the repetition is Guicciardini's and the hinge sits inside it.

**`milita`.** Guicciardini's `non milita` is the Latinate argumentative sense:
the reason has no force, does not apply. Modern English "militate" survives
almost only in "militate against", which pulls the opposite way, so the cognate
is not kept here.

**`imperio` and `imperadori`.** One Italian root, two unrelated English words:
"rule" and "emperors". The rhyme in `mancò la virtù degli imperadori ...
perderono ... lo imperio d'Italia` cannot be carried over.

**The close.** The witness prints no full stop after `inclinazione sua`; the
established text supplies one. Unresolved — see decisions.md O07.
