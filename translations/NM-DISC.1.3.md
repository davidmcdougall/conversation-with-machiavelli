# Discourses I.3

*What accidents made the tribunes of the plebs be created in Rome, which made
the republic more perfect.*

## NM-DISC.1.3.01

As all those demonstrate who reason of civil living, and as every history is
full of examples of it, it is necessary for whoever disposes a republic, and
orders laws in it, to presuppose all men to be bad, and that they are always
to use the malignity of their spirit whenever they have free occasion for it;
and when some malignity stays hidden for a time, it proceeds from a hidden
cause, which, through no experience of the contrary having been seen, is not
known; but it is afterwards uncovered by time, which they say is the father of
every truth.

## NM-DISC.1.3.02

It seemed that there was in Rome between the plebs and the senate, the
Tarquins having been driven out, a very great union, and that the nobles had
laid down that pride of theirs, and had become of popular spirit, and bearable
by anyone, even though the lowest. This deceit stayed hidden, nor was the cause
of it seen, as long as the Tarquins lived; and the nobility, fearing them, and
having fear that the plebs, ill treated, might draw near to them, bore itself
humanely toward it; but as soon as the Tarquins were dead, and fear had
fled from the nobles, they began to spit against the plebs that venom which
they had held in their breast, and in all the ways they could they offended it:
which thing bears witness to what I have said above, that men never work
anything well except through necessity; but where choice abounds, and where
licence can be used, everything is at once filled with confusion and disorder.

## NM-DISC.1.3.03

Therefore it is said that hunger and poverty make men industrious, and the laws
make them good. And where a thing of itself works well without the law, the law
is not necessary; but when that good usage is lacking, the law is at once
necessary. Therefore, once the Tarquins were lacking, who with the fear of them
held the nobility reined in, it was fitting to think of a new order, which should make
that same effect that the Tarquins made when they were alive. And therefore,
after many confusions, uproars and dangers of scandals, which arose between the
plebs and the nobility, one came, for the security of the plebs, to the
creation of the tribunes; and these they ordered with so many preeminences and
so much reputation that they could always thereafter be intermediaries between
the plebs and the senate, and forestall the insolence of the nobles.

## Notes

**`cattivi`.** "Bad", as everywhere in this edition (D03): the chapter's
premise is that all men are to be presupposed `cattivi`, and Guicciardini's
reply turns on the same word four times. `Malignità` is "malignity", as in
I.2.

**`presupporre`.** "To presuppose": to take as given for the purpose of
ordering, not to believe. Guicciardini's first sentence makes the difference
the whole question.

**`vivere civile`.** "Civil living": the infinitive as a noun, as `vivere
libero` in I.2. Guicciardini answers with `vivere umano`, "human living".

**`gli abbiano sempre ad usare`.** "That they are always to use": `avere a`
with the infinitive, as elsewhere (D61). The clause stays under `presupporre`:
it is what the founder must assume.

**`occulta`, `nascoso`.** Both "hidden": the .01 claim, that malignity stays
hidden for a time, comes true in .02, where the nobles' deceit "stayed hidden".
Two Italian words, one English word, recorded.

**`quella loro superbia`.** "That pride of theirs": `superbia` is the name of
the last king, Tarquinius Superbus, the Proud. The nobles had put off the
king's vice only while the king could return.

**`di sopra ho detto`.** "What I have said above": I.1, where men act "either
by necessity or by choice", and choice breeds idleness.

**`l'offendevano`.** "Offended it" in the older sense: harmed, injured, as in
I.2 (`offendere quelli governatori`). Not giving umbrage.

**`il tempo`.** The subject is held back in the Italian until the sentence's
end, where time arrives with its proverb attached. The English delays it with
a passive (D18).

**`operano mai nulla bene`.** "Never work anything well": `operare` is "to
work" in this chapter, and `una cosa ... opera bene` in .03 is "a thing ...
works well". Guicciardini quotes the claim without `nulla`: "men never work
well except through necessity".

**`elezione`.** "Choice", as in I.1 and I.2: here the condition of having
alternatives, set against necessity. It is the same word as the election of
magistrates.

**`però`.** "Therefore", three times in .03 (D22). The chapter moves from the
nobles' example to the proverb and then to the tribunes by consequence, not by
contrast.

**`consuetudine`.** "Usage" (D41): where good usage holds, no law is needed.

**`mancare`.** "Lacking" twice, of the good usage and of the Tarquins, which
the Italian joins with one verb: when the kings were gone, the fear that had
done a law's work went with them. `Mancati` also carries "dead".

**`a freno`.** "Reined in": `freno` is the bit and bridle.

**`convenne`.** "It was fitting": the impersonal of fitness, with no subject
named. Kept apart from `bisogna`, "it is needful", and `è necessario`, "it is
necessary".

**`scandali`.** "Scandals" in the older sense: public disturbances, occasions of
strife. Not moral disgrace.

**`e quelli ordinarono`.** The Italian names no subject; `quelli`, "these", is
the tribunes, as object, and the English supplies "they" as the Romans who
ordered them. A reader can take `quelli` as the subject ("and those [the
Romans] ordered [them]"); the sense is the same.

**`mezzi`.** "Intermediaries": a role, not a place. Guicciardini restates it as
`in mezzo`, "in the middle", and denies it: the tribunes tempered the nobles,
not the plebs. Canestrini's note to the reply quotes this clause.

**`ovviare`.** "Forestall": to meet a thing in the way and prevent it. The
modern "obviate" has moved to removing a need.

**The Tarquins.** Tarquinius Superbus and his house, expelled in the tradition
in 509 BC. The first tribunes of the plebs followed the plebs' secession to the
Sacred Mount, in the tradition 494 BC.
