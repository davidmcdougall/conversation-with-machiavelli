# Discourses I.2

*Of how many species republics are, and of which was the Roman republic.*

## NM-DISC.1.2.01

I wish to set aside the reasoning about those cities that have had their beginning
subject to others, and I shall speak of those that have had their beginning far
from all external servitude, but have at once governed themselves at their own
discretion, either as republics or as a principate; which have had, as [they had] diverse
beginnings, diverse laws and orders. For to some, either at their beginning or
after no long time, the laws have been given by one alone, and at a stroke, like
those that were given by Lycurgus to the Spartans; some have had them by chance,
and at several times, and according to accidents, like Rome.

## NM-DISC.1.2.02

So that that republic can be called happy which is allotted a man so prudent that
he gives it laws ordered in such a way that, without need of correcting them, it
can live securely under them. And one sees that Sparta observed them more than
eight hundred years without corrupting them, and without any dangerous tumult;
and on the contrary that city holds some degree of unhappiness which, not having
chanced upon a prudent orderer, is necessitated to reorder itself by itself; and
of these, again, that one is more unhappy which is more distant from order; and
that one is more distant which with its orders is altogether outside the right
road that could lead it to the perfect and true end; for those that are in this
degree, it is almost impossible that they should set themselves right by some accident.
Those others which, if they do not have the perfect order, have taken a
beginning good and apt for becoming better, can through the occurrence of accidents
become perfect. But this will indeed be true, that they will never order themselves
without dangers, for most men never agree to a new law that concerns a new
order in the city unless it is shown them by a necessity that it is needful to do it;
and since this necessity cannot come without danger, it is an easy thing for
that republic to be ruined before it has brought itself to a perfection of order.
Of which the republic of Florence bears full witness, which was reordered by the
accident of Arezzo in [15]02, and disordered by that of Prato in [15]12.

## NM-DISC.1.2.03

Wishing then to discourse what the orders of the city of Rome were, and what
accidents led it to its perfection, I say how some who have written of republics
say that there is in them one of the three states, called by them principate, of
optimates, and popular, and how those who order a city must turn to one of
these, according as seems to them more to the purpose. Some others, and according to the
opinion of many wiser, are of the opinion that there are six sorts of
governments, of which three are very bad, three others are good in themselves,
but so easy to be corrupted that they too come to be pernicious. Those that are good
are the three written above; those that are evil are three others, which depend
on these three, and each of them is in such a way similar to the one that is near it, that
they readily leap from the one to the other; for the principate readily becomes
tyrannical; the optimates with facility become a state of few; the popular
without difficulty converts itself into the licentious. So much so that if an
orderer of a republic orders in a city one of those three states, he orders it
there for little time; for he can make no remedy there, to make it not slide
into its contrary, through the likeness that *virtù* and vice have in this
case.

## NM-DISC.1.2.04

These variations of governments arose by chance among men; for in the beginning
of the world, the inhabitants being sparse, they lived for a time dispersed in
the likeness of beasts; afterwards, generation multiplying, they gathered together,
and to be able to defend themselves better they began to regard among themselves
him who was more robust and of greater heart, and made him as head, and
obeyed him. From this arose the knowledge of things honourable and good, different from the pernicious and evil; for, seeing that if one harmed his
benefactor, hatred and compassion came of it among men, blaming the
ungrateful and honouring those who were grateful, and thinking too that those
same injuries could be done to them, to flee a like ill they were brought to
make laws, and to order punishments for whoever should contravene; whence came
the knowledge of justice. Which thing made it that, being afterwards to choose
a prince, they did not go after the stoutest, but after him who was more prudent
and more just.

## NM-DISC.1.2.05

But as afterwards they began to make the prince by succession, and not by choice,
at once the heirs began to degenerate from their forebears, and, leaving virtuous
works, they thought that princes were to do nothing else but surpass the others in
sumptuousness, and in lasciviousness, and in every other delightful quality. In
such a way that, the prince beginning to be hated, and through such hatred to fear, and
passing quickly from fear to offences, there presently arose a tyranny. From this
arose next the beginnings of ruins, and of conspiracies and plots against
princes, made not by those who were either timid or weak, but by those who
through generosity, greatness of spirit, riches and nobility exceeded the
others, [and] who could not bear the dishonourable life of that prince. The multitude,
then, following the authority of these powerful ones, armed itself against
the prince, and, he being extinguished, obeyed them as its liberators. And they,
holding in hatred the name of a single head, constituted a government of
themselves, and in the beginning, having respect to the past tyranny, governed
themselves according to the laws ordered by them, putting every convenience of
their own after the common utility, and things private and public
with the utmost diligence they governed and conserved. This administration having come
afterwards to their sons, who, not knowing the variation of fortune, never
having tried ill, and not wishing to stay content with civil equality, but
having turned to avarice, to ambition, to the usurpation of women, made it so
that from a government of optimates it became a government of few, without
having respect to any civility; so that in a short time, it happened to them as
to the tyrant, for the multitude, wearied of their governments, made itself the
minister of whoever designed in any way to offend those governors, and so
someone presently rose who with the help of the multitude extinguished them.

## NM-DISC.1.2.06

And the memory of the prince, and of the injuries received from him, being still
fresh, having undone the state of the few, and not wishing to remake that of the
prince, they turned to the popular state, and ordered it in such a way that
neither the powerful few nor a prince should have any authority in it. And
because all states in the beginning have some reverence, this popular state was
maintained a little, but not much, especially once that generation which had
ordered it was extinguished; for at once they came to licence, where neither
private men nor public were feared; of such quality that, each living in his own
way, a thousand injuries were done every day: so that, constrained by necessity,
or by the suggestion of some good man, or to flee such licence, they return anew
to the principate, and from that, from degree to degree, they come back towards licence, in
the ways and for the causes said. And this is the circle in which, turning, all
republics have governed themselves, and govern themselves; but rarely do they return into the
same governments, for almost no republic can be of so much life that it can
pass many times through these mutations and remain on its feet. But it indeed
happens that, in the travail of a republic, lacking always counsel and forces,
it becomes subject to a near state that is better ordered than it; but granted
that this were not so, a republic would be apt to turn round again for an
infinite time in these governments.

## NM-DISC.1.2.07

I say then that all the said ways are pestiferous, through the brevity of
the life that is in the three good ones, and through the malignity that is in
the three evil. So that, those who prudently order laws having known this
defect, fleeing each of these ways by itself, they chose one that should partake
of all, judging it firmer and more stable, because the one guards the other,
there being in one and the same city the principate, the optimates, and the
popular government. Among those who have for like constitutions merited most
praise is Lycurgus, who ordered his laws in Sparta in such a way that, giving
their parts to the kings, to the optimates, and to the people, he made a state
that lasted more than eight hundred years, with the utmost praise to himself,
and quiet to that city. The contrary happened to Solon, who ordered the laws in
Athens, who, through ordering there only the popular state, made it of such
short life that before he died he saw born the tyranny of Pisistratus: and albeit
forty years afterwards his heirs were driven out of it, and Athens returned to
liberty, because it took up again the popular state, according to the orders of
Solon, it did not hold it more than a hundred years, even though to maintain it
it made many constitutions, by which the insolence of the great and the licence
of the generality were repressed, which had not been considered by Solon;
nevertheless, because it did not mix them with the power of the principate and
with that of the optimates, Athens lived, in respect of Sparta, a very short
time.

## NM-DISC.1.2.08

But let us come to Rome, which, notwithstanding that it did not have a Lycurgus
to order it in such a way in the beginning that it could live free a long time,
nonetheless so many were the accidents that arose in it, through the disunion
there was between the plebs and the senate, that what an orderer had not done,
chance did. For if Rome was not allotted the first fortune, it was allotted the
second; for the first orders, if they were defective, nonetheless did not
deviate from the right path that could lead them to perfection. For Romulus and
all the other kings made many and good laws, conformable also to free
living; but because their end was to found a kingdom and not a republic, when that
city remained free there were lacking in it many things that it was necessary to
order in favour of liberty, which had not been ordered by those kings. And though
those kings of its lost their rule for the causes and ways discoursed,
nonetheless those who drove them out, ordering there at once two consuls to
stand in the place of the king, came to drive out of Rome the name, and not the
royal power; so that, there being in that republic the consuls and the senate,
it came to be mixed only of two qualities of the three written above, that is,
of principate and of optimates.

## NM-DISC.1.2.09

There remained to it only to give place to the popular government; whence, the
Roman nobility having become insolent for the causes that will be said below,
the people rose against it; so that, not to lose the whole, it was constrained
to concede to the people its part; and, on the other hand, [that] the senate and
the consuls should remain with so much authority that they could hold their
degree in that republic. And so arose the creation of the tribunes of the plebs,
after which creation the state of that republic came to be established, all
three qualities of government having there their part. And so favourable was
fortune to it that, albeit one passed from the government of the kings and of the
optimates to the people, by those same degrees and for those same causes that
have been discoursed above, nonetheless there was never taken, to give authority
to the optimates, all the authority from the royal qualities, nor was the
authority altogether diminished for the optimates, to give it to the people;
but, remaining mixed, it made a perfect republic, to which perfection it came
through the disunion of the plebs and the senate, as will be amply demonstrated
in the two next following chapters.

## Notes

**The *currere* family.** Machiavelli will "discourse" what Rome's orders were,
and twice refers back to causes "discoursed" above; "the occurrence of
accidents" (.02) is the one *occorrere* form. The English keeps both, because
Guicciardini's reply takes up the verb: "to discourse this article
minutely".

**`specie`.** "Species", as Guicciardini's `spezie` in the reply and in I.1:
the kinds of republic, not a biological term.

**`Principato`, `Ottimati`, `Popolare`.** The three good states are named, as
the Italian names them, by the one, the best men and the people: principate,
optimates, popular. Their corruptions are the tyrannical, the "state of the
few", and the licentious. "Aristocracy" and "democracy" are the Greek names
this chapter does not use.

**`Stato`.** "State" throughout, as the constitution or condition a city is in:
one of the three states, a state of few, the popular state, and at the end the
state of the Roman republic "came to be established". Not the modern
nation-state; a "near state" in .06 is a neighbouring power.

**`modo`, `modi`.** "Way", "ways", as in I.12 ("this way has been observed"):
the six forms are "all the said ways", and the cycle runs "in the ways and for
the causes said".

**`virtù ed il vizio`.** *Virtù* is left in Italian throughout this edition.
Here it stands against vice, and so has its moral sense: the good form slides
into its corruption because the two resemble each other. "Virtuous works" in
.05 is its adjective.

**`sortire`.** "To be allotted": to receive by lot. A republic that draws a
prudent founder is happy; Rome drew the second fortune, not the first.

**`gli assai uomini`.** "Most men": `assai` as an adjective, many.

**Arezzo and Prato.** The print gives the years as roman numerals, `II` and
`XII`: 1502, when the revolt of Arezzo led Florence to make Piero Soderini
gonfaloniere for life, and 1512, when the sack of Prato brought the Medici back
and ended the republic Machiavelli served.

**`e secondo l'opinione di molti più savj`.** "And according to the opinion of
many wiser": "wiser" may belong to the "some others" (wiser, in many people's
opinion) or to the "many" (the opinion of many wiser men), and `molti più`
can also be old Tuscan for "much" (much wiser). The English leans to the
second reading; the Italian does not decide.

**`civiltà`.** "Civility": the civil way of living together under laws, not
politeness.

**`generosità`.** Largeness of spirit, beside "greatness of spirit".

**`vivere libero`.** "Free living": the infinitive as a noun, living as a free
city.

**`potestà`, `potenza`.** Both "power". Recorded so it does not read as an
oversight: `potestà` is the power held by an office, `potenza` the might a form
of government carries.

**`Costituzioni`.** "Constitutions" in the older sense: enactments,
ordinances. Solon's successors made many.

**`restassero`.** The senate and consuls "should remain": the subjunctive hangs
on what the nobility was constrained to concede, and the English supplies
"[that]".

**`simile`, `grado`, `rispetto`.** Held as "like" (or "similar"), "degree"
and "respect" throughout the chapter, so that `di grado in grado` (.06) and
`per quelli medesimi gradi` (.09) are visibly the same steps, and a city can
"hold its degree".

**The sources.** The six forms and their cycle are Polybius, *Histories* book
6, which Machiavelli follows closely without naming him.
