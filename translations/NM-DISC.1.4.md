# Discourses I.4

*That the disunion of the plebs and of the Roman senate made that republic free
and powerful.*

## NM-DISC.1.4.01

I do not wish to fail to discourse upon these tumults that there were in Rome
from the death of the Tarquins to the creation of the tribunes; and afterwards
upon some other things against the opinion of many, who say that Rome was a
tumultuary republic, and full of so much confusion that, if good fortune and
military *virtù* had not supplied their defects, it would have been inferior to
every other republic. I cannot deny that fortune and soldiery were causes of
the Roman empire; but it indeed seems to me that these men do not perceive that
where there is good soldiery it is fitting that there be good order, and
seldom also does it occur that there is not good fortune.

## NM-DISC.1.4.02

But let us come to the other particulars of that city. I say that those who
condemn the tumults between the nobles and the plebs, it seems to me that they
blame those things that were the first cause of keeping Rome free, and that
they consider more the uproars and the cries that arose from such tumults than
the good effects that they brought forth; and that they do not consider how
there are in every republic two diverse humours, that of the people and that
of the great; and how all the laws that are made in favour of liberty arise
from their disunion, as can readily be seen to have come about in Rome; for from
the Tarquins to the Gracchi, which was more than three hundred years, the
tumults of Rome seldom brought forth exile, and very seldom blood. Nor on that account can these tumults be judged harmful, nor a republic
divided, which in so much time, through its differences, sent into exile no
more than eight or ten citizens, and killed very few of them, and fined
not many either.

## NM-DISC.1.4.03

Nor can a republic be called unordered in any way with reason where there are
so many examples of *virtù*, for good examples arise from good education, good
education from good laws, and good laws from those tumults that many
inconsiderately condemn; for whoever examines well their end will not find
that they have brought forth any exile or violence to the disfavour of the common
good, but laws and orders for the benefit of public liberty.

## NM-DISC.1.4.04

And if anyone should say: the ways were extraordinary, and almost savage, to
see the people together crying out against the senate, the senate against the
people, running tumultuously through the streets, shutting the shops, the whole
plebs of Rome departing, all which things frighten, to say nothing of others,
whoever reads; I say that every city must have its ways with which the
people can vent its ambition, and above all those cities that wish to avail
themselves of the people in important things; among which the city of Rome had
this way, that when that people wished to obtain a law, either it did some of
the aforesaid things, or it would not give its name to go to war, so that to
placate it it was needful in some part to satisfy them. And the desires of free
peoples are seldom pernicious to liberty, for they arise either from
being oppressed or from suspicion of having to be oppressed. And if
these opinions were false, there is the remedy of harangues, that some
good man rise who, by oration, may demonstrate to them how they deceive
themselves; and peoples, as Tully says, albeit they are ignorant, are capable
of the truth, and readily yield when what is true is told them by a man worthy
of faith.

## NM-DISC.1.4.05

One must, then, blame the Roman government more sparingly, and consider that
so many good effects as came out of that republic were not caused except by
very good causes. And if the tumults were the cause of the creation of the
tribunes, they merit the highest praise; for, besides giving the popular
administration its part, they were constituted as guard of Roman liberty,
as will be shown in the following chapter.

## Notes

**`discorrere`.** "To discourse" (D01): Machiavelli's opening verb, and
Guicciardini's in his last paragraph, where he declines to discourse on the
rest of the Roman orders.

**`tumulti`, `tumultuaria`, `tumultuariamente`.** "Tumults", "tumultuary",
"tumultuously": one root through the chapter, kept, because the chapter is a
defence of the word.

**`che dicono, Roma essere stata`.** The Italian has the Latin construction,
the accusative with the infinitive; the English says "who say that Rome was",
since the Latin order does not survive into English (D19).

**`la virtù militare`.** "Military *virtù*": the capacity that, on the many's
opinion, made up for Rome's disorders. Guicciardini gives the opinion back at
the end of his reply: the `virtù` of its military discipline "sustained all
the other defects".

**`a' loro difetti`.** "Their defects": the Italian does not say whose, the
Romans' or the republic's.

**`milizia`.** "Soldiery", as in I.1 (D60): the armed force and its order,
not a citizen militia.

**`Imperio`.** "Empire", lower case (D124): the dominion Rome acquired, the
republic's as much as the emperors'.

**`conviene`.** "It is fitting" (D85): where the soldiery is good, the order
must be good too.

**`occorre`.** "It occurs" (D01): the verb of Machiavelli's `ragioni che mi
occorrono` in I.12.

**`rade volte`, `radissime`.** "Seldom", "very seldom".

**`Io dico, che coloro ..., mi pare che biasimino`.** The sentence changes
construction after its subject: "those who condemn ..., it seems to me that
they blame". Kept.

**`dannano`.** "Condemn", twice. The fining of citizens, `condannò in
danari`, is "fined", so that "condemn" stays with the tumults' critics.

**`duoi umori diversi`.** "Two diverse humours": the physician's word for the
dispositions of a body, here the body politic. `Popolo` and `Grandi` are "the
people" and "the great".

**`seguìto`.** "Come about": the accent marks the participle of `seguire`,
"to follow", in its sense of taking place.

**`pertanto`.** "On that account": kept apart from `però`, "therefore" (D22),
and `adunque`, "then" (D74).

**`inordinata`.** "Unordered": the negative of `ordine`, order, as
`disordine` is disorder.

**`inconsideratamente`.** "Inconsiderately": the critics do not consider
(`considerino`) what the tumults bring forth.

**`efferati`.** "Savage": wild, made fierce.

**`partirsi tutta la Plebe di Roma`.** The secessions of the plebs, when it
left the city in a body.

**`non che altro, chi legge`.** "To say nothing of others, whoever reads":
the scenes frighten even a reader of them.

**`dare il nome`.** "Give its name": enrol for military service. Refusing it
was the plebs' strike.

**`a placarlo ... soddisfargli`.** "To placate it ... to satisfy them": the
Italian moves from the people as one body to its members, and so does the
English.

**`d'avere a essere oppressi`.** "Of having to be oppressed": the fear of a
future oppression. `Avere a` is "be to" elsewhere (D61); here the English
cannot take it twice over.

**`concioni`.** "Harangues", as in FG I.2: speeches to the assembled people.
`Orando`, "by oration", is their verb.

**`uomo da bene`.** "Good man": a man of standing and integrity.

**Tully.** Marcus Tullius Cicero, whom Machiavelli calls by his nomen, as older English did.
The thought is in Cicero's *On Friendship* (25.95): the assembly, made up of
the most ignorant, can yet tell a demagogue from a serious man.

**`ottime`.** "Very good": the elative of `buono`, as `pessimo` is "very bad".

**`meritano somma laude`.** The subject runs on from the tumults, though what
was constituted for the guard of liberty were the tribunes. The Italian is as
elliptical as the English.

**`per guardia della Libertà romana`.** "As guard of Roman liberty": the
tribunes as the keepers of liberty, the subject of the next chapter, which
asks where that guard is more securely placed.
