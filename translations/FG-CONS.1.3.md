# Consideration on chapter III of Book I

*What accidents made the tribunes of the plebs be created in Rome; which made
the republic more perfect*

## FG-CONS.1.3.01

It is laid down too absolutely that men never work well except through
necessity, and that whoever orders a republic must presuppose them all bad,
for there are many who, even having the capacity to do ill, do good, and all
men are not bad. It is true that both in ordering a republic and in every other
business one must order things in such a way that whoever should wish to do ill
cannot, not because all men are always bad, but to provide against those who
should be bad; and one is to consider in this matter that men, all of them, are
by nature inclined to the good, and to all, *data paritate terminorum*, the
good is more pleasing than the ill, and if anyone has another inclination, it
is so much against what is ordinary in the others, and against that first
object which nature holds out to us, that he must rather be called a monster
than a man. Everyone, then, is naturally inclined to the good, but because our
nature is fragile, and in human living one meets at every step with the
occasions that can divert from the good, such as voluptuousness, ambition,
avarice, the wise, foreseeing this danger, where they have been able to take
from men the capacity of doing ill, have done it, and where it has not been
possible to do it absolutely, because it cannot be done always, but rather at rare times,
they added another remedy, that is, to entice men to the good with rewards, and
to frighten them from doing ill with penalties.

## FG-CONS.1.3.02

The cause of choosing the tribunes was that which is said in the Discourse,
that is, to make a defence for the plebs against the nobility, that is, the
patricians, which effect resulted in four ways: the first, that the plebs,
having a particular magistracy, came to have a public head, with whom its
conveniences could be consulted and treated, and, having recourse to whom, the
plebs was not despised as a body that had no head, the second, through the authority
of interceding, which was such that no public deliberation could be made in
Rome against the will of even one alone of the tribunes, the third, through
being able to put new laws before the people; the fourth, through calling to
the judgment of the people those citizens that should seem [fit] to each of
them. — Which authorities were not understood from the beginning of their
creation, but in process of time either usurped or enlarged by interpretation
of the law with which [the tribunes] were created: which authorities did not do what the
Discourse says, that is, that the tribunes should be a magistracy in the
middle between the senate and the plebs, for they were indeed a tempering of the power
of the nobles, but not, *e converso*, of the licence of the plebs.

## Notes

**The two editions.** This edition prints the text of the first edition of
the *Considerazioni*, Giuseppe Canestrini's (Florence, 1857), and compares it
with Roberto Palmarocchi's of 1933. Where they differ, the variants list gives
Palmarocchi's reading.

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply,
with a semicolon where the 1824 *Discorsi* has a comma, and without a full
stop.

**The paragraphs.** Canestrini prints this reply in two paragraphs, and they
are kept: one against the premise, one against the tribunes.

**`[È] posto`.** Canestrini prints `E posto`, "And laid down", which leaves the
opening period without a verb. The 1933 edition's `È posto`, "It is laid
down", is taken, and the letter is bracketed in the Italian. Printers of the
time often set a capital È without its accent, but not Canestrini here: `È
vero` and `È adunque` in the same reply carry it.

**`troppo assolutamente`.** "Too absolutely": Guicciardini does not deny the
premise; he denies it without limit. The reply is built on the difference
between "all men are bad" and "one must order against those who should be
bad".

**`cattivi`.** "Bad", four times, Machiavelli's word (D03). `Tutti gli uomini
non sono cattivi` is kept as "all men are not bad": the Italian places the
negation where the English does, and a reader can hear either "no man is bad"
or "not all men are bad". Guicciardini means the second.

**`assolutamente`.** "Absolutely", twice: the premise is laid down "too
absolutely", and the wise, where they could not remove the capacity for ill
"absolutely", added rewards and penalties. The second answers the first.

**`gli uomini non operano mai bene`.** "Men never work well": his quotation of
Machiavelli, without Machiavelli's `nulla`, "anything".

**`bene` and `male`.** "Good" and "ill", as in I.2: `fare male` "to do ill",
`fanno bene` "do good", `piace più il bene che 'l male` "the good is more
pleasing than the ill". `Cattivo` stays "bad".

**`etiam`.** Latin, "even", in the middle of the Italian; Canestrini prints it
in roman type.

**`facultà`.** "Capacity", as in I.12. Canestrini's scan leaves the first
occurrence in doubt between `facoltà` and `facultà`; the second is clear.
Either way it is the same word.

**`data paritate terminorum`.** Latin: "equal terms being given", other things
being equal. A schoolman's qualification inside the claim that the good pleases
more.

**`obietto`.** "Object": what nature puts before us to aim at.

**`vivere umano`.** "Human living", against Machiavelli's `vivere civile`,
"civil living".

**`e' savii`.** "The wise": the lawgivers, whose two remedies are to remove the
capacity for ill where they can, and where they cannot, rewards and penalties.

**`eleggere`.** "Choosing", as in I.1 and I.2: the edition's word for
`eleggere` and `elezione` in both authors.

**`comodi`.** "Conveniences" (D59): the plebs' interests and advantages.

**`uno magistrato particulare`.** "A particular magistracy": one of the plebs'
own. `Magistrato` is "magistracy" for the office and "magistrate" for the man,
as in I.2.

**`lo intercedere`.** "Interceding": the tribune's veto, *intercessio*, by
which one tribune could stop almost any act of a magistrate or the senate.

**`che paressi a ciascuno di loro`.** "That should seem [fit] to each of
them": any tribune could summon a citizen to trial before the people.

**`intese`.** "Understood": the powers were not read into the tribunes' first
institution, but came later, by usurpation or by widening the interpretation
of the law. `Creati` is masculine: it is the tribunes who were created by that
law, and the English supplies them in brackets.

**`in mezzo tra 'l senato e la plebe`.** "In the middle between the senate
and the plebs": Guicciardini restates Machiavelli's `mezzi`, "intermediaries",
as a place, and reverses the order of the two bodies. Canestrini's note quotes
Machiavelli's clause. The tribunes were no middle: they tempered one side.

**`temperamento`.** "A tempering": the tribunes moderated the nobles' power,
as I.12's `complessione temperata` is a tempered complexion.

**`e converso`.** Latin, "conversely".

**The close.** Canestrini prints a full stop after `plebe`, and the English
ends with one.
