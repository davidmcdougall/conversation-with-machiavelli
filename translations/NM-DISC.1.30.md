# Discourses I.30

*Which ways a prince or a republic must use to flee this vice of ingratitude, and which that captain or that citizen [must use] so as not to be oppressed by it.*

## NM-DISC.1.30.01

A prince, to flee this necessity of having to live with suspicion, or be ungrateful, must personally go on the expeditions, as those Roman emperors did in the beginning, as the Turk does in our times, and as those who are virtuous have done and do. For, winning, the glory and the acquisition is all theirs: and when they are not there, the glory being another's, it does not seem to them that they can use that acquisition if they do not extinguish in another that glory which they have not known how to gain for themselves, and become ungrateful and unjust; and without doubt their loss is greater than the gain.

## NM-DISC.1.30.02

But when, either through negligence or through little prudence, they remain at home idle, and send a captain, I have no precept to give them other than what they know by themselves. But I say indeed to that captain, judging that he cannot flee the bites of ingratitude, that he should do one of the two things: either at once after the victory leave the army, and put himself in the hands of his prince, guarding himself from every insolent or ambitious act, so that that one, stripped of every suspicion, may have cause either to reward him or not to offend him; or, when this does not seem to him [good] to do, spiritedly take the contrary part, and hold all those ways by which he may believe that acquisition to be his own and not his prince's, making the soldiers and the subjects well-disposed to himself, and make new friendships with the neighbours, occupy with his men the fortresses, corrupt the princes of his army, and of those whom he cannot corrupt secure himself, and by these ways seek to punish his lord for that ingratitude which he would use towards him.

## NM-DISC.1.30.03

There are no other paths; but, as was said above, men do not know how to be either wholly wicked or wholly good. And it always befalls that at once after the victory, to leave the army they do not wish, to carry themselves modestly they cannot, to use violent bearings, and such as have the honourable in them, they do not know how. So that, standing ambiguous, between that delay of theirs and ambiguity, they are oppressed.

## NM-DISC.1.30.04

As to a republic, wishing to flee this vice of the ungrateful [one], the same remedy cannot be given as to the prince; that is, that it should go and not send on its expeditions, being necessitated to send a citizen of its own. It is fitting, on that account, that for a remedy I give it that it hold the same ways that the Roman republic held, so as to be less ungrateful than the others; which arose from the ways of its government. For, the whole city, both the nobles and the ignoble, employing itself in the war, there rose always in Rome, in every age, virtuous men, and adorned with various victories, that the people had no cause to doubt any one of them, [they] being many, and the one watching the other. And they maintained themselves so far whole, and respectful of giving no shadow of any ambition, nor cause to the people to offend them as ambitious, that, coming to the dictatorship, that one carried away greater glory from it who laid it down the sooner. And so, like ways not being able to generate suspicion, they did not generate ingratitude. In such a way that a republic that does not wish to have cause to be ungrateful must govern itself as Rome; and a citizen who wishes to flee those bites of its must observe the bearings observed by the Roman citizens.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Quali modi debbe usare un Principe o una Repubblica per fuggire questo vizio della ingratitudine, e quali quel Capitano o quel Cittadino per non essere oppresso da quella`. "Which ways a prince or a republic must use to flee this vice of ingratitude, and which that captain or that citizen [must use] so as not to be oppressed by it": `debbe`, must (D274); `fuggire`, flee, the chapter's verb four times; `quel Capitano o quel Cittadino`, "that captain or that citizen", the demonstrative pointing back to the captain of I.29. The rubric names the rulers, then the men they send; the chapter takes each ruler with his man, the prince and the captain, then the republic and the citizen, the last in one clause. Guicciardini answers only the prince (Guicciardini .01).

**`Un Principe per fuggire questa necessità di avere a vivere con sospetto, o essere ingrato`.** "A prince, to flee this necessity of having to live with suspicion, or be ungrateful": the necessity is I.29's, where suspicion was found `tanto ... naturale ... nei Principi` that they cannot use gratitude. `avere a` + infinitive of necessity, "have to" (D61, D261); the bare `essere` hangs on it too, and the English keeps the Italian's comma so that "be ungrateful" is the second thing the prince would have to do, not a second purpose.

**`debbe personalmente andare nelle espedizioni`.** "Must personally go on the expeditions": `espedizione`, expedition, as I.29's `una espedizione importante`; `personalmente`, personally, the word Guicciardini takes up (Guicciardini .01). The remedy removes the cause: if the prince wins, there is no captain's glory to suspect.

**`come facevano nel principio quelli Imperadori romani`.** "As those Roman emperors did in the beginning": `principio`, beginning (glossary). Machiavelli names none.

**`come fa ne' tempi nostri il Turco`.** "As the Turk does in our times": the Ottoman sultan, presumably Selim I, the `Gran Turco` of I.1, who led his campaigns of 1514-17 in person; the date of the chapter is not fixed. The 1554 edition reads `a i tempi nostri`, the same sense.

**`e come hanno fatto e fanno quelli che sono virtuosi`.** "And as those who are virtuous have done and do": `virtuoso`, the adjective of *virtù* (glossary), of princes. Canestrini's note to Guicciardini's reply paraphrases this whole first sentence; it is his, and is not carried.

**`Perché vincendo, la gloria e lo acquisto è tutto loro`.** "For, winning, the glory and the acquisition is all theirs": the singular verb with the double subject is kept, the two taken as one thing; `acquisto`, acquisition, with `acquistare`, acquire (glossary); `gloria`, glory.

**`e quando non vi sono, sendo la gloria d'altrui`.** "And when they are not there, the glory being another's": `vi`, there, on the expedition.

**`non pare loro potere usare quello acquisto, s'ei non spengono in altrui quella gloria`.** "It does not seem to them that they can use that acquisition if they do not extinguish in another that glory": `spegnere`, extinguish. The 1554 edition reads `se non spengono`, without the pronoun.

**`che loro non hanno saputo guadagnarsi`.** "Which they have not known how to gain for themselves": `guadagnare`, gain (glossary); `sapere` + infinitive, know how. The 1554 edition reads `eßi` for `loro`.

**`e diventare ingrati ed ingiusti`.** "And become ungrateful and unjust": the infinitive has no clear governor. It may run on from `potere`, what the prince seems to himself to have to do, or stand loosely as the consequence of extinguishing the glory. The English keeps the loose coordination, and no note settles it.

**`e senza dubbio è maggiore la loro perdita, che il guadagno`.** "And without doubt their loss is greater than the gain": plain English order (D263, exposition). `perdita`, `guadagno`, loss, gain (FG I.23).

**`Ma quando o per negligenza o per poca prudenza e' si rimangono a casa oziosi`.** "But when, either through negligence or through little prudence, they remain at home idle": `e'`, they, the princes; `ozioso`, idle, with `ozio`, idleness (glossary). The two causes are faults, and Machiavelli leaves the prince who commits them to his own knowledge.

**`io non ho che precetto dar loro altro, che quello che per loro medesimi si sanno`.** "I have no precept to give them other than what they know by themselves": `precetto`, precept. Machiavelli declines to advise the prince who will not go. Guicciardini calls the advice of the first sentence the Discourse's `ricordo` (Guicciardini .01).

**`Ma dico bene a quel Capitano, giudicando che non possa fuggire i morsi della ingratitudine`.** "But I say indeed to that captain, judging that he cannot flee the bites of ingratitude": `bene` strengthens the verb, "indeed", as D174's `Debbe bene`, against the precept withheld from the prince. The gerund's subject is not expressed: it may be Machiavelli, who judges the captain's case, or the captain, judging his own. The English keeps both. The 1554 edition makes it Machiavelli's, `che giudicando io, ch'ei non possa fuggire`, "since I judge that he cannot flee"; the 1824 is kept as printed (D320; NM-DISC-I30-D003). `morsi`, bites, the word Guicciardini uses of princes' harder ingratitude at FG I.29.

**`che faccia una delle due cose`.** "That he should do one of the two things": the article kept; the two are offered as the only ones (`Altre vie non ci sono`, .03).

**`o subito dopo la vittoria lasci l'esercito, e rimettasi nelle mani del suo Principe`.** "Either at once after the victory leave the army, and put himself in the hands of his prince": `subito`, at once (D177), the word I.29 used of the suspicion that arises `subito ... dopo la vittoria`.

**`guardandosi da ogni atto insolente o ambizioso`.** "Guarding himself from every insolent or ambitious act": I.29 made the captain's `modo o termine usato insolentemente` the thing that increases the prince's suspicion; `insolente`, insolent (glossary `insolenza`).

**`acciocché quello spogliato d'ogni sospetto abbia cagione o di premiarlo, o di non l'offendere`.** "So that that one, stripped of every suspicion, may have cause either to reward him or not to offend him": `quello`, that one, the prince, as `quello` in .04; `premiare`, reward; `offendere`, offend, the two of I.29 .02. The 1554 edition reads `l'offenderlo`.

**`o quando questo non gli paja di fare`.** "Or, when this does not seem to him [good] to do": `parere di` + infinitive, to see fit; the supply is bracketed (D53).

**`prenda animosamente la parte contraria`.** "Spiritedly take the contrary part": `animosamente`, from `animo`, spirit (glossary), so "spiritedly", before its verb as in the Italian. The 1554 edition reads `prende`, the indicative; the 1824 subjunctive runs with `faccia`, `tenga`, `occupi`, and is kept (D319; NM-DISC-I30-D002).

**`e tenga tutti quelli modi, per li quali creda che quello acquisto sia suo proprio e non del Principe suo`.** "And hold all those ways by which he may believe that acquisition to be his own and not his prince's": `tenere`, hold (D242); `modi`, ways (glossary); the subjunctive `creda`, "may believe". The acquisition of .01 is now the captain's to keep.

**`facendosi benevoli i soldati ed i sudditi`.** "Making the soldiers and the subjects well-disposed to himself": `benevolo`, well-disposed, wishing him well.

**`occupi con li suoi uomini le Fortezze`.** "Occupy with his men the fortresses": in the Italian's order; `Fortezze`, fortresses, strongholds (D312), as I.29 .05, where Ferdinand took them from Consalvo.

**`corrompa i principi del suo esercito`.** "Corrupt the princes of his army": `principi` here are the chief men of the army, its commanders. The glossary's "prince" is held, as D05 held it where the draft had "the other leading men of the city"; the witness prints the word lower case, against `Principe`, the sovereign, capitalised throughout, a distinction the English cannot carry. `corrompere`, corrupt (glossary), as with bribes at I.8.

**`e di quelli che non può corrompere si assicuri`.** "And of those whom he cannot corrupt secure himself": the fronted object kept as a contrast with `corrompa i principi` (D263); `assicurarsi di`, secure oneself of (D219), the phrase I.29 used of the prince's security against the captain.

**`e per questi modi cerchi di punire il suo Signore di quella ingratitudine che esso gli userebbe`.** "And by these ways seek to punish his lord for that ingratitude which he would use towards him": `usare`, use, the verb of ingratitude through I.29 (D313); the conditional `userebbe`, "would use", makes the punishment pre-emptive. `Signore`, lord, kept apart from `Principe`, prince, as at I.29.

**`Altre vie non ci sono`.** "There are no other paths": `via`, path (D131), against `modi`, ways.

**`come di sopra si disse, gli uomini non sanno essere né al tutto tristi, né al tutto buoni`.** "As was said above, men do not know how to be either wholly wicked or wholly good": I.27's rubric uses `al tutto` in nearly these words, and I.26 closes on `non sanno essere né tutti buoni, né tutti cattivi`; `tristo`, wicked (D03).

**`E sempre interviene che subito dopo la vittoria, lasciare lo esercito non vogliono, portarsi modestamente non possono, usare termini violenti, e che abbiano in sé l'onorevole, non sanno`.** "And it always befalls that at once after the victory, to leave the army they do not wish, to carry themselves modestly they cannot, to use violent bearings, and such as have the honourable in them, they do not know how": a catalogue of three objects fronted before their verbs, kept in the Italian's order (D263, a catalogue; D194). `volere`, `potere`, `sapere`: will, power, knowledge, each failing in turn. `intervenire`, befall (D298). `termine`, bearing, conduct, as I.29's `modo o termine`; `portarsi`, carry oneself, kept off "bear" so that the two Italian words do not share an English root.

**`Talché stando ambigui, tra quella loro dimora e ambiguità, sono oppressi`.** "So that, standing ambiguous, between that delay of theirs and ambiguity, they are oppressed": `ambigui`, `ambiguità`, one root kept; `dimora`, delay, lingering; `opprimere`, oppress, the rubric's word, the landing set off by the Italian's comma. The 1554 edition reads `dubbiosi`, "doubtful", the same sense.

**`Quanto ad una Repubblica, volendo fuggire questo vizio dello ingrato`.** "As to a republic, wishing to flee this vice of the ungrateful [one]": the gerund's subject is the republic; `dello ingrato`, of the ungrateful one, varying the rubric's `della ingratitudine`.

**`cioè che vada e non mandi nelle espedizioni sue, sendo necessitata a mandare un suo cittadino`.** "That is, that it should go and not send on its expeditions, being necessitated to send a citizen of its own": a republic cannot go in person; whoever leads its army is a citizen.

**`che la tenga i medesimi modi che tenne la Repubblica romana, ad esser meno ingrata che le altre`.** "That it hold the same ways that the Roman republic held, so as to be less ungrateful than the others": `la`, it, the republic, as subject. At I.29 Rome was the least ungrateful of all the peoples that ever had empire.

**`Perché adoperandosi t[u]tta la Città, e gli Nobili e gl'Ignobili, nella guerra`.** "For, the whole city, both the nobles and the ignoble, employing itself in the war": the reflexive kept, and the apposition beside its noun; `Nobili`, nobles; `Ignobili`, the ignoble (D118). The 1824 prints `tntta`, an inverted or foul sort on a clean page, which both readers saw; emended from the 1554 `tutta` (D318; NM-DISC-I30-D001).

**`surgeva sempre in Roma in ogni età uomini virtuosi, e ornati di varie vittorie, che il Popolo non aveva cagione di dubitare di alcuno di loro`.** "There rose always in Rome, in every age, virtuous men, and adorned with various victories, that the people had no cause to doubt any one of them": the singular verb with the plural subject kept as "there rose"; `dubitare di`, doubt, apprehension (D205). The `che` has no `tanti` to answer: the 1554 edition prints `tanti huomini uirtuosi`, "so many virtuous men ... that", which makes the people's ease a result of their number; the 1824 construes loosely and is kept, and the English keeps its looseness (D321; NM-DISC-I30-D004).

**`sendo assai, e guardando l'uno l'altro`.** "[They] being many, and the one watching the other": `assai`, of number, many (glossary, I.7); `guardare`, watch: each a check on the others, where the captain of .02 could only guard himself.

**`E intanto si mantenevano interi, e rispettivi di non dare ombra di alcuna ambizione`.** "And they maintained themselves so far whole, and respectful of giving no shadow of any ambition": `intanto ... che`, "so far ... that" (D174), the 1554 printing it in two words; `mantenere`, maintain (D220); `interi`, whole, uncorrupted; `rispettivo`, respectful (D155), here of the care that keeps a man from seeming ambitious; `ombra`, shadow, as I.25's `l'ombra ... de' modi antichi`. The 1554 edition reads `con rispetto`, the same sense.

**`né cagione al Popolo come ambiziosi d'offenderli`.** "Nor cause to the people to offend them as ambitious": the people's `offendere` answers the prince's of .02.

**`che venendo alla Dittatura, quello maggior gloria ne riportava, che più tosto la deponeva`.** "That, coming to the dictatorship, that one carried away greater glory from it who laid it down the sooner": `Dittatura`, dictatorship, the Roman magistracy for an emergency, held for six months at most; `riportare`, carry away, as Antonius' reward at I.29; `deporre`, lay down (glossary); `più tosto`, the sooner, of time, where I.29's `piuttosto` was "rather". Machiavelli names no dictator.

**`E così non potendo simili modi generare sospetto, non generavano ingratitudine`.** "And so, like ways not being able to generate suspicion, they did not generate ingratitude": `simile`, like (glossary); the chain of I.29 run backwards: no suspicion, no ingratitude.

**`In modo che una Repubblica che non voglia avere cagione d'essere ingrata, si debbe governare come Roma`.** "In such a way that a republic that does not wish to have cause to be ungrateful must govern itself as Rome": `in modo che`, in such a way that (glossary); `si debbe`, must (D274); no verb supplied after "as Rome" (D242).

**`e uno cittadino che voglia fuggire quelli suoi morsi, debbe osservare i termini osservati dai cittadini romani`.** "And a citizen who wishes to flee those bites of its must observe the bearings observed by the Roman citizens": `suoi`, its, the republic's or ingratitude's, and the English leaves it open; `termini`, bearings, as in .03; the repetition `osservare ... osservati` kept. The rubric's last part is answered in one clause.
