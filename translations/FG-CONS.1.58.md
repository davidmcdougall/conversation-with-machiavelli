# Consideration on chapter LVIII of Book I

*The multitude is wiser and more constant than a prince*

## FG-CONS.1.58.01

A difficult enterprise, and very alien from the opinion of men, he takes up, without doubt, who attributes to the people constancy and prudence, and who in these two qualities sets it before princes; who, when they are regulated by the laws, no one who has written of political things ever doubted that the government of one was better than that of a multitude even regulated by the laws, to which not only the government of one prince, but also that of the optimates, is preferred. Because where there is a lesser number, *virtù* is more united, and more able to produce its effects; there is more order in things, more thought and examination, in affairs more resolution; but where there is a multitude, there there is confusion; and in so much dissonance of brains, where there are various judgments, various thoughts, various ends, there can be neither reasonable discourse, nor founded resolution, nor firm action. Men are lightly moved by every vain suspicion, by every vain uproar; they do not discern, they do not distinguish, and with the same levity they return to the deliberations that they had earlier condemned, to hating what they loved, to loving what they hated; therefore not without cause is the multitude likened to the waves of the sea, which, according to the winds that blow, go now here, now there, without any rule, without any firmness. In sum, it cannot be denied that a people by itself is an ark of ignorance and of confusion; therefore merely popular governments have been in every place little durable, and, besides infinite tumults and disorders, of which they have been full while they lasted, they have brought forth either tyranny or the last ruin of their city.

## FG-CONS.1.58.02

The examples are so many and so known that there is no occasion to repeat them, and such that deservedly they have brought forth that most ancient and common opinion of all the writers, that in the multitude there is neither prudence nor constancy. Neither the reasons nor the examples alleged by the author of the Discourse are repugnant to it, for one who considers well; because, in so far as he alleges that in a people regulated by the laws there is no less *virtù* or prudence than in a prince regulated by the laws, and adduces for an example the Roman people, I say principally that neither the reason nor his example is to the purpose of the case, because it is one thing to consider a multitude that deliberates by itself, another a popular government ordered in such a way that the grave and important deliberations are to be made by the more prudent. In the first case there will often be variety, ignorance and confusion, and let the multitude be regulated by the laws as much as it will; in the second case, if things are deliberated prudently and stably, it does not proceed from those defects not being in the multitude, but from their not being in those more prudent. Such was the Roman people, in which the most important things were deliberated by the senate, by the consuls and by the principal magistrates, and in which, if the multitude had had to deliberate, even though it had been regulated by good laws, full of customs holy and most careful of its liberty, there would have appeared many times in its deliberations, with most grave damage to its republic, that imprudence and variety which the writers reprove in the other multitudes.

## FG-CONS.1.58.03

Afterwards, even if we called the deliberations of the Romans deliberations of the multitude, let there be taken on the other side a prince who is among the other princes in that degree of *virtù* that the Roman people was among the other peoples; I believe without doubt he will proceed in all his things with greater prudence and with greater constancy than the Roman people would proceed; because, for the reasons said above, where the terms are even, there is more order, more distinction, more resolution, more firmness in one than in many. And on the contrary, if one takes a people unbound from the laws and a prince free and unbound, such as almost all are, and those of France too, whom the Author calls bound, in whose power it is in their kingdom to do what they wish, I say that in a prince there may be found perhaps more other vices than in a people, and more promptness to execute them than a people has, which when the Author discourses, he departs from the terms of his question; but commonly there would be found more prudence and more constancy, which is precisely the title of the Author, than would be found in a multitude; in which, when it is unbound, one will never see anything but imprudence and inconstancy, appetite for new things, immoderate suspicion, infinite envy against all those who have substance or quality. And although among princes some are found most imprudent, and their imprudence, when it is in that last species, is perhaps more pernicious than that of the multitude, I say that, taking, *verbi gratia*, two hundred years of a kingdom, one would find prudent and imprudent kings; but taking two hundred years of a multitude, one would find a continuation of imprudence and of variety. Nor are the examples by which it is shown that in a prince there are many more defects than in a people to the purpose, because the assumption is not to dispute of the other vices, but only whether in peoples there is more imprudence and inconstancy than in princes. So too it is impertinent to say that a city makes more augmentation under a popular government than under a prince, because that arises from other causes; but if you gave me fifty years of a good popular government and as many of a prince equally good, I do not doubt that it would make greater augmentation under a prince. But the successors not being, later, always alike makes the augmentation of the popular government go on more continuously than that of a principate, and it can very well stand together that it is a better fortune for a city to fall into popular government than under princes, which consideration is outside our dispute; and nonetheless that there is ordinarily more imprudence and more inconstancy in a people than in a prince

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini's setting of the chapter title: Machiavelli's rubric with `principe` lower case and no stop. It is heading apparatus, not Guicciardini's (D32). The readers split on the adjective, `savia` and `savio`; only the feminine agrees with `moltitudine`, and the 1933 edition and Machiavelli's rubric read `savia` (FG-CONS-I58-D002, D395). Guicciardini calls it `il titulo dello Autore` (.03).

**`Difficile impresa e molto aliena dalla opinione degli uomini piglia, sanza dubio, chi attribuisce al populo la constanza e la prudenza`.** "A difficult enterprise, and very alien from the opinion of men, he takes up, without doubt, who attributes to the people constancy and prudence": the object is fronted and the subject, `chi`, comes last, and the English keeps the order (D263, a contrast): Machiavelli did not know whether he was taking upon himself a hard province, `Io non so se io mi prenderò una provincia dura` (Machiavelli .01); Guicciardini says without doubt that he has. `molto aliena dalla opinione degli uomini` answers Machiavelli's `contro alla comune opinione` (Machiavelli .05). `impresa`, enterprise (glossary); `pigliare`, take (glossary). `populo`, people; `constanza`, constancy, Machiavelli's `costante`; `prudenza`, prudence (glossary).

**`e chi in queste due qualità lo antepone a' principi, e' quali quando sono regolati dalle leggi, nessuno che ha scritto delle cose politiche dubitò mai che il governo di uno non fussi migliore`.** "And who in these two qualities sets it before princes; who, when they are regulated by the laws, no one who has written of political things ever doubted that the government of one was better": `e' quali` opens a relative that is never completed, and the sentence restarts with `nessuno`; the English keeps the break. After `dubitò`, `non` is the pleonastic negative of the verb of doubting and is not rendered; nobody doubted that the government of one is better. `antepone`, sets before; `regolati`, regulated, Machiavelli's participle (Machiavelli .02). `qualità`, quality (glossary).

**`alla quale è preposto non solo il governo di uno principe, ma ancora quello degli ottimati`.** "To which not only the government of one prince, but also that of the optimates, is preferred": the order normalised (D263). `preposto` of one thing set above another in judgment, "preferred"; the glossary's "appointed" (NM I.10) is the sense of a man placed over a charge. (D401) `ottimati`, optimates (glossary).

**`Perché dove è minore numero, è la virtù più unita, e più abile a produrre gli effetti suoi`.** "Because where there is a lesser number, *virtù* is more united, and more able to produce its effects": *virtù* untranslated (D16); `abile a` + infinitive, "able to"; D374's "eligible" is scoped to `abili al magistrato`, fit for an office (FG I.47). (D401) `esamine`, examination; `negozii`, affairs; `resoluzione`, resolution, three times in the reply.

**`ma dove è moltitudine, quivi è confusione`.** "But where there is a multitude, there there is confusion": `quivi`, there, resuming `dove`, kept. `dissonanza di cervelli`, dissonance of brains; `giudicii`, judgments (glossary); `discorso ragionevole`, reasonable discourse, the *currere* noun (D01): in the multitude there can be no reasonable discourse. `fondata`, founded (glossary, `fondare`).

**`Muovonsi gli uomini leggermente per ogni vano sospetto, per ogni vano romore`.** "Men are lightly moved by every vain suspicion, by every vain uproar": `romore`, uproar (D155), a stir without a stated content, not D362's "rumour". `sospetto`, suspicion (glossary); `leggerezza`, levity (glossary), with `leggermente`, lightly; `dannare`, condemn (D99); `prima`, earlier (D334).

**`non discernono, non distinguono, e con la medesima leggerezza`.** "They do not discern, they do not distinguish, and with the same levity": the readers split on the stop after `distinguono`, comma or semicolon; read with the 1933 edition as a comma (FG-CONS-I58-D004, D110).

**`però non sanza cagione è assomigliata la moltitudine alle onde del mare`.** "Therefore not without cause is the multitude likened to the waves of the sea": `però`, therefore (D22). The fronted `non sanza cagione` is kept: it answers Machiavelli's `non senza cagione si assomiglia la voce d'un Popolo a quella di Dio` (Machiavelli .07) in nearly his words, the voice of God turned into the waves of the sea (D263, a contrast). `regola`, rule (glossary); `fermezza`, firmness.

**`In somma e' non si può negare che uno populo per sé medesimo non sia una arca di ignoranza e di confusione`.** "In sum, it cannot be denied that a people by itself is an ark of ignorance and of confusion": `non` after `negare` is pleonastic, as after `dubitò` above. `arca`, ark, a chest, a vessel full of; `ignoranza`, ignorance (glossary). The stop before `però` was split between the readers and is read with the 1933 edition as a semicolon (FG-CONS-I58-D004, D110).

**`però e' governi meramente populari sono stati in ogni luogo poco durabili`.** "Therefore merely popular governments have been in every place little durable": `però`, therefore (D22); `meramente`, merely (D298); `tumulti`, tumults (glossary); `partorire`, bring forth, twice in the reply; `tirannide`, tyranny (glossary); `ultima ruina`, the last ruin.

**`Gli esempli sono tanti e sì noti che non accade replicargli`.** "The examples are so many and so known that there is no occasion to repeat them": `non accade`, the idiom, there is no occasion; D298's "happen" is for events, and the idiom lies outside it. `sì` was doubtful to both readers, who preferred `si`; only the adverb construes, as the 1933 edition reads (FG-CONS-I58-D002, D398). `meritamente`, deservedly (D374); `comune di tutti gli scrittori`, common of all the writers, Machiavelli's `tutti gli scrittori` (Machiavelli .01).

**`Alla quale non repugnano, chi bene considera, né le ragioni né gli esempli allegati per lo autore del Discorso`.** "Neither the reasons nor the examples alleged by the author of the Discourse are repugnant to it, for one who considers well": the order is normalised, as exposition (D263). `repugnare`, be repugnant to; `allegare`, allege, Machiavelli's own verb (Machiavelli .06, .09); `il Discorso`, the Discourse, his name for Machiavelli's chapter (FG I.49). The Discourse's author is lower case here and `Autore` capitalised in .03, as Canestrini prints them.

**`e adduce per esemplo il populo romano`.** "And adduces for an example the Roman people": `per esemplo`, for an example (glossary). `né lo es[e]mplo suo fa a proposito del caso`: "nor his example is to the purpose of the case". Canestrini prints `esamplo`, read by both readers at an a-shaped letter, a line after `esemplo`; the 1933 edition reads `esemplo`: emended (FG-CONS-I58-D001, D396).

**`altro uno governo populare ordinato in modo che le deliberazioni gravi e importanti abbino a essere fatte da' più prudenti`.** "Another a popular government ordered in such a way that the grave and important deliberations are to be made by the more prudent": `avere a` destinative, "be to" (D261); `in modo che`, in such a way that (glossary); `deliberazione`, deliberation (glossary). Machiavelli's `Popolo ... bene ordinato` (Machiavelli .05) is the ordered government; Guicciardini moves the prudence from the people to its magistrates.

**`e sia la moltitudine regolata dalle leggi quanto vuole`.** "And let the multitude be regulated by the laws as much as it will": a concessive subjunctive. `non procede perché`: "it does not proceed from", `procedere`, proceed (glossary), the cause. `prudentemente e stabilmente`, prudently and stably, take up Machiavelli's `prudente`, `stabile` (Machiavelli .05, .07); `quelli difetti`, those defects, his `difetto` (Machiavelli .01, .02).

**`da' principali magistrati, [e] nel quale se la moltitudine avessi avuto a deliberare`.** "By the principal magistrates, and in which, if the multitude had had to deliberate": two relatives on `il populo romano`. Canestrini prints `o`, or, read by both readers and seen by the collator; it sets up no alternative, and the 1933 edition reads `e`: emended (FG-CONS-I58-D001, D397). `avere a` after a pluperfect, "have to" (D261).

**`ancora che fussi regolata di buone leggi, piena di costumi santi e accuratissimi della sua libertà`.** "Even though it had been regulated by good laws, full of customs holy and most careful of its liberty": `ancora che`, even though (glossary); `di` of the agent, "by"; `costumi`, customs (D41); `santo`, holy (D313). `accuratissimi` agrees with `costumi`, and the English sets both adjectives after the noun so that the care for liberty stays with the customs, not with the multitude. The 1933 edition reads `amantissimi`, most loving; Canestrini construes and is kept (FG-CONS-I58-D003, D400). `riprendere`, reprove (D173).

**`Di poi quando bene noi chiamassimo le deliberazioni de' Romani deliberazioni della moltitudine`.** "Afterwards, even if we called the deliberations of the Romans deliberations of the multitude": `di poi`, afterwards (D187), here the next step of the argument; `quando bene`, even if (glossary); `grado`, degree (glossary). `piglisi al rincontro uno principe`, "let there be taken on the other side a prince": `al rincontro`, on the other side (D24), reverses Machiavelli's `all'incontro loro si debbe porre una moltitudine` (Machiavelli .02), where a multitude was set against the bound kings; here a prince is set against the Roman people. The glossary keeps the two words apart, so the English shows the answer only in this note.

**`credo sanza dubio procederà in tutte le sue cose con maggiore prudenza e con maggiore constanza che non procederia il populo romano`.** "I believe without doubt he will proceed in all his things with greater prudence and with greater constancy than the Roman people would proceed": `credo`, I believe (glossary), the reply's only `credo`, strengthened by `sanza dubio`, without doubt: belief, not certainty. `procederia`, conditional, "would proceed"; the 1933 edition reads `procedeva`, "proceeded" (FG-CONS-I58-D003, D400). The semicolon before `credo` is Canestrini's.

**`dove e' termini siano pari, è più ordine, più distinzione, più resoluzione, più fermezza in uno che in molti`.** "Where the terms are even, there is more order, more distinction, more resolution, more firmness in one than in many": `termini`, terms, the conditions compared, as FG I.3's *data paritate terminorum*; D325's "bearings" is the sense of conduct. (D401) `pari`, even (D262). The comma after `ordine` was doubtful to both readers and is read with the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-I58-D004, D110).

**`E pel contrario se si piglia uno populo sciolto dalle leggi e uno principe libero e sciolto`.** "And on the contrary, if one takes a people unbound from the laws and a prince free and unbound": `sciolto`, unbound, Machiavelli's word (Machiavelli .02, .10); `pel contrario`, on the contrary, which shares the English with `anzi` reversing (D135, Machiavelli .02), a recorded collapse (D401).

**`e quegli di Francia ancora, che lo Autore chiama legati`.** "And those of France too, whom the Author calls bound": Machiavelli did not use `legati`; he said France's kingdom was `moderato più dalle leggi` and set its kings among the princes held by laws (Machiavelli .02). Guicciardini's word for Machiavelli's claim is the one he contests. `in potestà de' quali è nel regno suo fare ciò che vogliono`: "in whose power it is in their kingdom to do what they wish"; `potestà`, power (D78); `suo`, their, with the plural `vogliono`.

**`dico che in uno principe si potrà trovare forse più altri vizii che in uno populo`.** "I say that in a prince there may be found perhaps more other vices than in a people": `forse`, perhaps, a real concession, limited at once to vices other than imprudence and inconstancy; `vizio`, vice (glossary); `prontezza`, promptness, with `pronto`, prompt (D298); `eseguire`, execute (glossary).

**`e' quali quando lo Autore discorre si parte da' termini della sua quistione`.** "Which when the Author discourses, he departs from the terms of his question": `e' quali`, the vices, is the object of `discorre`, transitive as Machiavelli's `se si discorreranno tutti i disordini` (Machiavelli .09), and the subject changes to `lo Autore`; the English keeps the shape. `discorrere`, discourse (D01); `quistione`, question.

**`ma comunemente si troveria più prudenza e più constanza, che è proprio il titulo dello Autore, che non si troveria in una moltitudine`.** "But commonly there would be found more prudence and more constancy, which is precisely the title of the Author, than would be found in a multitude": `comunemente`, commonly, a limit on the claim; `troveria`, conditional, here and twice below; the 1933 edition reads `troverrà`, will be found, each time (FG-CONS-I58-D003, D400). `il titulo dello Autore` is Machiavelli's rubric, which names wisdom and constancy; Guicciardini keeps to it. `facultà`, substance (D198); `appetito`, appetite (D117); `invidia`, envy (glossary).

**`E se bene de' principi se ne truova imprudentissimi`.** "And although among princes some are found most imprudent": `se bene`, although (D31); `ne` partitive, "some". `quella ultima spezie`, that last species (glossary): either the unbound prince just set against the unbound people, or the extreme of imprudence named in `imprudentissimi`; the English leaves it as open as the Italian. `pernizioso`, pernicious (glossary); *verbi gratia*, Latin, "for example", literally "for the sake of a word" (latin.md). `de' re prudenti e imprudenti`: kings both prudent and imprudent.

**`perché lo assunto non è disputare degli altri vizii, ma solo se ne' populi è più imprudenza e inconstanza che ne' principi`.** "Because the assumption is not to dispute of the other vices, but only whether in peoples there is more imprudence and inconstancy than in princes": `assunto`, the assumption in its older sense, the proposition taken up to be proved, as the `impresa` taken up in the first line; `disputare`, dispute, the verb of Machiavelli's last sentence (Machiavelli .12).

**`Così è impertinente il dire che più augumento fa una città sotto uno governo populare che sotto uno principe`.** "So too it is impertinent to say that a city makes more augmentation under a popular government than under a prince": `Così` comparative, as with the examples of other vices, not consecutive (that is `però`'s, D22). `impertinente`, not pertaining to the question; `augumento`, augmentation (D69), Machiavelli's `augumenti eccessivi` (Machiavelli .08). `perché nasce da altre cagioni`, "because that arises from other causes", answers Machiavelli's `Il che non può nascere da altro` (Machiavelli .08) in his verb, `nascere`, arise (D04). `altanti`, as many. `non dubito`, "I do not doubt", his first person, under the hypothesis of fifty good years each.

**`Ma non essere poi sempre e' successori simili, fa che lo augumento del governo populare va più continuando che quello di uno principato`.** "But the successors not being, later, always alike makes the augmentation of the popular government go on more continuously than that of a principate": the infinitive clause is the subject of `fa`; `poi`, later (D250), the successors after a good first prince. `successori`, successors (glossary); `simile`, alike (glossary); `principato`, principate (glossary).

**`e può molto bene stare insieme, che sia migliore fortuna di una città a cadere in governo populare che sotto a principi`.** "And it can very well stand together that it is a better fortune for a city to fall into popular government than under princes": `a principi` was read by both readers without the apostrophe; the 1933 edition reads `e' principi`; kept (FG-CONS-I58-D003, D399). `fuora della disputa nostra`, outside our dispute: he does not argue the point.

**`e nondimeno, che ordinariamente sia più imprudenza e più inconstanza in uno populo che in uno principe`.** "And nonetheless that there is ordinarily more imprudence and more inconstancy in a people than in a prince": the second thing that can stand together with the first, after Canestrini's semicolon, which keeps it apart from what is `fuora della disputa nostra`. `nondimeno`, nonetheless (D45); `ordinariamente`, ordinarily, after the verb (D151), the limit on the conclusion. The reply ends without a stop, and so does the English (D40).
