# Consideration on chapter XII

## FG-CONS.2.12.01

If in the present Discourse many examples are found for the one and the other opinion, there are also many reasons which make the case so doubtful that it is not of easy resolution, and, [for one] wishing to deliberate it well, [it] has need of many considerations which have been passed over by the Author. For that distinction alone is not enough: either I have the subjects armed or they are disarmed; but it is necessary to think further: either my peoples are faithful or they are inclined to rebellions; either the towns are strong, or they are weak; either I can, even though I have the war at home that consumes my revenues, as to money sustain it long, or I could not uphold it. One has also to consider the conditions of the enemy, that is, what soldiery he has, what countries, what revenues, what way to sustain the war at home, what way to make it outside home; because the government and all the proceedings of the war have always to be regulated according to the qualities and the progress of the enemy. There is a difference also, when I await war from others, [between] saying: I carry it to his home; [and] saying: I go out of my country, and meet the enemy outside his country (and this is the example of King Ferdinand). There is a difference [between] saying: I begin the war on his [ground] before he has begun it on me; [and] saying: I have the war already at home, but to constrain the enemy to depart from it, I begin it also on his, as Scipio did when Hannibal was in Italy, as Agathocles did, besieged by Carthaginians, as the Florentines [did] so many times in the wars made on them by the Visconti. And as to this last case, I shall always judge that whoever has the war at home, if he has opportunity at the same time to begin it in that of the enemy, must do it; because, being a thing unexpected, it disorders all the enemy's designs; and every small success that he may have there constrains him to withdraw with all or with part of his forces to defend his home; and it befalls as with the remedies that these physicians use to cure sicknesses, among which diversion is always judged a powerful remedy and much approved.

## FG-CONS.2.12.02

There remains the resolution of the other cases, in which, proceeding by distinction, I say that when the enemy from whom you fear the war has more army and more power than you, that you cannot think of making the war on him at home, because many forces and many opportunities are required to carry the war to the home of others, which are not so necessary to whoever makes war at his own home, because he makes use of the favour of the country, of the subjects, and of the difficulties of the enemies, with which remedies he can go on temporizing. And in this degree was King Ferdinand, who could not put into the open country an army even with that of the enemies. But when you feel yourself, both in troops and in money and in the other opportunities of war, even with the enemy, and ordered with those forces that are necessary to make war at his home, I should be inclined to counsel not awaiting the war at one's own home, because, winning, the reward is greater, that victory being able readily to bring you the acquisition of the kingdom of others, whereas victory at your home brings you nothing other than the liberation of your state; losing, the damage is less, because you lose nothing other than that army, and you have more time to remake yourself; whereas, losing at home, if the enemy accelerates the victory, as Hannibal could do at Cannae, as in modern times Paolo Orsino to Ladislaus, Duke John to King Ferdinand, one battle is enough to make you lose the state. Carrying the war to the enemy's home, you have already disturbed his design of making the war at your home, you have impeded the preparations necessary to this effect, in such a way that, even vanquishing you, he has need of time and of new orders to come to wage war on you at home; which gives you space to reorder and remake yourself. And so much the more readily I should adhere to this conclusion, the more I saw the enemy not to have a strong country, or not to have faithful subjects, or [his] state so conditioned that his revenues could readily be disordered, or it to be difficult for him, if he had a rout, to remake himself in a short space of time. I see that the Romans always, when they could, forestalled the wars at others' home, against Philip king of Macedonia, against Antiochus, against the Carthaginians; and when they did not do it, they were discontented not to have done it. Nor does what the Writer says move me, that if the Romans had had, in so much space of time, those three routs in France that they had in Italy from Hannibal, they would without doubt have been despatched; because an impossible case is set, that whoever has a rout at the home of others, above all in a distant place, could so suddenly, after the first rout, have sent there again, the one after the other, two new armies. And whoever resolves well on the course of fleeing war at home by carrying it to the home of others, goes there on such a foundation that he can as well hope to rout the enemy as fear to be routed; otherwise he awaits it at home, as the Romans did from Hannibal; who, being already for many years, as Livy says, inexpert in war, and having the war with a captain and with soldiers most expert, if they were routed at home, would perhaps more readily have been, at the beginning of the war, routed by him in Spain or in Africa

## Notes

**The heading and the rubric.** Canestrini's heading, CONSIDERAZIONE SUL CAPITOLO XII, without the `del libro II` he prints over the reply to II.10; the column is headed as the page is (D443). His rubric is Machiavelli's word for word (Machiavelli, rubric); the stop after it, doubtful to both readers, is not set (D440). His two notes, on Ferdinand of Naples and on Paolo Orsini, are his commentary and are not carried.

**`Se nel presente Discorso si truovano esempli assai nell'una e l'altra opinione`.** "If in the present Discourse many examples are found for the one and the other opinion": `Discorso`, Discourse, the chapter; `esempli`, examples; `assai` of number, "many" (glossary, I.7), here and at `ragioni assai`, which answers Machiavelli's `ne ho sentito addurre ragioni da ogni parte` (Machiavelli .01). `opinione`, opinion (D02).

**`sì dubio, che non è di facile resoluzione`.** "So doubtful that it is not of easy resolution": `facile`, easy (D27); `resoluzione`, resolution, returned to at the head of the second paragraph (`Resta la resoluzione`).

**`a volerlo bene deliberare ha bisogno di molte considerazioni che sono state pretermesse dallo Autore`.** "[For one] wishing to deliberate it well, [it] has need of many considerations which have been passed over by the Author": the subject of `ha bisogno` is the case, supplied in brackets, and `a volerlo` is the condition of the one deliberating; `bisogno`, need (glossary); `considerazione`, consideration (glossary), the word of Canestrini's heading; `pretermettere`, pass over (FG II.proem). `Autore`, the Author, Machiavelli, capitalised as printed, as `lo Scrittore` below.

**`Perché non basta sola quella distinzione`.** "For that distinction alone is not enough": the distinction is Machiavelli's own (`si abbia a fare questa distinzione`, Machiavelli .05), restated in Guicciardini's first person. `bastare`, be enough (D205); `Perché` here is causal and opens the period, "for".

**`o io ho e' sudditi armati o e' sono disarmati`.** "Either I have the subjects armed or they are disarmed": Machiavelli's `paese armato` (Machiavelli .05) becomes `sudditi`, subjects, Machiavelli's word in .07; the article is kept, against the possessive of `e' populi miei` that follows.

**`o e' populi miei sono fedeli o e' sono inclinati alle ribellioni, o le terre sono forti, o le sono deboli`.** "Either my peoples are faithful or they are inclined to rebellions; either the towns are strong, or they are weak": `populi`, peoples (D402); `fedele`, faithful (glossary); `terre`, towns, as Machiavelli's `assai Terre` (Machiavelli .04).

**`ancora che io abbia la guerra in casa che mi consumi le entrate`.** "Even though I have the war at home that consumes my revenues": `ancora che`, even though (D31); `che mi consumi`, a restrictive relative in the subjunctive, without commas; `entrate`, revenues, against Machiavelli's `rendite`, revenues (Machiavelli .05), a recorded collapse (D444). Both readers doubted the last letter of the second `entrate`; read with the 1933 edition (D439).

**`in quanto al danaio sostenerla lungamente, o io non potrei reggerla`.** "As to money sustain it long, or I could not uphold it": `danaio`, money (D431), Canestrini's form of `danaro`, as the 1933 edition prints it; `in quanto al danaio` before the verb, as printed; `lungamente`, long (D140); `sostenere` a war, sustain (D431). `reggere` here bears the war, not a people: "uphold" (D444). "Govern" (D64) stays for rule, and "bear" is `comportare`'s (FG I.39).

**`che milizia ha, che paesi, che entrate, che modo`.** "What soldiery he has, what countries, what revenues, what way": `milizia`, soldiery (D60); `modo`, way (glossary).

**`il governo e tutte le azioni della guerra s'hanno sempre a regolare`.** "The government and all the proceedings of the war have always to be regulated": `governo`, government (D262); `azioni`, proceedings (D358), the doings of a war; `regolare`, regulate (D402); `avere a`, "have to" in the passive (D261).

**`il dire: io la porto a casa sua; il dire: io esco del mio paese`.** "[Between] saying: I carry it to his home; [and] saying: I go out of my country": the brackets supply the terms of the difference, which the Italian sets side by side; `a dire` in the second pair is rendered as `il dire`, the brackets supplying the terms of both differences. The difference is between carrying the war into the enemy's home and meeting him beyond his own frontier.

**`(e questo è lo esemplo del re Ferrando)`.** "(And this is the example of King Ferdinand)": Machiavelli's Ferdinand of Naples (Machiavelli .03), whose counsel was to await the enemy within the kingdom, and whose son sent an army into Romagna. The parenthesis names the example, not which part of it Guicciardini means, and the note does not settle that.

**`È differenza`.** "There is a difference": both readers doubted the accent; only the verb construes, and the 1933 edition reads it (D439).

**`come fe Scipione ... come fece Agatocle ... come e' Fiorentini`.** "As Scipio did ... as Agathocles did ... as the Florentines [did]": the third verb is left to the first two, and supplied in brackets; `tante volte`, so many times; Machiavelli's Scipio and Agathocles (Machiavelli .02), now set as the case of the man who already has the war at home; the Florentines against the Visconti are Guicciardini's own, and answer Machiavelli's Florentines who assailed the duke of Milan (Machiavelli .05).

**`io giudicherò sempre`.** "I shall always judge": `giudicare`, judge (D374), a judgment, the act marked (register, D264).

**`lo debba fare`.** "Must do it": `dovere`, must (D274).

**`ogni piccolo successo che vi abbia, lo constrigne`.** "Every small success that he may have there constrains him": `abbia`, subjunctive, "may have"; the subject is the man who carries the war, and `lo` is the enemy.

**`disordina`.** "It disorders": `disordinare`, disorder (glossary), as `disordinargli le entrate` below.

**`con tutto [o] con parte delle forze sue`.** "With all or with part of his forces": the first letter of p. 64 is broken, both readers taking it for an a; emended from the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-II12-D002, D442). The 1933 edition also reads `tutte` (FG-CONS-II12-D004).

**`remedio potente`.** "A powerful remedy": `potente`, powerful (D06).

**`interviene come de' remedii che usano questi fisici`.** "It befalls as with the remedies that these physicians use": `intervenire`, befall (D298); `rimedio`, `remedio`, remedy (glossary), which returns at `co' quali remedii` (.02); `infermità`, sickness (glossary). `diversione`, diversion, both readers doubting its article, read `la` with the 1933 edition (D439).

**`Resta la resoluzione degli altri casi, ne' quali procedendo per distinzione, dico`.** "There remains the resolution of the other cases, in which, proceeding by distinction, I say": `dico`, I say, Guicciardini's own resolution, and `per distinzione`, his method against the one distinction he has found not enough by itself.

**`quando lo inimico da chi tu [temi] la guerra`.** "When the enemy from whom you fear the war": Canestrini prints `tenti`, which gives the clause no sense; the whole word emended from the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-II12-D001, D441). The case is the rubric's, `temendo di essere assaltato`. Both readers doubted `lo`; read with the 1933 edition (D439).

**`che tu non puoi pensare di fargli la guerra in casa`.** "That you cannot think of making the war on him at home": `che` resumes `dico che` after the `quando` clause, and the English keeps the second "that". The period is complete: the case is stated, then its reason (`perché bisognano`). The second case is built the same way (`Ma quando ..., io sarei inclinato`).

**`perché bisognano molte forze`.** "Because many forces are required": `bisognare`, be required (D260), personal here, with `forze` its subject; see the note on `bisogni` at Machiavelli I.2.

**`si serve del favore del paese`.** "He makes use of the favour of the country": `servirsi`, make use of, kept apart from `valersi`, avail oneself (D162), which is Machiavelli's verb at .02 and .06.

**`può andarsi temporeggiando`.** "He can go on temporizing": `temporeggiare`, temporize, the progressive kept.

**`E in questo grado era il re Ferrando, il quale non poteva mettere in campagna esercito pari a quello degli inimici`.** "And in this degree was King Ferdinand, who could not put into the open country an army even with that of the enemies": the inversion is kept so that the relative clause stays on its noun (D263); `grado`, degree (glossary), as Machiavelli's `ciascuno nel suo grado` (Machiavelli .07); `campagna`, open country (glossary); `pari`, even (D262).

**`e di gente e di danari`.** "Both in troops and in money": `gente`, troops (glossary); `danari`, money (D431).

**`e ordinato di quelle forze`.** "And ordered with those forces": `ordinato di`, ordered with, furnished; `ordinare`, order (glossary), as Machiavelli's `ordinati alla guerra` (Machiavelli .07), which the condition answers.

**`io sarei inclinato a consigliare`.** "I should be inclined to counsel": a conditional, not a verdict; with `aderirei` below, "I should adhere", the second judgment of the reply held in the conditional.

**`perché vincendo, il premio è maggiore ... perdendo, il danno è minore`.** "Because, winning, the reward is greater ... losing, the damage is less": `premio`, reward (glossary); `danno`, damage (D106). The two gerunds are kept as the Italian sets them; `vincere` without an object, win (D431); with an object, vanquish (D313), as `etiam vincendoti`. `facilmente`, readily (D27), here and at `facilmente si potessi disordinargli`, `più facilmente ... rotti`.

**`come potette fare Annibale a Canne, come a' tempi moderni Paolo Orsino a Ladislao, il duca Giovanni al re Ferrando`.** "As Hannibal could do at Cannae, as in modern times Paolo Orsini [did] to Ladislaus, Duke John to King Ferdinand": The second and third members leave the verb to the first, `potette fare`, "could do": the examples are of what the victor was in a position to do, and the English supplies nothing. `Canne`, both readers doubting the last letter, read with the 1933 edition (D439). `Paolo Orsino` as printed (names.md); the identifications are in the commentary as the editor's.

**`una giornata è bastante a farti perdere lo Stato`.** "One battle is enough to make you lose the state": `giornata`, battle (D49).

**`etiam vincendoti`.** "Even vanquishing you": `etiam`, Latin, even, in Guicciardini's Italian (latin.md).

**`ha bisogno di tempo e di nuovi ordini`.** "He has need of time and of new orders": both readers doubted `e`; read with the 1933 edition (D439). `ordini`, orders (glossary).

**`a venire a guerreggiarti in casa`.** "To come to wage war on you at home": `guerreggiare`, wage war, kept apart from `fare guerra`, make war (D444).

**`il che ti dà spazio a riordinarti e rifarti`.** "Which gives you space to reorder and remake yourself": `riordinare`, reorder (glossary); `rifarsi`, remake oneself (D348), as Machiavelli's `rifarsi`, `rifarti` (Machiavelli .02, .04).

**`E tanto più facilmente aderirei a questa conclusione`.** "And so much the more readily I should adhere to this conclusion": the four conditions on the enemy all depend on `vedessi`, three as accusative and infinitive, the article of `lo Stato` bracketed as "[his]"; `facilmente`, readily (D27), the word of Guicciardini's `non concorro facilmente`; `aderire`, adhere, a conditional assent to his own conclusion, kept apart from `concorrere`, concur (D01).

**`Veggo che sempre e' Romani, quando potettono, prevennono le guerre a casa altri`.** "I see that the Romans always, when they could, forestalled the wars at others' home": `prevenire`, forestall, to be beforehand, carrying the war into the enemy's country; not to prevent it; Philip V of Macedon and Antiochus III, by the editor's identification.

**`Né mi muove quello che dice lo Scrittore`.** "Nor does what the Writer says move me": `lo Scrittore`, the Writer (glossary). The phrase is Machiavelli's own against Hannibal (`Né mi muove in questo caso l'autorità d'Annibale`, Machiavelli .06), turned on Machiavelli's counterfactual about the three routs.

**`sarebbono sanza dubio stati spacciati`.** "They would without doubt have been despatched": `spacciare`, despatch (D444), Machiavelli's verb (Machiavelli .05, .06); Guicciardini restates Machiavelli's indicative `erano spacciati` as a conditional.

**`perché si pone uno caso impossibile`.** "Because an impossible case is set": `porre`, set (D115). The impossibility is that a power routed abroad, above all far off, should have sent two new armies after the first rout; that Rome did so at home is the editor's reading of the three routs. `rimandare`, send again, not send back: the armies had not been there before.

**`massime in luogo lont[a]no`.** "Above all in a distant place": `massime`, above all (glossary). Canestrini prints `lontono`; emended from the 1933 edition (FG-CONS-II12-D003, D439).

**`E chi risolve bene il partito di fuggire la guerra in casa col portarla a casa di altri`.** "And whoever resolves well on the course of fleeing war at home by carrying it to the home of others": `partito`, course (D236); `fuggire`, flee (D325).

**`può così sperare di rompere lo inimico, come temere di essere rotto`.** "He can as well hope to rout the enemy as fear to be routed": `rompere`, rout, and `rotto`, routed (D431); `temere`, fear (glossary).

**`altrimenti la aspetta in casa`.** "Otherwise he awaits it at home": `altrimenti`, otherwise, the alternative to carrying the war abroad.

**`come dice Livio`.** "As Livy says": Guicciardini's citation; the passage is not identified here.

**`sarebbono forse più facilmente stati nel principio della guerra rotti da lui in Spagna o in Africa`.** "Would perhaps more readily have been, at the beginning of the war, routed by him in Spain or in Africa": `forse`, perhaps, limits the counter-claim; `in Spagna o in Africa` goes with `rotti`; the 1933 edition reads `forse molto più` (FG-CONS-II12-D004). The reply ends without a stop (D40).
