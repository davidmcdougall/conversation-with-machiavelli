# Discourses I.5

*Where the guard of liberty is more securely set, whether in the people or in
the great; and which have greater cause to make tumult, whoever wishes to
acquire or whoever wishes to maintain.*

## NM-DISC.1.5.01

Those who have prudently constituted a republic, among the most necessary
things ordered by them has been to constitute a guard for liberty; and
according as this is well placed, that free living lasts more or less. And
because in every republic there are great men and popular men, it has been
doubted in whose hands the said guard is better placed. And among the
Lacedaemonians, and in our times among the Venetians, it has been put in the
hands of the nobles; but among the Romans it was put in the hands of the
plebs. On that account it is necessary to examine which of these republics
made the better choice.

## NM-DISC.1.5.02

And if one went by the reasons, there is something to say on every side; but
if one examined their end, one would take the part of the nobles, for the
liberty of Sparta and of Venice having had a longer life than that of Rome.
And, coming to the reasons, I say, taking first the part of the Romans, that
one must put on guard over a thing those who have less appetite to usurp it.
And without doubt, if one considers the end of the nobles and of the ignoble,
one will see in those a great desire to dominate, and in these only a desire
not to be dominated, and consequently a greater will to live free, being less
able to hope to usurp it than the great are; so that, the commoners being
appointed to the guard of a liberty, it is reasonable that they should have more care
of it, and, not being able to occupy it themselves, should not permit others
to occupy it.

## NM-DISC.1.5.03

On the other hand, whoever defends the Spartan and Venetian order says that
those who put the guard in the hands of the powerful do two good works: the
one, that they satisfy more the ambition of those who, having more part in the
republic, through having this staff in hand have cause to be more content;
the other, that they take away a quality of authority from the restless
spirits of the plebs, which is the cause of infinite dissensions and scandals
in a republic, and apt to reduce the nobility to some desperation that in time
may produce bad effects. And they give as example that same Rome, which, through
the tribunes of the plebs having this authority in their hands, it was not
enough for them to have one plebeian consul, but they wished to have both. From
this they wished for the censorship, the praetor, and all the other degrees of command of the city; nor was this enough for them, but, led by the same fury, they
began afterwards in time to adore those men whom they saw apt to beat the
nobility; whence arose the power of Marius, and the ruin of Rome.

## NM-DISC.1.5.04

And truly, whoever discoursed well on the one thing and the other might stay in
doubt which should be chosen by him as guard of such liberty, not knowing which quality
of men is more harmful in a republic, either that which desires to acquire what
it has not, or that which desires to maintain the honour already acquired. And
in the end, whoever examines everything subtly will make of it this
conclusion: either you are reasoning of a republic that wishes to make an
empire, like Rome, or of one for which it is enough to maintain itself. In the first
case it is necessary for it to do everything like Rome; in the second it can
imitate Venice and Sparta, for those causes, and as will be said in the
following chapter.

## NM-DISC.1.5.05

But to return to discoursing which men are more harmful in a republic, either
those who desire to acquire or those who fear to lose what has been acquired,
I say that, Marcus Menennius having been made dictator, and Marcus Fulvius
master of the horse, both plebeians, to search out certain conspiracies that had
been made in Capua against Rome, authority was also given them by the people to
be able to search out who in Rome, through ambition and extraordinary ways, was
contriving to come to the consulate and to the other honours of the city. And,
it seeming to the nobility that such authority had been given to the dictator
against it, they spread it through Rome that it was not the nobles who sought
the honours through ambition and extraordinary ways, but the ignoble, who, not
trusting in their blood and their *virtù*, sought by extraordinary paths to come
to those degrees; and in particular they accused the dictator. And so powerful
was this accusation that Menennius, having made a harangue and complained of
the calumnies given him by the nobles, laid down the dictatorship and submitted
himself to the judgment that should be made of him by the people; and
afterwards, his cause argued, he was absolved of it, where it was much disputed
which is more ambitious, either he who wishes to maintain or he who wishes to
acquire;
for readily the one and the other appetite can be the cause of very great
tumults. Yet nonetheless most often they are caused by those who possess, for
the fear of losing begets in them the same longings that are in those who desire
to acquire; for it does not seem to men that they possess securely what a man
has unless he acquires something more anew. And moreover there is this, that,
possessing much, they can make alteration with greater power and greater
motion. And still more there is this, that their unruly and ambitious
comportment kindles in the breasts of those who do not possess a longing to
possess, either to avenge themselves against them by despoiling them, or to be
able themselves also to enter into that wealth, and into those honours that they see
ill used by the others.

## Notes

**The rubric.** Two questions, and the chapter answers them in order: where
the guard is better set (.01–.04), and which party is more harmful, those who
would acquire or those who would keep (.05). `Si ponga` is "is set";
Guicciardini quotes the verb, `il porre la guardia`, "the setting of the guard",
when he says he does not understand the question.

**`Quelli che ... hanno costituita ..., ... è stato costituire`.** The period
begins with its agents and changes construction: "Those who have prudently
constituted a republic, among the most necessary things ordered by them has
been to constitute a guard". The Italian does not repair it, and neither does
the English. `Costituire` is "constitute", both times, as in I.4.

**`guardia`.** "Guard": the charge of keeping liberty, and those who keep it.
I.4 ended on the tribunes as "guard of Roman liberty"; this chapter asks where
that guard belongs. `Mettere in guardia` is "put on guard over".

**`collocata`, `messa`, `si ponga`.** "Placed", "put", "set": three verbs,
three words.

**`quel vivere libero`.** "That free living": the infinitive as a noun, as in
I.2.

**`uomini grandi e popolari`.** "Great men and popular men": the two humours of
I.4, named by their men. Through the chapter they are also `Nobili` and
`Ignobili`, "nobles" and "the ignoble", and `Grandi` and `Popolani`, "the
great" and "the commoners"; the plebs (`Plebe`) is the Roman name.

**`Pertanto`.** "On that account" (D98).

**`elezione`.** "Choice", as throughout (I.1).

**`per aver avuta ... più lunga vita`.** "For the liberty of Sparta and of
Venice having had a longer life": the end, longevity, is the nobles' argument.

**`appetito`, `desiderio`, `volontà`, `voglie`.** "Appetite", "desire", "will",
"longings": four words, four words. The chapter's second question turns on
which appetite is more dangerous.

**`e' si debbe`.** "One must": `debbe`, as in I.4 (`ogni città debbe avere
i suoi modi`, "every city must have its ways"). `È necessario` is "it is
necessary".

**`usurparla`.** "To usurp it": the liberty, to make it one's own.

**`occupare`.** "Occupy", as in I.12: to seize and hold.

**`preposti a guardia d'una libertà`.** "Appointed to the guard of a
liberty": made its guard. The indefinite article is the Italian's.

**`l'ordine Spartano e Veneto`.** "The Spartan and Venetian order": `Veneto`,
the adjective, beside `Viniziani` for the Venetians themselves.

**`opere buone`.** "Good works": ordinary Italian for useful deeds; the
English keeps the phrase.

**`questo bastone`.** "This staff": the baton of command, the guard of
liberty as a token of authority.

**`una qualità di autorità`.** "A quality of authority": a kind and measure of
it. `Qualità` is "quality" through the chapter: "which quality of men" in .04.

**`scandali`.** "Scandals", in the older sense of public disturbance (D88).

**`che è cagione d'infinite dissensioni`.** "Which is the cause of infinite
dissensions": the authority, or the plebs holding it. The Italian does not
decide, and neither does the English.

**`cattivi effetti`.** "Bad effects" (D03).

**`la medesima Roma, che per avere ... non bastò loro`.** "That same Rome,
which, through the tribunes ... having this authority, it was not enough for
them": the relative `che` is left without a verb of its own, and the English
keeps the loose construction. `Per avere` is causal, as in `per avere questo
bastone`: the tribunes' authority is why the plebs wanted more.

**`che li vollono avere ambedue`.** "But they wished to have both": `non
bastò ... che` is "it was not enough ... but", twice in .03. `Volere` is
"wish" through the chapter. Two plebeian
consuls first held office together in 172 BC.

**`Da questa`.** "From this": either from the tribunes' authority or, in
sequence, from this point on. The 1554 edition's `da questo` favours the
second.

**`gradi dell'Imperio`.** "Degrees of command": the magistracies, the steps of
the Roman *imperium*, the power to command that consuls and praetors held
(D124).

**`adorare`.** "Adore": the word is Machiavelli's, and it is strong.
`Battere` is plain "beat".

**Marius.** Gaius Marius, seven times consul, raised to power by the plebs
against the nobility; his civil war with Sulla ended in Sulla's dictatorship.

**`fare uno Imperio`.** "Make an empire": to win dominion over others, as Rome
did. Guicciardini restates it as a republic "that acquires" (D124).

**`come nel seguente capitolo si dirà`.** I.6 asks whether Rome could have
been ordered without the enmity between people and senate.

**Marcus Menennius, Marcus Fulvius.** Machiavelli's names. In Livy (9.26,
314 BC) the dictator is Gaius Maenius and his master of the horse Marcus
Folius; the 1554 edition prints `Follio`. The names are kept as Machiavelli
gives them; the facing Italian shows the form.

**`Capova`.** Capua.

**`straordinarj`, `straordinarie`.** "Extraordinary": outside the ordinary
orders. `Modi` are "ways", `vie` "paths".

**`virtù`.** Left in Italian (D16): what the ignoble, on the nobles' charge,
could not trust in.

**`concione`.** "Harangue", as in I.4.

**`agitata la causa sua`.** "His cause argued": the case heard and debated.

**`ne farà questa conclusione`.** "Will make of it this conclusion": `fare`
is "make" or "do" through the chapter.

**`facilmente`.** "Readily", the glossary's word, kept for it: either appetite
readily causes tumults. The 1554 edition omits it, and `assai` before it.

**`Pur nondimeno`.** "Yet nonetheless": two adversatives, both kept.

**`di nuovo dell'altro`.** "Something more anew": a man does not feel secure in
what he has unless he adds to it.

**`maggior moto`.** "Greater motion": the means to set things moving. The 1554
edition has `maggiore mouimento`.

**`scorretti`.** "Unruly": uncorrected, ungoverned.

**`portamenti`.** "Comportment": their bearing and conduct.
