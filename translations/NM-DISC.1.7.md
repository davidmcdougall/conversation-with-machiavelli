# Discourses I.7

*How necessary in a republic accusations are for maintaining liberty.*

## NM-DISC.1.7.01

To those who in a city are appointed for the guard of its liberty, no more
useful and necessary authority can be given than is that of being able to
accuse citizens to the people, or to any magistracy or council, when they
should sin in any thing against the free state. This order makes two most
useful effects for a republic. The first is that the citizens, for fear of
being accused, do not attempt things against the state, and, attempting them,
are forthwith and without respect oppressed. The other is that a path is given
by which to vent those humours which grow in cities, in whatever way, against
whatever citizen. And when these humours have not [an outlet] by which to vent themselves
ordinarily, they have recourse to extraordinary ways, which make a republic
ruin altogether. And there is nothing that makes a republic so stable and
firm as ordering it in such a way that the alteration of these humours that
agitate it has a path to vent itself, ordered by the laws.

## NM-DISC.1.7.02

Which can be demonstrated by many examples, and above all by that which Livy
adduces of Coriolanus, where he says that, the Roman nobility being irritated
against the plebs, because it seemed to it that the plebs had too much
authority through the creation of the tribunes who defended it, and Rome
having, as happens, come into great scarcity of victuals, and the senate
having sent to Sicily for grain, Coriolanus, an enemy to the popular faction,
counselled that the time had come to be able to chastise the plebs, and take
from it that authority which it had acquired, and taken in prejudice of the
nobility, [by] keeping it famished and not distributing the corn to it; which
sentence having come to the ears of the people, it came into such indignation
against Coriolanus that at his coming out of the senate they would have killed
him tumultuously, if the tribunes had not summoned him to appear to defend his
cause.

## NM-DISC.1.7.03

Upon which accident is noted that which has been said above, how useful and
necessary it is that republics, with their laws, give the anger that the
universality conceives against one citizen [an outlet] by which to vent
itself; for when these
ordinary ways are not there, recourse is had to the extraordinary, and
without doubt these make much worse effects than those make. For if a citizen
is oppressed ordinarily, even though wrong should be done him, there follows
either little or no disorder in the republic; for the execution is done without
private forces and without foreign forces, which are those that ruin free
living; but is done with public forces and orders, which have their
particular bounds, nor do they transcend to a thing that ruins the republic.

## NM-DISC.1.7.04

And as to corroborating this opinion with examples, I wish that of the
ancients this one of Coriolanus may suffice me, upon which let each consider
how much ill would have resulted to the Roman republic if he had been killed
tumultuously; for there would have arisen from it offence from private men to private
men, which offence generates fear, fear seeks defence, for defence the partisans
are procured, from the partisans arise the parties in cities, and from the parties
the ruin of them. But the thing having been governed through whoever had
authority over it, all those ills came to be taken away that could have arisen
from it by governing it with private authority.

## NM-DISC.1.7.05

We have seen in our times what novelty it has made to the Republic of
Florence, the multitude's not being able to vent its spirit ordinarily against
one of its citizens, as happened in the time of Francesco Valori, who was as
prince of the city, who, being judged ambitious by many, and a man who should wish
with his audacity and animosity to transcend civil living, and there being no
path in the republic to be able to resist him except with a sect contrary to
his, it arose from it that he, not having fear except of extraordinary ways,
began to make supporters who should defend him; on the other hand those who
assailed him, not having an ordinary path to repress him, thought of the
extraordinary paths, so much so that it came to arms. And where, if he could
have been opposed by the ordinary, his authority would have been extinguished
with damage to him alone; having to be extinguished by the extraordinary, it
followed with damage not only to him but to many other noble citizens.

## NM-DISC.1.7.06

One could also allege, in fortification of the above-written conclusion, the
accident that followed, again in Florence, upon Pietro Soderini, which
followed altogether through there not being in that republic any way of
accusations against the ambition of powerful citizens; for to accuse a
powerful one to eight judges in a republic is not enough: it is needful that
the judges be many, for few always do in the way of the few. So that, if such
ways had been there, either the citizens would have accused him, [if] he was
living ill, and by such means, without making the Spanish army come, would
have vented their spirit; or, [if] he was not living ill, they would not have
had the daring to work against him, for fear of being accused themselves, and
thus that appetite which was the cause of scandal would have ceased on every
side. So that one can conclude this: that whenever one sees that external
forces are called in by a party of men who live in a city, one can
believe it arises from the bad orders of that [city], through there not being,
within that circle, an order able to vent without extraordinary ways the
malign humours that arise in men; which is provided for altogether by
ordering there accusations to the many judges, and giving reputation to them.

## NM-DISC.1.7.07

Which ways were so well ordered in Rome that in so many dissensions of the
plebs and the senate, never did either the senate or the plebs, or any
particular citizen, design to avail himself of external forces; for, having
the remedy at home, they were not necessitated to go outside for it. And
albeit the above-written examples are quite sufficient to prove it,
nonetheless I wish to adduce another, recited by Livy in his History, who
reports how, a sister of Arruns having been violated in Chiusi, in those times
a most noble city in Tuscany, by a Lucumo, and Arruns not being able to avenge
himself because of the power of the violator, he went off to find the French,
who then reigned in that place which today is called Lombardy, and urged them
to come with armed hand to Chiusi, showing them how with their [own] advantage
they could avenge him of the injury received: for if Arruns had seen that he
could avenge himself by the ways of the city, he would not have sought the
barbarian forces. But as these accusations are useful in a republic, so are
calumnies useless and damaging, as we shall discourse in the following
chapter.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Quanto siano necessarie` is "how necessary", a measure and
not a question: the chapter gives the measure. `L'accuse`, "accusations",
are the public charge brought before a court of many, set in I.8 against
`le calunnie`, the private slander.

**`A coloro che in una città son preposti per guardia della sua libertà`.**
"To those who in a city are appointed for the guard of its liberty": the
guard of I.5, now armed. The chapter is the handing-on I.6 promised: the
tribunes' authority to accuse "as will be discoursed in the following
chapter". Machiavelli does not name the tribunes in this opening sentence;
they enter with Coriolanus (.02).

**`quando che peccassero`.** "When they should sin": the subjunctive keeps it
a possibility.

**`Questo ordine`.** "This order" (glossary, `ordine`).

**`incontinente e senza rispetto`.** "Forthwith and without respect": at once,
and without regard to who they are (glossary, `rispetto`).

**`sfogare`, `sfogarsi`.** "Vent", "vent themselves", seven times, the
chapter's governing verb: the humours are a pressure that will find an outlet,
ordinary or extraordinary. `Onde sfogare` is "by which to vent":
`onde`, "whence", the outlet; where the Italian has no noun for it, the
English supplies "[an outlet]". At .03 the anger vents itself: `diano onde
sfogarsi all'ira`.

**`umori`.** "Humours", the word of I.4 and of Guicciardini's I.6; here the
ill will that gathers against a particular citizen.

**`ordinariamente`, `straordinarj`.** "Ordinarily", "extraordinary": by the
orders or outside them, as in I.5. `Per l'ordinario`, `per lo straordinario`
(.05) is "by the ordinary", "by the extraordinary".

**`modi` / `via`, `vie`.** "Ways" / "path", "paths" (D131), held throughout:
"a path is given by which to vent" (.01), "a path to vent itself".
`Ordinariamente` placed after the verb, "oppressed ordinarily" (.03), so that
it reads "by the orders" and not "usually".

**`ordinata dalle leggi`.** "Ordered by the laws": the `ordine` family held
through `ordinare`, `ordinarj`, `ordinariamente`, `ordinati`.

**`Coriolano`.** Gnaeus Marcius Coriolanus. Livy 2.34–35: in the famine of
491 BC he proposed in the senate to withhold the Sicilian grain until the
plebs gave up the tribunate; the tribunes summoned him to trial, and he went
into exile among the Volsci.

**`la Nobiltà romana`, `la Plebe`.** "The Roman nobility", "the plebs"
(glossary).

**`nimico alla fazione popolare`.** "An enemy to the popular faction":
`nimico`, Machiavelli's form of `nemico`.

**`gastigare`.** "Chastise".

**`in pregiudizio della Nobiltà presa`.** "Taken in prejudice of the
nobility": to its injury. The 1554 edition has no "acquired, and"
(NM-DISC-I7-D002).

**`la qual sentenza`.** "Which sentence": an opinion pronounced, as in
Guicciardini's I.5 (glossary).

**`lo arebbero tumultuariamente morto`.** "They would have killed him
tumultuously": `morire` transitive, "kill", as in .04; the plural verb after
the singular `Popolo` is Machiavelli's.

**`accidente`.** "Accident": an event, as in I.2 (glossary).

**`l'universalità`.** "The universality": the whole body of citizens.

**`forze forestiere`.** "Foreign forces": `forestiere` as adjective, kept
apart from the noun `forestieri`, "strangers" (D57).
`Forze esterne` (.06, .07) is "external forces" (D55).

**`termini`.** "Bounds", as in I.6.

**`saria resultato`, `ne nasceva`, `potevano nascere`.** "Would have
resulted", "there would have arisen from it", "could have arisen": the
imperfect in a contrary-to-fact sequence (D130). The chain that follows is
general, in the present, as in the Italian.

**`Francesco Valori`.** Leader of the Piagnoni, Savonarola's party, and
gonfaloniere in 1497; killed by his enemies in the riot of 8 April 1498, when
San Marco was stormed. "As prince of the city": first among its citizens,
without office to make him so.

**`Setta`.** "Sect": a faction.

**`fautori`.** "Supporters".

**`oppugnavano`.** "Assailed".

**`Pietro Soderini`.** Piero Soderini, gonfaloniere for life from 1502, under
whom Machiavelli served; the Spanish army that sacked Prato in August 1512
restored the Medici, and he fled. Machiavelli's form is kept. "Again in
Florence" is `pur in Firenze`.

**`otto giudici`.** "Eight judges": probably the Otto di Guardia, Florence's
magistracy of criminal justice, which heard offences against the state among
others.

**`bisogna`.** "It is needful" (D75).

**`Tantoché`.** "So that", twice: consecutive.

**`vivendo egli male`.** "[If] he was living ill": the gerund is conditional,
and the English supplies the "if"; its pair, `non vivendo male`, likewise.

**`quello cerchio ordine`.** "Within that circle, an order": both the 1824 and
the 1554 editions print `cerchio`, and the phrase is hard. The English takes
`cerchio` as the city's circuit, the bounds within which an order should
contain its humours, and keeps the noun.

**`agli assai giudici`.** "To the many judges": the article points back to
"it is needful that the judges be many". The 1554 edition has `a i molti
giudici`.

**`una parte d'uomini`.** "A party of men": `parte` as `le parti nelle
cittadi`, "the parties in cities" (.04), the step before ruin.

**`appetito`.** "Appetite" (D117); the 1554 edition has `ingordigia`,
greed.

**`disegnò`.** "Designed": planned.

**`rimedio`.** "Remedy" (glossary).

**`Chiusi`, `Lucumone`, `Arunte`.** Clusium, an Etruscan city; a Lucumo, an
Etruscan magnate (Livy's name for the seducer, the ward of Arruns); Arruns.
Livy 5.33: Arruns, by the tradition Livy reports, drew the Gauls to Clusium
to avenge his wife's seduction; Livy doubts only that these were the first
Gauls over the Alps. Machiavelli has them already settled in Lombardy, and
makes the wronged woman a sister.

**`i Francesi`.** "The French": Machiavelli's name for the Gauls, kept, as
`Lombardia` is "Lombardy" for Cisalpine Gaul.

**`armata mano`.** "Armed hand": in arms.

**`con loro utile`.** "With their own advantage": `utile` as noun.

**`cerco`.** "Sought": `cercato`, the short participle.

**`calunnie`.** "Calumnies": the subject of I.8. `Dannose` is "damaging"
(D106).

**`discorreremo`.** "We shall discourse": the chapter closes on the verb of
D01, as I.6 did.
