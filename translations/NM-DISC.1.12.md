# Discourses I.12

*Of how much importance it is to take account of religion, and how Italy, for
having been wanting in it through the Roman Church, is ruined.*

## NM-DISC.1.12.01

Those princes, or those republics, that wish to keep themselves uncorrupted have
above all else to keep uncorrupted the ceremonies of religion, and to hold them
always in their veneration. For no greater sign can be had of the ruin of a
province than to see the divine worship despised. This is easy to understand,
once one knows upon what is founded the religion in which the man is born. For
every religion has the foundation of its life upon some principal order of its
own. The life of the gentile religion was founded upon the responses of the
oracles and upon the sect of the diviners and the haruspices; all their other
ceremonies, sacrifices, rites, depended on these. For they readily believed that
the god who could foretell your future good, or your future ill, could also grant
it to you. From this arose the temples, from this the sacrifices, from this the
supplications, and every other ceremony in venerating them; for the oracle of
Delos, the temple of Jupiter Ammon, and other celebrated oracles kept the world
in admiration, and devout. When these afterwards began to speak in the manner of
the powerful, and this falsity was discovered among the peoples, men became
unbelieving, and apt to disturb every good order.

## NM-DISC.1.12.02

The princes, then, of a republic and of a kingdom ought to maintain the
foundations of the religion they hold; and this done, it will be an easy thing
for them to maintain their republic religious, and in consequence good and
united. And all the things that arise in favour of it, even though they should
judge them false, they ought to favour and augment; and so much the more ought
they to do it, the more prudent they are, and the more knowing in natural things.
And because this way has been observed by wise men, there has arisen from it the
opinion of miracles, which are celebrated in religions, even false ones; for the
prudent augment them, from whatever beginning they arise; and their authority
then gives those credit with anyone.

## NM-DISC.1.12.03

Of these miracles there were many at Rome, and among the others there was, that
while the Roman soldiers were sacking the city of the Veientes, some of them
entered the temple of Juno, and drawing near to her image and saying to it,
*vis venire Romam?* it seemed to some that they saw her nod, and to some others
that she said yes. For those men being filled with religion — which Livy shows,
because in entering the temple they entered without tumult, all devout and full
of reverence — it seemed to them that they heard the answer which they had
perhaps presupposed to their question; which opinion and credulity, by Camillus
and the other princes of the city, was wholly favoured and augmented.

## NM-DISC.1.12.04

Which religion, if it had been maintained among the princes of the Christian
republic according as it was ordained by its Giver, the Christian states and
republics would be more united and far more happy than they are. Nor can one make
any greater conjecture of its decline than to see how those peoples who are
nearer to the Roman Church, head of our religion, have less religion. And whoever
should consider its foundations, and see how far the present use differs from
them, would judge to be near, without doubt, either the ruin or the scourge.

## NM-DISC.1.12.05

And because there are some of the opinion that the well-being of Italian affairs
depends on the Church of Rome, I wish to discourse against it those reasons which
occur to me, and I shall allege two most powerful ones, which in my view have no
repugnance. The first is that, through the evil examples of that court, this
province has lost all devotion and all religion: which drags behind it infinite
inconveniences and infinite disorders; for just as where there is religion every
good is presupposed, so where it is lacking the contrary is presupposed. We
Italians have, then, this first debt to the Church and to the priests: that we
have become without religion and bad; but we have a greater one still, which is
the cause of our ruin. This is that the Church has kept and keeps this province
of ours divided.

## NM-DISC.1.12.06

And truly no province was ever united or happy unless it comes wholly under the
obedience of one republic or one prince, as has happened to France and to Spain.
And the cause that Italy is not in that same condition, nor has it too either a
republic or a prince to govern it, is only the Church; for having dwelt there and
held temporal rule, it has not been so powerful, nor of such *virtù*, as to have
been able to occupy the rest of Italy and make itself prince of it. And on the
other hand it has not been so weak that, for fear of losing the dominion of
temporal things, it could not summon a powerful one to defend it against whoever
in Italy had become too powerful; as has been seen anciently in many experiences,
when by means of Charlemagne it drove the Lombards out of it, who were already
almost kings of all Italy; and when in our times it took the power from the
Venetians with the help of France, afterwards drove the French out of it with the
help of the Swiss.

## NM-DISC.1.12.07

The Church, then, not having been powerful enough to occupy Italy, nor having
permitted another to occupy it, has been the cause that it has not been able to
come under one head, but has been under many princes and lords; from which has
arisen so much disunion and so much weakness that it has been brought to have
been prey, not only of powerful barbarians, but of whoever assails it. For which
we other Italians have debt with the Church, and with no other. And whoever
wished to see the truth of this more ready by certain experience would need to be
of such power as to send the Roman court, with the authority it has in Italy, to
dwell in the lands of the Swiss, who today are those peoples who live, both as to
religion and as to military orders, according to the ancients; and he would see
that in a short time more disorder would be made in that province by the wicked
customs of that court than by any other accident that could at any time arise
there.

## Notes

**.04, `hanno meno religione`.** The witness capitalises `Religione` throughout
this chapter — four times on the same page — and drops the capital at exactly
this phrase, where Machiavelli says that those nearest the Roman Church have
least religion. English cannot carry the distinction. Whether the compositor
meant it or not, the page prints it. (Source v0.2.1; the reading was recovered
by the dual cold pass and adjudicated on the page image, 2026-09-18.)

**.01, `l'oracolo di Delo`.** The governing 1824 witness reads *Delos*; the 1554
Giglio comparison witness reads *Delfo*, Delphi.

**.03, `vis venire Romam?`** — "Do you wish to come to Rome?" Left in Latin, as
the witness prints it, in italic.

**.06, `virtù`.** Left untranslated. Machiavelli sets it beside `potente` in the
same clause — the Church was neither powerful enough nor of such *virtù* — so it
cannot be simply power, and it is plainly not moral virtue.
