# Discourses I.58

*The multitude is wiser and more constant than a prince.*

## NM-DISC.1.58.01

That nothing is more vain and more inconstant than the multitude, both our Livy and all the other historians affirm. Because it often occurs, in narrating the proceedings of men, to see the multitude to have condemned someone to death, and afterwards to have wept for and supremely desired that same [man]; as one sees the Roman people to have done with Manlius Capitolinus, whom, having condemned to death, it supremely afterwards desired. And the words of the author are these: *Populum brevi, posteaquam ab eo periculum nullum erat, desiderium ejus tenuit.* And elsewhere, when he shows the accidents that arose in Syracuse after the death of Hieronymus, grandson of Hiero, he says: *Haec natura multitudinis est, aut humiliter servit, aut superbe dominatur.* I do not know whether I shall take upon myself a hard province, and one full of so much difficulty that it may be fitting for me either to abandon it with shame or to follow it with charge, wishing to defend a thing which, as I have said, is accused by all the writers. But however it may be, I do not judge, nor shall I ever judge, it to be a defect to defend some opinions with reasons, without wishing to use therein either authority or force.

## NM-DISC.1.58.02

I say, then, that of that defect of which the writers accuse the multitude men can be accused particularly, and above all princes; because each one who is not regulated by the laws would make those same errors as the unbound multitude. And this can be known readily, because there are and have been many princes, and of good and wise ones there have been few: I say of princes who have been able to break that rein that can correct them; among whom are not those kings who were born in Egypt when, in that most ancient antiquity, that province was governed with laws; nor those who were born in Sparta; nor those who in our times are born in France, which kingdom is moderated by the laws more than any other kingdom of which in our times one has notice. And these kings who are born under such constitutions are not to be put in that number from which one is to consider the nature of each man by himself, and to see whether he is like the multitude; because over against them one must set a multitude likewise regulated by the laws as they are, and there will be found in it that same goodness that we see to be in them, and it will be seen neither proudly to dominate nor humbly to serve; as the Roman people was, which, while the republic lasted uncorrupted, never served humbly, nor ever dominated proudly; on the contrary, with its orders and magistracies it held its degree honourably.

## NM-DISC.1.58.03

And when it was necessary to rise up against a powerful one, it did it, as one sees in Manlius, in the Ten, and in others who sought to oppress [the republic]; and when it was necessary to obey the dictators and the consuls for the public safety, it did it. And if the Roman people desired Manlius Capitolinus [when] dead, it is no marvel; because it desired his *virtù*, which had been such that the memory of them brought compassion to each one, and would have had the force to make that same effect in a prince; because it is the sentence of all the writers that *virtù* is praised and admired even in one's enemies: and if Manlius, amid so much desire, had risen again, the people of Rome would have given the same judgment of him as it did when, having drawn him out of prison, it a little afterwards condemned him to death; notwithstanding that one sees princes held wise who have had some person die and then supremely desired them, as Alexander [did] Clitus and other friends of his, and Herod Mariamne.

## NM-DISC.1.58.04

But what our historian says of the nature of the multitude he does not say of that which is regulated by the laws, as the Roman was, but of the unbound, as the Syracusan was, which made those errors that men make who are infuriated and unbound, as Alexander the Great and Herod did in the said cases. Therefore the nature of the multitude is not more to be inculpated than that of princes, because all err equally when all can err without respect. Of which, besides what I have said, there are many examples, both among the Roman emperors and among the other tyrants and princes, where one sees so much inconstancy and so much variation of life as was never found in any multitude.

## NM-DISC.1.58.05

I conclude, then, against the common opinion, which says that peoples, when they are princes, are various, mutable, ungrateful, affirming that these sins are not in them otherwise than they are in particular princes. And someone accusing peoples and princes together might say what is true; but taking the princes out of it, he deceives himself: because a people that commands and is well ordered will be stable, prudent and grateful not otherwise than a prince, or better than a prince, even one esteemed wise; and on the other hand a prince unbound from the laws will be ungrateful, various and imprudent more than a people. And [I affirm] that the variation of their proceeding arises not from a different nature, because in all it is of one way, and if there is advantage of good it is in the people, but from having more or less respect to the laws within which the one and the other lives.

## NM-DISC.1.58.06

And whoever will consider the Roman people will see it to have been for four hundred years an enemy of the royal name, and a lover of the glory and the common good of its fatherland; he will see so many examples used by it that testify to the one thing and the other. And if anyone should allege to me the ingratitude that it used against Scipio, I answer what was discoursed long above on this matter, where peoples were shown to be less ungrateful than princes.

## NM-DISC.1.58.07

But as to prudence and stability, I say that a people is more prudent, more stable, and of better judgment than a prince. And not without cause is the voice of a people likened to that of God; because one sees a universal opinion make marvellous effects in its prognostications, so that it seems that by an occult *virtù* it foresees its ill and its good. As to judging things, one sees very seldom, when it hears two haranguers who tend in different directions, when they are of equal *virtù*, that it does not take the better opinion, and that it is not able to grasp that truth which it hears. And if in hard things, or those that seem useful, as is said above, it errs, many times a prince too errs in his own passions, which are many more than those of peoples.

## NM-DISC.1.58.08

One sees it also, in its choices to magistracies, make far better choice than a prince; nor will it ever be persuaded to a people that it is good to draw to dignity a man infamous and of corrupted customs, which readily and by a thousand paths is persuaded to a prince: one sees a people begin to hold a thing in horror, and stay many centuries in that opinion; which is not seen in a prince. And for the one and the other of these two things I wish the Roman people to be enough for me as witness, which in so many hundreds of years, in so many choices of consuls and of tribunes, did not make four choices of which it had to repent. And it had, as I have said, the royal name so much in hatred that no debt to any citizen of its who attempted that name could make him flee the due penalties. One sees, besides this, the cities where peoples are princes make in very brief time excessive augmentations, and much greater than those that have always been under a prince; as Rome did after the expulsion of the kings, and Athens after it freed itself from Pisistratus. Which cannot arise from anything other than that the governments of peoples are better than those of princes.

## NM-DISC.1.58.09

Nor do I wish all that our historian says of it, in the above-alleged text and in any other, to be opposed to this opinion of mine; because if one will discourse all the disorders of peoples, all the disorders of princes, all the glories of peoples, all those of princes, the people will be seen to be by far superior in goodness and in glory. And if princes are superior to peoples in ordering laws, forming civil lives, ordering statutes and new orders, peoples are so much superior in maintaining the things ordered that they add without doubt to the glory of those who order them.

## NM-DISC.1.58.10

And in sum, to epilogize this matter, I say: as the states of princes have lasted much, the states of republics have lasted much, and the one and the other has had need to be regulated by the laws; because a prince who can do what he wishes is mad, a people that can do what it wishes is not wise. If, then, one will reason of a prince obliged to the laws, and of a people chained by them, more *virtù* will be seen in the people than in the prince; if one will reason of the one and the other unbound, fewer errors will be seen in the people than in the prince, and those lesser, and they will have greater remedies: because a licentious and tumultuary people can be spoken to by a good man, and can readily be brought into the good path; to a bad prince there is no one who can speak, nor is there other remedy than the steel. From which one can make conjecture of the importance of the illness of the one and of the other: for if to cure the illness of the people words are enough, and for that of the prince the steel is required, there will never be anyone who does not judge that where greater cure is required there are greater errors.

## NM-DISC.1.58.11

When a people is well unbound, one does not fear the madnesses that it does, nor has one fear of the present ill, but of that which can arise from it, since a tyrant can arise amid so much confusion. But with wicked princes the contrary befalls, that one fears the present ill, and in the future one hopes, men persuading themselves that his bad life can make a liberty rise. So that you see the difference of the one and of the other, which is as much as from the things that are to those that are to be. The cruelties of the multitude are against whoever they fear may occupy the common good; those of a prince are against whoever they fear may occupy his own good.

## NM-DISC.1.58.12

But the opinion against peoples arises because, of peoples, each one speaks ill without fear and freely, even while they reign; of princes one speaks always with a thousand fears and a thousand respects. Nor does it seem to me out of purpose, since this matter draws me to it, to dispute in the following chapter which confederations one can trust more, whether those made with a republic, or those made with a prince.

## Notes

**The rubric.** Machiavelli's rubric, two comparatives on one subject: `savia`, wise (`savio`, glossary, kept apart from `sagace`), and `costante`, constant, which the body opposes to `incostante`, inconstant, in its first line. `moltitudine`, multitude, and `Principe`, prince (glossary). Guicciardini's reply calls the rubric `il titulo dello Autore`, the Author's title (Guicciardini .03).

**`Nessuna cosa esser più vana e più incostante che la moltitudine, così Tito Livio nostro, come tutti gli altri Istorici affermano`.** "That nothing is more vain and more inconstant than the multitude, both our Livy and all the other historians affirm": the infinitive clause stands first as the object of `affermano`, and the English keeps it first, so that the authorities the chapter will contradict come last (D263, a landing); `così ... come`, both ... and. `Tito Livio`, Livy (names.md); `nostro`, our, Machiavelli's habit with Livy; `Istorici`, historians, as `Istoria`, history (glossary).

**`Perché spesso occorre nel narrare le azioni degli uomini`.** "Because it often occurs, in narrating the proceedings of men": `occorrere`, occur (D01), the *currere* family in the chapter's second sentence; `azioni` of narrated doings, "proceedings" (D358). `sommamente`, supremely (D190).

**`il quale avendo condannato a morte, sommamente dipoi desiderava`.** "Whom, having condemned to death, it supremely afterwards desired": the relative is the object of both verbs. `dipoi`, afterwards (D187). The 1554 prints `lo desideraua`, with the pronoun (NM-DISC-I58-D005). In the sentence before, `vedere la moltitudine avere condannato ... e quel medesimo dipoi pianto e sommamente desiderato` keeps the multitude as the subject of all three participles, and the English supplies "[man]" for `quel medesimo` (D53).

**`Populum brevi, posteaquam ab eo periculum nullum erat, desiderium ejus tenuit`.** Livy 6.20: "In a short time, once there was no danger from him, desire for him took hold of the people." The Latin Library text (thelatinlibrary.com/livy/liv.6.shtml, consulted 2026-11-12) reads `Populum brevi, postquam periculum ab eo nullum erat, per se ipsas recordantem virtutes desiderium eius tenuit`, with `postquam` and a different order, and with the people "recalling his virtues in themselves". The quotation as printed in the 1824, and in the 1554, lacks that phrase; Machiavelli's own explanation below, `e' desiderava le sue virtù` (.03), gives its sense in his words. Printed in italic in the 1824; the 1554 follows it with an Italian rendering, `Cioè, d'indi a poco ...`, which the 1824 does not print (NM-DISC-I58-D004).

**`gli accidenti che nacquero in Siracusa dopo la morte di Girolamo nipote di Jerone`.** "The accidents that arose in Syracuse after the death of Hieronymus, grandson of Hiero": `accidente`, accident (glossary); `nascere`, arise (D04). Hieronymus succeeded his grandfather Hiero II as king of Syracuse in 215 BC and was killed the next year; `nipote` is grandson here. The tumults after his death, in which the people killed the royal women, are Livy's 24.21-26.

**`Haec natura multitudinis est, aut humiliter servit, aut superbe dominatur`.** Livy 24.25: "This is the nature of the multitude: either it serves humbly, or it dominates proudly." The Latin Library text (thelatinlibrary.com/livy/liv.24.shtml, consulted 2026-11-12) reads `ea natura multitudinis est: aut servit humiliter aut superbe dominatur`, and goes on `libertatem, quae media est, nec struere modice nec habere sciunt`: liberty, which lies between, they know neither how to build with measure nor how to hold. Machiavelli quotes the two extremes and not the mean; he takes up the extremes in the same verbs, denied of Rome, `né superbamente dominare, né umilmente servire` (.02). The 1554 adds an Italian rendering here too (NM-DISC-I58-D004).

**`Io non so se io mi prenderò una provincia dura`.** "I do not know whether I shall take upon myself a hard province": `provincia`, province (glossary), in the sense of a charge or task undertaken, which the English cognate still carries. `convenire` impersonal, be fitting (D85); `carico`, charge (glossary), the blame that follows the task; `vergogna`, shame. Guicciardini answers with `Difficile impresa ... piglia, sanza dubio` (Guicciardini .01).

**`io non giudico, né giudicherò mai essere difetto difendere alcune opinioni con le ragioni, senza volervi usare o l'autorità o la forza`.** "I do not judge, nor shall I ever judge, it to be a defect to defend some opinions with reasons, without wishing to use therein either authority or force": `giudicare`, judge (D374); `difetto`, defect (glossary), which the next sentence turns on the accusers; `vi`, therein, in the defence.

**`Dico adunque come di quello difetto, di che accusano gli scrittori la moltitudine, se ne possono accusare gli uomini particolarmente, e massime i Principi`.** "I say, then, that of that defect of which the writers accuse the multitude men can be accused particularly, and above all princes": `adunque`, then (D74); `dico come`, "I say that", here and at .07, `come` introducing what is said (at .10 `Come ... hanno durato` is "as"); `accusare`, accuse (glossary); `particolarmente`, particularly, man by man against the multitude together (D374); `massime`, above all (glossary). The 1554 reads `tutti gli huomini`, all men (NM-DISC-I58-D005).

**`ciascuno che non sia regolato dalle leggi farebbe quelli medesimi errori, che la moltitudine sciolta`.** "Each one who is not regulated by the laws would make those same errors as the unbound multitude": the chapter's two participles, `regolato`, regulated, and `sciolto`, unbound, hold through both units, one English word each (glossary, D402; FG I.10 `autorità sciolta`); `incatenato`, chained (.10), is the strong form of the first, and `obbligato` (.10) is "obliged" (D401).

**`perché e' sono e sono stati assai Principi; e de' buoni, e de' savj ne sono stati pochi`.** "Because there are and have been many princes, and of good and wise ones there have been few": `e'`, the impersonal subject; `assai` of number, many (glossary). The 1554 reads `perche de cattiui sono, & sono stati assai Prencipi`, "of bad ones there are and have been many princes", which makes the antithesis explicit; the 1824 construes and is kept (NM-DISC-I58-D003, D394). `savj`, wise, as the rubric's `savia`.

**`quel freno che li può correggere`.** "That rein that can correct them": `freno`, rein (D389). `correggere`, correct.

**`intra i quali non sono quelli Re che nascevano in Egitto`.** "Among whom are not those kings who were born in Egypt": `intra`, among; `nascere` of persons, be born, the root of `nascere`, arise (D04), which English cannot keep in one verb. `il quale Regno è moderato più dalle leggi`: `moderare`, moderate (glossary); France's kingdom moderated by laws is NM I.16's `Regno di Francia` (names.md). `notizia`, notice (D373).

**`non sono da mettere in quel numero, donde si abbia a considerare la natura di ciascun uomo per sé`.** "Are not to be put in that number from which one is to consider the nature of each man by himself": `avere a` destinative, "be to" (D261); `considerare`, consider (glossary); `per sé`, by himself. `costituzioni`, constitutions, as `costituire`, constitute (glossary).

**`perché all'incontro loro si debbe porre una moltitudine medesimamente regolata dalle leggi`.** "Because over against them one must set a multitude likewise regulated by the laws": `all'incontro` of position, "over against", set opposite for comparison; D313's "in return" is scoped to requital, and D24's "on the other side" belongs to `al rincontro`, which Guicciardini uses to reverse this comparison, setting a prince against the Roman people (Guicciardini .03). (D401) `si debbe`, must (D274); `porre`, set (D115).

**`e vedrassi quella né superbamente dominare, né umilmente servire`.** "And it will be seen neither proudly to dominate nor humbly to serve": Livy's `superbe dominatur` and `humiliter servit` turned into Italian and denied. `dominare`, dominate (glossary). `anzi`, reversing after the negatives, "on the contrary" (D135); `grado`, degree (glossary); `Magistrati`, magistracies, the offices (D93).

**`E quando era necessario insurgere contro a un potente, lo faceva, come si vede in Manlio, ne' Dieci, ed in altri che cercarono opprimerla`.** "And when it was necessary to rise up against a powerful one, it did it, as one sees in Manlius, in the Ten, and in others who sought to oppress [the republic]": `la` is feminine, the republic of .02, not the people; the English names it in brackets (D53). `insurgere`, rise up, the root of `surgere`, rise (D358); `un potente`, a powerful one (D06). `i Dieci`, the Ten, the decemvirs of 451-449 BC (names.md, as NM I.40). `salute pubblica`, the public safety.

**`E se il Popolo romano desiderava Manlio Capitolino morto, non è maraviglia; perché e' desiderava le sue virtù`.** "And if the Roman people desired Manlius Capitolinus [when] dead, it is no marvel; because it desired his *virtù*": the people longed for him after his death, the case of .01; "desired him dead" would say the opposite, and the English supplies "[when]" (D53). *virtù* untranslated (D16), here plural, his qualities, `le quali erano state tali`; "which ... of them" keeps the plural in the pronouns. This is the sense Livy gives and the printed quotation lacks (.01 note).

**`perché l'è sentenza di tutti gli scrittori, come la virtù si lauda e si ammira ancora negli inimici suoi`.** "Because it is the sentence of all the writers that *virtù* is praised and admired even in one's enemies": `l'`, the expletive pronoun, "it"; the 1554 omits it (NM-DISC-I58-D005). `sentenza`, sentence (glossary); `suoi` refers to the impersonal subject of `si lauda`, the one who praises: "one's".

**`e se Manlio infra tanto desiderio fusse resuscitato`.** "And if Manlius, amid so much desire, had risen again": `resuscitato` with `essere`, intransitive, "risen again" rather than "resuscitated", now medical. `tratto che lo ebbe di prigione`: in Livy the senate releases Manlius by decree in the face of the plebs' agitation (6.17, `ex senatus consulto Manlius vinculis liberatur`, the Latin Library text, consulted 2026-11-12), and the people condemn him later (6.20); Machiavelli gives both acts to the people.

**`come Alessandro, Clito, ed altri suoi amici, ed Erode, Marianne`.** "As Alexander [did] Clitus and other friends of his, and Herod Mariamne": the verb is understood from `hanno fatto morire`, and the English supplies it once (D53). Alexander the Great killed Clitus in a drunken quarrel and mourned him; Herod the Great had his wife Mariamne put to death and grieved for her, as Josephus tells. `Marianne` is Machiavelli's form of the name (names.md, added with this pair).

**`Però non è più da incolpare la natura della moltitudine che de' Principi`.** "Therefore the nature of the multitude is not more to be inculpated than that of princes": `però`, therefore (D22). `incolpare`, inculpate, new here (D402), kept apart from `accusare`, accuse (glossary), and `biasimo`, blame (D173). `rispetto`, respect (glossary): without regard to anything that restrains them.

**`e tra gl'Imperatori romani, e tra gli altri Tiranni e Principi`.** "Both among the Roman emperors and among the other tyrants and princes": `Imperatori`, emperors, as `Imperadore` (glossary); `Tiranni`, tyrants (glossary). `incostanza`, inconstancy, answering `incostante` in the first line; `variazione di vita`, variation of life, as `varj` below.

**`Conchiudo adunque contro alla comune opinione`.** "I conclude, then, against the common opinion": the 1554 reads `oltre alla commune oppenione`, "beyond", or "besides", the common opinion, which would make the conclusion an addition rather than a contradiction; the 1824 `contro` construes, answers the `provincia dura` of .01, and is kept (NM-DISC-I58-D003, D394). `comune`, common.

**`i Popoli, quando sono Principi, sono varj, mutabili, ingrati`.** "Peoples, when they are princes, are various, mutable, ungrateful": `Principi` here of peoples that rule. `vario`, various, kept as the root of `variazione` and of Guicciardini's `varietà` (Guicciardini .02); the sense is changeable. `ingrato`, ungrateful (glossary). `affermando`, affirming, Machiavelli's own act, governed by `Conchiudo`; the next period, `E che la variazione ... nasce`, depends on `affermando` or on `Conchiudo`, and the English supplies "[I affirm]" (D53), the nearer verb; "[I conclude]" would serve the other reading. `peccati`, sins.

**`ma traendone i Principi, s'inganna`.** "But taking the princes out of it, he deceives himself": `ingannarsi`, deceive oneself (D374). `comanda`, commands; `stimato`, esteemed; `eziandio`, even; `dall'altra parte`, on the other hand (D24).

**`e se vi è vantaggio di bene è nel Popolo`.** "And if there is advantage of good it is in the people": a conditional aside, not the thesis. `vantaggio`, advantage, new here; it shares the English with `utile` as a noun (D283), a recorded collapse (D401). `procedere`, proceeding (glossary).

**`E chi considererà il Popolo romano`.** "And whoever will consider the Roman people": the future, kept; the 1554 reads `considera` (NM-DISC-I58-D003, D394). `nome Regio`, royal name; `patria`, fatherland (glossary); `usare` of examples, and of ingratitude below, "use" (D313).

**`rispondo quello che di sopra lungamente si discorse in questa materia; dove si mostrò i Popoli essere meno ingrati dei Principi`.** "I answer what was discoursed long above on this matter, where peoples were shown to be less ungrateful than princes": `discorrere`, discourse (D01); `lungamente`, long (D140). The place is I.29, where Scipio's case is weighed and the people judged less ungrateful.

**`E non senza cagione si assomiglia la voce d'un Popolo a quella di Dio`.** "And not without cause is the voice of a people likened to that of God": the proverb *vox populi vox Dei*. The fronted `non senza cagione` is kept: a plain English writer fronts a negative adverbial for emphasis (D263); Guicciardini answers it in nearly the same words, `non sanza cagione è assomigliata la moltitudine alle onde del mare` (Guicciardini .01). `assomigliare`, liken; `pronostichi`, prognostications; `talché`, so that (glossary); `occulta virtù`, an occult *virtù* (D16), a hidden capacity.

**`quando egli ode due concionanti che tendano in diverse parti`.** "When it hears two haranguers who tend in different directions": `concionanti`, those who make `concioni`, harangues (glossary); `diverso`, different (glossary). `rarissime volte`, very seldom (D97). `capace` of understanding a truth heard, "able to grasp" (D350, D373).

**`E se nelle cose gagliarde, o che pajano utili, come di sopra si dice, egli erra`.** "And if in hard things, or those that seem useful, as is said above, it errs": `gagliardo`, hard (D313), here bold, vigorous, not difficult; the place above is presumably I.53, on the people deceived by a false appearance of good. `pajano`, subjunctive; the 1554 reads `paiono`, at a letter the scan breaks, and the 1824 is kept (NM-DISC-I58-D002, D393).

**`Vedesi ancora nelle sue elezioni ai Magistrati fare di lunga migliore elezione che un Principe`.** "One sees it also, in its choices to magistracies, make far better choice than a prince": `elezione`, choice (glossary), the word repeated; `di lunga`, by far. `tirare alla dignità`, draw to dignity (D374); `infame`, infamous (glossary); `via`, path (D131). `si persuaderà ad un Popolo ... si persuade ad un Principe`, "will it be persuaded to a people ... is persuaded to a prince": the thing persuaded is the subject both times, as the Italian has it, so that the people and the prince stand in the same place; `corrotti`, corrupted (glossary).

**`E dell'una e dell'altra di queste due cose voglio mi basti per testimone il Popolo romano`.** "And for the one and the other of these two things I wish the Roman people to be enough for me as witness": `volere`, wish (D117); `bastare`, be enough (D205). `di che quello si avesse a pentire`, "of which it had to repent": `avere a` of necessity, "have to" (D261).

**`che nessuno obbligo di alcuno suo cittadino, che tentasse quel nome, potette fargli fuggire le debite pene`.** "That no debt to any citizen of its who attempted that name could make him flee the due penalties": `obbligo`, debt (D17), what the people owed a citizen for his services. The Italian says `alcuno`; Manlius, who had saved the Capitol, is the instance in this chapter (and I.24). `fuggire`, flee (D325); `pene`, penalties (glossary).

**`fare in brevissimo tempo augumenti eccessivi`.** "Make in very brief time excessive augmentations": `augumento`, augmentation (D69). `cacciata de' Re`, the expulsion of the kings; `Pisistrato`, Pisistratus (names.md), as I.2 and I.28: Pisistratus died in power, and Athens was freed from his son Hippias in 510 BC; Machiavelli names the house by its founder.

**`Né voglio che si opponga a questa mia opinione tutto quello che lo Istorico nostro ne dice nel preallegato testo`.** "Nor do I wish all that our historian says of it, in the above-alleged text and in any other, to be opposed to this opinion of mine": the order is normalised, as exposition (D263). `preallegato`, above-alleged, the two Livy passages of .01; `allegare`, allege, as `se alcuno mi allegasse` (.06).

**`perché se si discorreranno tutti i disordini de' Popoli`.** "Because if one will discourse all the disorders of peoples": `discorrere` transitive, discourse over, run through (D01), future as the Italian; Guicciardini uses the verb so, of the Author (Guicciardini .03).

**`i Popoli sono tanto superiori nel mantenere le cose ordinate, ch'egli aggiungono senza dubbio alla gloria di coloro che l'ordinano`.** "Peoples are so much superior in maintaining the things ordered that they add without doubt to the glory of those who order them": `mantenere`, maintain (glossary); `ordinare`, order (glossary), throughout the period. `egli`, they, the plural form; the 1554 `essi` (NM-DISC-I58-D005).

**`Ed in somma, per epilogare questa materia, dico`.** "And in sum, to epilogize this matter, I say": `epilogare`, epilogize, sum up in an epilogue. `Come hanno durato assai gli Stati dei Principi, hanno durato assai gli Stati delle Repubbliche`: `Come`, as; `assai` with a verb, "much" (D348); `Stato`, state (D265). The second `hanno` is printed with a damaged sort, read `hanne` by one reader; it is read `hanno`, as the 1554 has it (NM-DISC-I58-D001, D392). `pazzo`, mad; `savio`, wise, the rubric's word.

**`Se adunque si ragionerà d'un Principe obbligato alle leggi, e d'un Popolo incatenato da quelle`.** "If, then, one will reason of a prince obliged to the laws, and of a people chained by them": `obbligato` here is held by the laws, not indebted to them; D204's "indebted" would make the laws a creditor. The nearest precedent is D229, `essersi quelli Re obbligati ad infinite leggi`, "having bound themselves to infinite laws" (NM I.16), of the same kings of France; "bound" is kept here for Guicciardini's `legati` (Guicciardini .03), which he puts in Machiavelli's mouth. (D401) `incatenato`, chained.

**`ad un Popolo licenzioso e tumultuario, gli può da un uomo buono essere parlato`.** "A licentious and tumultuary people can be spoken to by a good man": the Italian is impersonal and passive, with `ad un Popolo` resumed by `gli`; the English keeps the people the subject of both passives, against the prince to whom `non è alcuno che possa parlare`. `licenzioso`, licentious, with `licenza`, licence; `tumultuario`, tumultuary (glossary); `ridurre`, bring (D314); `cattivo`, bad (D03); `ferro`, the steel, the sword.

**`Da che si può far coniettura della importanza della malattia dell'uno e dell'altro`.** "From which one can make conjecture of the importance of the illness of the one and of the other": `malattia`, illness, both times, which `curare`, `cura` and the steel continue as a physician's figure. The 1554 reads `malitia`, malice, both times, which would make it a judgment of character; the 1824 is kept (NM-DISC-I58-D003, D394). `coniettura`, conjecture.

**`a quella del Principe bisogna il ferro`.** "For that of the prince the steel is required": `bisogna`, it is required (D260); see the note on `bisogni` at Machiavelli I.2. Again in `dove bisogna maggior cura`, "where greater cure is required": `cura` is the treatment, keeping the figure.

**`Quando un Popolo è bene sciolto`.** "When a people is well unbound": `bene` intensifying, fully unbound. `pazzie`, madnesses, the noun of `pazzo` (.10); `paura`, fear (D313). `potendo nascere fra tanta confusione un Tiranno`: "since a tyrant can arise amid so much confusion".

**`Ma ne' Principi tristi interviene il contrario`.** "But with wicked princes the contrary befalls": `tristo`, wicked (D03); `intervenire`, befall (D298); `persuadersi`, persuade oneself (D358); `surgere`, rise (D358).

**`la quale è quanto dalle cose che sono a quelle che hanno ad essere`.** "Which is as much as from the things that are to those that are to be": `avere a` destinative, "be to" (D261).

**`Le crudeltà della moltitudine sono contro a chi ei temono che occupi il ben comune; quelle d'un Principe sono contro a chi ei temono che occupi il ben proprio`.** "The cruelties of the multitude are against whoever they fear may occupy the common good; those of a prince are against whoever they fear may occupy his own good": `ei temono` is plural both times, with the multitude and with the prince, and the English keeps "they" both times rather than mend the second. The 1554 reads `temano`, the subjunctive, both times, at a letter the scan breaks; the 1824 is kept (NM-DISC-I58-D002, D393). `occupare`, occupy (glossary).

**`Né mi par fuor di proposito, poiché questa materia mi vi tira, disputare nel seguente capitolo di quali confederazioni altri si possa più fidare`.** "Nor does it seem to me out of purpose, since this matter draws me to it, to dispute in the following chapter which confederations one can trust more": the next chapter is I.59, which Guicciardini does not answer. `confederazioni`, confederations; `altri`, one, impersonal.
