# Consideration on chapter VII of Book I

*How necessary in a republic accusations are for maintaining liberty*

## FG-CONS.1.7.01

It is most true that it is very useful, indeed almost necessary, that in a
city there be easy ways of oppressing, by the path of the laws and of the
judgments, the bad citizens, and especially those who should machinate
against the state, but it is needful also to take heed that they be ordered in such a
way that the innocent be not readily vexed or punished. For, besides being
unjust, it is also pernicious to the city, for, this danger hanging over the
men noble and of more quality, they, living with this continual
suspicion, become of necessity malcontent, and the ill content of the more
powerful becomes in many ways dangerous to the republic, and although one
citizen's being condemned wrongly is in itself of little importance, it
becomes important through the terror that it gives to the others, and also he
can be of a quality to do damage to the city, as is seen of Alcibiades, and
was about to be seen of Themistocles, driven unjustly from Athens, and Rome
felt it in Coriolanus.

## FG-CONS.1.7.02

It is necessary, then, to measure this part well, and, according to my
opinion, too dangerous to make judge of the accusations the people, which
neither understands nor examines things well, [and] is easy to move to
uproars and false calumnies. Nor does this authority sit well in few citizens
either, for, if they are chosen of a narrow number, they become too powerful,
if of a broad one, they proceed too respectful, and in fact the judges want
to be many, that is, more than fifty. And certainly the model of the Quarantia
of Florence would not have been ill considered, if many things that were disposed
ill had been moderated. And that it is not good to make the people judge
of the accusations, besides the reasons said above, one is to consider that
often the citizens who wish to make themselves great walking by the path of
the people, that is, proposing things that please the multitude, which,
considering the surface or the titles, not the end to which one tends, is led
into servitude before it perceives where it is being taken, in such a way that
it is impossible to oppress such men by the path of the people: for an
example there are the Gracchi, who, authors of seditious laws, and tending on
the road to taking away the authority from the senate, could not be oppressed
except against the will of the people, the like [with] Manlius Capitolinus, against
whom it was needful to create the dictator, for until the practice of making
himself king was discovered, the people followed him.

## FG-CONS.1.7.03

It is needful, then, that the republic be ordered in such a way, either that
the accusations have different judgments according as the orders and the
humours of the city are different, or that the men appointed to the judgments
be mixed in such a way that there be a tempering to be fitted to
every species of ills, taking heed that, with not restricting it to a small
number, they be men as choice as can be, and that they approach more to the
mean than to any of the extremes

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply,
with `le accuse` for the 1824 `l'accuse`, lower-case `repubblica`, and
without a full stop.

**The paragraphs.** Canestrini prints three, and they are kept.

**`[È] verissimo`.** "It is most true": assent, and the strongest so far. The
witness prints `E` without the accent; as printed the period has no verb, and
it is emended as his I.3 `[È] posto` was (D114, D144). The same at `[È]
adunque` (.02). The assent is to the utility of accusations; the reply is
about who is to judge them.

**`anzi quasi necessario`.** "Indeed almost necessary": `anzi` here corrects
upward after an affirmative, and neither reverses (D135, "on the contrary")
nor corrects a negative (D90, "but rather"). "Indeed" (D147).

**`modi facili`, `facilmente`, `è facile`.** "Easy ways", "readily", "is
easy": the family held as D27 rules it. The ways to oppress the bad are to be
easy, and the innocent not readily vexed: the reply turns on the two.

**`per via delle leggi e de' giudicii`.** "By the path of the laws and of the
judgments": `via` is "path" (D131), here and at `per via del populo` (.02),
where the path is the people's favour. `Giudicii` are judgments in the sense
of courts; `giudici` (.02) are the judges.

**`e' cattivi cittadini`.** "The bad citizens" (D03). The English keeps
them next to `quelli che machinassino`, "those who should machinate", as the
Italian does. `In spezie` is "especially", which keeps the root of `spezie`,
"species" (.03), and leaves "particular" to `particulare`.

**`machinassino`.** "Should machinate": plot.

**`avvertire`, `avvertendo`.** "Take heed", "taking heed": .01 and the last
period. The reply opens and closes on the same caution.

**`andando questo pericolo sopra`.** "This danger hanging over": bearing on
them.

**`uomini nobili e di più qualità`.** "Men noble and of more quality": one
group, two attributes. `Qualità` as in I.5 (glossary). The same word returns of the condemned man,
"of a quality to do damage".

**`malcontenti`, `la mala contentezza`.** "Malcontent", "the ill content":
one root, kept.

**`lo essere condannato uno cittadino a torto`.** "One citizen's being
condemned wrongly": the nominal infinitive (D109). It answers Machiavelli's
.03: if a citizen is oppressed ordinarily, "even though wrong should be done
him, there follows either little or no disorder". Guicciardini grants the
"little" (`di poca importanza`, "of little importance") and then makes it
important, through the terror and through the man.

**`e anche può essere lui`.** "And also he can be": `anche` adds a second
reason; it does not mean "he too".

**`fu per vedersi di Temistocle`.** "Was about to be seen of Themistocles":
`essere per` prospective (D45). Themistocles, ostracised, fled to the Persian
king and was given cities, and died at Magnesia before he was used against Greece, of illness or, as
some said, by poison (Thucydides 1.138); Alcibiades, condemned in his absence, went over to
Sparta and did Athens great harm.

**`lo sentì Roma in Coriolano`.** "Rome felt it in Coriolanus": Machiavelli's
own example, turned. For Machiavelli the tribunes' summons saved Rome from a
riot; for Guicciardini the condemnation made Coriolanus an enemy, who led the
Volsci against the city.

**`questa parte`.** "This part": this matter.

**`fare che delle accusazioni sia giudice il populo, il quale`.** "To make
judge of the accusations the people, which": the Italian order, so that
"which" takes the people and not the accusations.

**`muoversi a' rumori`.** "To move to uproars": `rumore` in the period sense,
public tumult, not hearsay. Canestrini prints `è facile` with no conjunction
before it; the 1933 edition reads `ed [è] facile`, with its own brackets. The
English supplies "[and]" (D53).

**`Non sta anche bene`.** "Nor does ... sit well ... either": `anche` with the
negative.

**`eletti`.** "Chosen" (glossary, `eleggere`).

**`procedono troppo respettivi`.** "Proceed too respectful": with too much
regard to persons, afraid to condemn the powerful. `Rispetto` is "respect"
(glossary), as in Machiavelli's "without respect" (.01).

**`e in fatto`.** Canestrini's letter prints o-shaped, as his e does
throughout this page; read `e` (D145). The 1933 edition prints `ed`.

**`vogliono essere assai`.** "Want to be many": `volere` of a requirement,
not a wish, the one exception to D117's "wish" (D148); `vogliono farsi
grandi`, "wish to make themselves great", in the same paragraph, is a wish. Machiavelli says the same of Soderini's eight: "it is needful that
the judges be many" (.06). Guicciardini puts a number on it.

**`la Quarantia`.** The Forty, a Florentine court for crimes against the
state, modelled on the Venetian Quarantie; Soderini tried to introduce it,
and the popular government restored in 1527 re-established it. So
Canestrini's note, which is his and is not carried. He prints `Quarantía`
with an accent to mark the stress; the accent is normalised away (DEC-009
clause 1).

**`non era male considerato, se si fussino moderate`.** "Would not have been
ill considered, if ... had been moderated": the imperfect in a
contrary-to-fact condition (D47). `Male disposte` is "disposed ill": badly
arranged, not hostile.

**`s'ha a considerare`.** "One is to consider" (D61).

**The anacoluthon.** `che spesso e' cittadini che vogliono farsi grandi ...`
never gets its verb: the sentence turns to the multitude and then to `in modo
che`. The English leaves it unfinished, as he does.

**`la superficie o i titoli`.** "The surface or the titles": as printed. The
1933 edition reads `ed e' titoli`, "and the titles" (D146).

**`è prima condotta alla servitù che si accorga dove sia menata`.** "Is led
into servitude before it perceives where it is being taken".

**`a cammino di tòrre`.** "On the road to taking away": `tòrre` is `togliere`,
"take away" (D131).

**`e' Gracchi`.** Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, tribunes in 133 and 123–122
BC, whose agrarian and other laws the senate opposed; both were put down by
force, not condemned by the people: hence `contro alla volontà del
populo`.

**`il simile Manlio Capitolino`.** "The like [with] Manlius Capitolinus":
`il simile` adverbial, as with the Gracchi. Marcus Manlius, saviour of the Capitol, was charged with aiming at
kingship; a dictator imprisoned him in 385 BC; in 384, accused by tribunes,
he was condemned by the people and thrown from the Tarpeian rock (Livy
6.11–20). Once the design was out, the people did condemn him, as `insino`
allows. Guicciardini named
him in his reply to I.5.

**`la pratica`.** "The practice": the scheme.

**`temperamento`.** "A tempering", as in his reply to I.3.

**`appropriarsi`.** "To be fitted": "appropriated" now means seized.

**`mediocrità`.** "The mean": the middle, whether of condition or of
disposition. Not "mediocrity", which in English now disparages.

**`col non lo ristrignere`.** "With not restricting it": `col` with the
infinitive, which leaves the relation open, as the Italian does.

**No full stop.** Canestrini prints none after `estremi`; the 1933 edition
does. The English ends as the Italian does (D40).
