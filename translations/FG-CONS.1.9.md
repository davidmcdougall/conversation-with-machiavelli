# Consideration on chapter IX of Book I

*How it is necessary to be alone in wishing to order a republic anew, or to reform it wholly outside its ancient orders*

## FG-CONS.1.9.01

There is no doubt that one alone can set better order to things than many do,
and that one in a disordered city merits praise if, not being able to reorder
it otherwise, he does it with violence and with fraud, and extraordinary
ways. But it is to be prayed to God that republics have no necessity of being
mended by a like path, for besides that the spirits of men are fallacious,
and one can under this honest colour occupy the tyranny, there is also danger
that the will, good from the beginning, become bad, for whoever does this
cannot at once, the laws constituted, lay down the authority, for, being
introduced by violence, they would forthwith be annihilated, and therefore it
is needful he continue so long in the authority that the progress of time and
experience establish them, and in this space it can happen that the
sweetness of power and the licence of the principate make him change into
ill the intention that from the beginning had been good. This is, then, a way
of medicine desirable when there is no other hope of health, but dangerous
and of ill example, and he is supremely laudable who does not retain this
authority in himself except so far as is necessary to establish the things
ordered, as Lycurgus did, and if any other can be adduced for an example. But
whoever retains the power while he lives, although he governs rightly, and
leaves after him a good form of government, I do not know how far he is to be
praised, for it cannot be interpreted except that he is moved by his own
ambition, and although what he has done is useful to the city, and he is not
detestable, as is he who uses ill the authority occupied, yet also he is
not free of all reproof. As to what the Discourse says, that Romulus looked to
the common good and not to his own ambition, having constituted a senate, I
say nothing else now, for it is needful first to read well and consider the
life of Romulus, who, if I remember well, it was suspected had been killed by
the senate, for arrogating to himself too much authority: it is needful to
consider it well

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply,
with lower-case `repubblica`, `esser` for `essere` and `delli` for `degli`,
and without a full stop.

**The paragraph.** Canestrini prints one, and it is kept.

**`Non è dubio che uno solo`.** "There is no doubt that one alone": his
first words are Machiavelli's thesis granted, in Machiavelli's `un solo`
(.02). Unqualified assent, as `[È] verissimo` in his I.7.

**`porre migliore ordine alle cose`.** "Set better order to things": `porre`,
"set" (D115).

**`merita laude`.** "Merits praise" (glossary, `laude`). `Merita` answers
Machiavelli's last line: Romulus `meritare scusa, e non biasimo`, "merits
excuse, and not blame". Guicciardini goes further than excuse, and then
qualifies it.

**`con la violenza e con la fraude, e modi estraordinarii`.** "With violence
and with fraud, and extraordinary ways": Machiavelli's `violento` and
`azione straordinaria` (.02), with fraud added, which Machiavelli does not
name. The 1933 edition reads `la violenzia o con la fraude`, violence or
fraud (FG-CONS-I9-D002); both readers read Canestrini's `e`. Canestrini
prints a mark after `fraude` that neither reader could weigh; it is kept as a
comma (D110), and the English keeps the afterthought.

**`è da pregare Dio`.** "It is to be prayed to God".

**`necessità di essere racconcie per simile via`.** "Necessity of being
mended by a like path": `racconciare` is Machiavelli's verb (`per
racconciare`, "to mend", .02). `Via` "path" (D131).

**`fallaci`.** "Fallacious": deceptive, not to be relied on.

**`sotto questo onesto colore`.** "Under this honest colour": a pretext of
honour. `Onesto`, the older sense, honourable.

**`occupare la tirannide`.** "Occupy the tyranny": Machiavelli's phrase for
what the ephors feared of Agis (.04).

**`periculo`.** "Danger" (glossary).

**`la voluntà da principio buona non diventi cattiva`.** "The will, good at
the beginning, become bad": `voluntà` "will" (glossary), `cattiva` "bad"
(D03). `Non` after `periculo che`, an expression of fearing, not a
negative. `Da principio`, "from the beginning", as Machiavelli's `da
principio ordinato bene` (.02); again at the end of the period.

**`constituite le leggi`.** "The laws constituted": an absolute participle;
the verb is Machiavelli's `costituire` (.02).

**`deporre la autorità`.** "Lay down the authority": Machiavelli forbade
bequeathing the authority and wanted the thing ordered left in the care of
many (.02); Guicciardini praises the founder who lays it down, and says he
cannot do so at once. The rest of the reply turns on the difference.

**`incontinenti annichilate`.** "Forthwith annihilated": `incontinenti`,
immediately, kept apart from `subito`, "at once".

**`e però bisogna continui`.** "And therefore it is needful he continue"
(D22, D75).

**`il progresso del tempo e la esperienza le stabilisca`.** "The progress of
time and experience establish them": two subjects, a singular verb, and the
English cannot show it.

**`la dolcezza della potenza e la licenza del principato`.** "The sweetness of
power and the licence of the principate" (glossary, `licenza`; D76,
`Principato`). Guicciardini names what holding the founder's authority
does to the man who holds it.

**`mutare in mala la intenzione`.** "Change into ill the intention": `mala`,
"ill", as `mala opinione` (D165).

**`[È] adunque questo uno modo di medicina`.** "This is, then, a way of
medicine": Canestrini prints `E` without the accent, and as printed the
period has no finite verb; emended as his I.3, I.7 and I.8 were (D114, D144,
D157, D167). The 1933 edition prints `È adunche` (FG-CONS-I9-D001).
`Adunque` "then" (D74); `modo` "way". The founder's violence becomes a
medicine; his reply to I.4 had the figure already, the tribunes a good
remedy (`remedio`) for a sickness that need not have been.

**`speranza di salute`.** "Hope of health": the medical figure is held.
Machiavelli's `salute di Roma` (I.8) was safety.

**`di malo esemplo`.** "Of ill example": answering Machiavelli's `di cattivo
esempio`, "of bad example" (.01), with `malo` for `cattivo` (D165).

**`laudabile sommamente`.** "Supremely laudable".

**`non ritiene in sé questa autorità se non quanto`.** "Does not retain this
authority in himself except so far as".

**`come fece Licurgo`.** "As Lycurgus did": who, in Plutarch, gave Sparta his
laws, bound the citizens by oath to keep them until his return, and left the
city for good. Machiavelli named Lycurgus among the founders who took an
authority to themselves (.04); Guicciardini makes him the model of laying it
down.

**`e se alcuno altro si può addurre in esemplo`.** "And if any other can be
adduced for an example": elliptical, as the Italian, and doubtful that there
is one. Machiavelli could give infinite examples, Lycurgus among them (.04);
Guicciardini is not sure of one besides Lycurgus.

**`se bene governa rettamente`.** "Although he governs rightly": `se bene`,
"although" (D31).

**`forma buona di reggimento`.** "A good form of government" (D64).

**`non so quanto sia da essere laudato`.** "I do not know how far he is to be
praised": suspension (glossary, `non so`), not modesty. The question is how
much, and he leaves it open.

**`interpretare`.** "Interpreted".

**`mosso per ambizione propria`.** "Moved by his own ambition": Machiavelli's
phrase turned, `non per ambizione propria` (.03).

**`detestabile, come chi usa male`.** "Detestable, as is he who uses ill":
the comparison is with the man who is detestable, and "like him" after the
negative would have reversed it. `Detestabile` is the word of his I.8.

**`la autorità occupata`.** "The authority occupied" (glossary, `occupare`).

**`pure non manca anche lui di ogni reprensione`.** "Yet also he is not
free of all reproof": `ogni` after a negative, any. `Pure`, "yet"; `anche
lui`, "also he" (D154). `Reprensione` is the noun of Machiavelli's
`riprendere`, "reprove" (.02): the orderer whom no wise mind, says
Machiavelli, will reprove for his extraordinary action gets some reproof,
for keeping the power.

**`il Discorso`.** "The Discourse": Machiavelli's chapter.

**`che Romulo spettò al bene comune`.** "That Romulus looked to the common
good": `spettare`, from Latin *spectare*, to look towards. Guicciardini's
`Romulo` is the Latin form; the 1933 edition prints `Romolo`
(FG-CONS-I9-D004). Machiavelli's `fusse per il bene comune, e non per
ambizione propria` (.03), restated.

**`avendo constituito uno senato`.** "Having constituted a senate":
Machiavelli's `ordinato un Senato` in another verb.

**`non dico ora altro`.** "I say nothing else now": he defers, and will not
take Machiavelli's proof until the life has been considered.

**`bisogna prima bene leggere e considerare la vita di Romulo`.** "It is
needful first to read well and consider the life of Romulus": Machiavelli
wrote "whoever considers well the authority that Romulus reserved to himself"
(.03); Guicciardini asks for the life to be considered, not the authority.
`Leggere`: the life is something to be read, perhaps Plutarch's *Life* of
Romulus, which carries the tradition he recalls.

**`se bene mi ricordo`.** "If I remember well": here `se bene` is "if well",
not the concessive of a few lines before.

**`si dubitò non fussi ammazzato dal senato`.** "It was suspected had been
killed by the senate": `dubitare` with expletive `non`, to suspect, fear.
Livy (1.16) tells that Romulus vanished in a storm while reviewing the army,
that the senators said he had been taken up to the gods, and that a few
whispered that the senators had torn him to pieces. The senate Machiavelli
offers as proof of Romulus' good intent is, in this memory, suspected of
killing him.

**`per arrogarsi troppa autorità`.** "For arrogating to himself too much
authority". Livy gives no reason; Plutarch (*Romulus* 26–27) and Dionysius
of Halicarnassus (2.56) make it his growing absolutism.

**`bisogna considerarla bene`.** "It is needful to consider it well": `la` is
the life of Romulus. The colon before it is supplied from the 1933 edition
(C005).

**No full stop.** Canestrini prints none after `bene`; the 1933 edition does.
The English ends as the Italian does (D40).
