# Discourses I.1

*What have universally been the beginnings of any city whatever, and what was
that of Rome.*

## NM-DISC.1.1.01

Those who read what the beginning of the city of Rome was, and by what
legislators, and how ordered, will not marvel that so much *virtù* was
maintained for several centuries in that city; and that there then arose from it
that rule to which that republic attained.

## NM-DISC.1.1.02

And wishing first to discourse of its birth, I say: that all cities are built
either by men native to the place where they are built, or by strangers.

## NM-DISC.1.1.03

The first case occurs when it does not seem to the inhabitants, dispersed in
many and small parts, that they live secure, each part being unable by itself,
both through its site and through its small number, to resist the onrush of
whoever should assault them, and, the enemy coming, they are not in time to
unite for their defence; or if they were, they would have to leave many of their
retreats abandoned, and so would come to be the sudden prey of their enemies; so
that, to flee these dangers, moved either by themselves or by someone among them
of greater authority, they draw together to live together in a place
chosen by them, more convenient to live in and easier to defend. Of these, among
many others, have been Athens and Venice. The first, under the authority of
Theseus, was built for similar causes by the dispersed inhabitants. The other,
many peoples having withdrawn into certain little islands that were at the head
of the Adriatic sea, to flee those wars which every day, through the coming of
new barbarians after the decline of the Roman rule, arose in Italy, began among
themselves, without any other particular prince to order them, to live under
those laws that seemed to them most apt to maintain them. Which succeeded for
them happily through the long idleness that the site gave them, that sea having
no outlet, and those peoples who afflicted Italy not having ships able to infest
them; so that any small beginning could bring them to that greatness in which
they are.

## NM-DISC.1.1.04

The second case, when a city is built by foreign peoples, arises from men either
free or dependent on others, as are the colonies sent either by a republic or by
a prince to relieve their lands of inhabitants, or for the defence of that
country which, newly acquired, they wish to maintain securely and without
expense; of which cities the Roman people built many, and throughout its rule:
or else they are built by a prince, not to live in, but for his glory, as the
city of Alexandria by Alexander. And through these cities not having their origin
free, it rarely occurs that they make great progress and can be numbered among
the heads of kingdoms. Similar to these was the building of Florence, for,
whether built by the soldiers of Sulla or by chance by the inhabitants of the
mountains of Fiesole (who, trusting in that long peace which arose in the world
under Octavian, withdrew to live in the plain upon the Arno), it was built under
the Roman rule, nor could it in its beginnings make other augmentations than
those that were granted it by the courtesy of the prince.

## NM-DISC.1.1.05

The builders of cities are free when some peoples, either under a prince or by
themselves, are constrained, either by disease, or by famine, or by war, to abandon
their native country and seek themselves a new seat; these, either they inhabit
the cities that they find in the countries they acquire, as Moses did, or they
build new ones, as Aeneas did. In this case is where the *virtù* of the builder
and the fortune of the thing built are known; which is more or less marvellous
according as he who has been its beginning is more or less virtuous. The *virtù*
of whom is known in two ways; the first is in the choice of the site, the other
in the ordering of the laws.

## NM-DISC.1.1.06

And because men act either by necessity or by choice, and because one sees
greater *virtù* to be there where choice has less authority, it is to be
considered whether it would be better to choose sterile places for the building
of cities, so that men, constrained to be industrious, less occupied by
idleness, might live more united, having through the poverty of the site less
cause of discords; as happened in Ragusa, and in many other cities built in
similar places; which choice would without doubt be wiser and more useful if men
were content to live on what is theirs, and did not wish to seek to command others.
Therefore, men not being able to secure themselves except with power, it is
necessary to flee this sterility of country, and to place oneself in most
fertile places, where, being able through the abundance of the site to expand,
they can both defend themselves from whoever should assault them, and oppress
whoever should oppose their greatness. And as to that idleness which the site
might bring them, one must order that the laws constrain them to those necessities to which the site would not constrain them; and imitate those who
have been wise, and have lived in most pleasant and most fertile countries, and
apt to produce idle men, and unfit for every virtuous exercise; who, to obviate
those harms which the pleasantness of the country would have caused through
idleness, have imposed a necessity of exercise on those who were to be soldiers;
in such a way that through such an order better soldiers have come to be there than in those countries which by nature have been harsh and sterile; among whom was the kingdom of the Egyptians, where, notwithstanding that the country is most
pleasant, that necessity ordered by the laws could do so much that most
excellent men arose there; and if their names were not extinguished by
antiquity, one would see how they would merit more praise than Alexander the
Great and many others of whom the memory is still fresh. And whoever had
considered the kingdom of the Sultan, and the order of the Mamluks and of that soldiery of theirs, before it was extinguished by Selim, the Grand Turk, would
have seen in it many exercises for the soldiers, and would have known in fact
how much they feared that idleness to which the benignity of the country could
lead them, had they not obviated it with most strong laws.

## NM-DISC.1.1.07

I say then that it is a more prudent choice to place oneself in a fertile
place, when that fertility is restricted with the laws within due bounds. To
Alexander the Great, wishing to build a city for his glory, came Dinocrates the
architect, and showed him how he could make it upon Mount Athos, which place,
besides being strong, could be brought into such a shape that that city would be given a human form, which would be a thing marvellous and rare, and worthy of
his greatness; and Alexander asking him what those inhabitants would live on, he
answered that he had not thought of it; at which [Alexander] laughed, and, leaving
that mountain alone, built Alexandria, where the inhabitants were to stay willingly for the fatness of the country and for the convenience of the sea and of
the Nile.

## NM-DISC.1.1.08

Whoever then examines the building of Rome, if Aeneas is taken for its first
progenitor, it will be of those cities built by strangers; if Romulus, of those
built by men native to the place; and in whatever way, he will see it to have a
free beginning, without depending on anyone; he will see also (as will be said
below) to how many necessities the laws made by Romulus, by Numa and by the
others constrained it; so that the fertility of the site, the convenience of the sea, the frequent victories, the greatness of its rule, could not for many
centuries corrupt it, and they maintained it full of as many *virtù* as ever
any other republic was adorned with. And because the things done by it, and
which are celebrated by Livy, came about either by public or by private counsel,
either inside or outside the city, I will begin to discourse upon those things
that occurred inside and by public counsel which I shall judge worthy of greater
note, adding to them all that might depend on them; with which Discourses this first
book, or this first part, will be concluded.

## Notes

**`discorrere`, `occorrere`.** The book's first chapter uses both verbs of its
method: Machiavelli will "discourse" of Rome's birth, the first case "occurs",
it "rarely occurs" that dependent cities grow, and he will discourse on "those
things that occurred". The English keeps the words, because Guicciardini's reply takes up the first and adds a third, `concorrere`.

**`virtù` and `virtuoso`.** *Virtù* is left untranslated: the capacity of a
founder or a people, not moral goodness. Its adjective, `virtuoso`, has to be
"virtuous" in English, and means the same: the builder who has more or less
*virtù*, the "virtuous exercise" that idleness unfits men for.

**`imperio`.** "Rule", here as elsewhere in this edition: `quello Imperio`,
the command over others that Rome's republic came to, and its rule over its
colonies. At .03 and .04 `lo Imperio romano` is what English readers call the
Roman Empire; the English keeps "rule" so that one Italian word stays one
English word across the book.

**`capi de' Regni`.** "Heads of kingdoms": chief cities, capitals.

**`forestieri`.** "Strangers", as the builders of a city who come from
elsewhere; "foreign" for the adjective (`genti forestiere`).

**`Raugia`**, Ragusa, now Dubrovnik. **`Salì, Gran Turco`**: Selim I, Ottoman
sultan, who destroyed the Mamluk sultanate of Egypt in 1516–17.

**The sources.** Dinocrates and Mount Athos are in Vitruvius, *De
architectura* 2, preface. Florence had two founding stories, Sulla's veterans or the people of
Fiesole coming down to the plain, and Machiavelli declines to choose between
them; either way the city was not born free.
