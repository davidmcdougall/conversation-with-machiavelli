# Consideration on chapter IV of Book I

*That the disunion of the plebs and of the Roman senate made that republic free
and powerful*

## FG-CONS.1.4.01

I have at another time written more largely, therefore I shall now pass
through it with brevity; but I say in conclusion that the cause of the
disunions of Rome between patricians and plebeians was from the orders of the
city being divided, that is, that one part were patricians, the other plebeians,
and that all the magistracies were the patricians', the plebs excluded, and
every hope taken from the plebeians of being able to attain them. For if from
the beginning either this distinction between patricians and plebeians had not
been, or if at least half of the honours had been given to the plebs, as was
done afterwards, those divisions would not have arisen, which cannot be
laudable, nor can it be denied that they were damaging, although perhaps in
some other republic less virtuous they would have done more harm; the plebs
would not have desired the creation of the tribunes, nor would that magistracy
have been necessary: for, the honours communicated, the power was
communicated, nor would liberty have run more danger from the patricians than
from the plebeians. And it is certain that, once the honours had been
communicated, that magistracy was perhaps of more damage than of use, and at
least in the latter times was instrument and colour to whoever wished to disturb
the republic, and above all one cannot, in my judgment, praise in them either
the authority of proposing new laws or of interceding.

## FG-CONS.1.4.02

It was not, then, the disunion between the plebs and the senate that made Rome
free and powerful, for it would have been better if the causes of the disunion
had not been there, nor were these seditions useful, but indeed less damaging
than they have been in many other cities, and it was very useful to its
greatness that the patricians should rather yield to the will of the plebs than
enter into thinking of a way of not having need of the plebs, but to praise the
disunions is like praising in a sick man the sickness, for the goodness of
the remedy that has been applied to him. This disorder was from the origin of
Rome, for in its beginning there was the distinction between the patricians
and the plebeians; but under the kings it did no harm, for, the authority being
in the kings, the senate could not by itself oppress the plebs; and what the
senate did not do, of thinking of the conveniences, the kings did, even at
times more ambitiously than was due, as one reads of Servius Tullius, and
they were accustomed also to choose at times some of the plebeians into the
patricians, which made the others tolerate more readily that degree which they
yet hoped to be able to reach. All which reasons ceased when the kings were
driven out, for the patricians became masters of the city and arbiters of
everything, the plebs had no one to flee to, nor anyone who should think of
its conveniences, nor the heads of the plebs any more hope of being chosen
into the patricians, for by them they were disdained as ignoble, and strangers
were rather chosen, as was Appius Claudius. Nor was this disorder noticed in
driving out the kings, men thinking more of the present ill, which was that of
the kings, and because whoever has not great skill in public things does not
know them except by experience; therefore seldom, or perhaps never, has
it happened that a republic has had its ordering perfect from the beginning.
Useful, then, was the remedy that was put to the seditions, but not indeed
useful the not removing from the beginning of the causes that afterwards made
them arise.

## FG-CONS.1.4.03

As to the other parts of the Roman government, as to those orders that regard
the form of the government of the republic, I do not wish now to discourse
particularly; but I do not believe they were such that whoever were to order a
republic ought to take them for an example. Most excellent was the military
discipline, and its *virtù* sustained all the other defects of the government;
which matter less in a city that governs itself upon arms than in those that
govern themselves with industry, with whirligigs and with the arts of peace.

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply,
without the capitals of the 1824 *Discorsi* and without a full stop.

**The paragraphs.** Canestrini prints this reply in three paragraphs, and
they are kept: the cause of the disunion, its remedy, and the rest of the
Roman orders.

**`altra volta scritto più largamente`.** "At another time written more
largely": Canestrini's note refers to other writings he was to publish in the
volumes that followed; Guicciardini had weighed the Roman orders at
length in the *Dialogo del reggimento di Firenze*.

**`però`.** "Therefore", twice (D22): he writes briefly because he has
written at length before; and because experience is the only teacher of public
things, a republic has seldom been ordered perfectly from the start.

**`nuove leggi`.** Canestrini's letter prints like an a, as barless e does
throughout this scan. Read as `nuove`, as the 1933 edition prints it.

**`ordini`.** "Orders", of the ranks of the city: patricians and plebeians.
The same word is the orders of government in .03.

**`patrizii`, `plebei`, `plebe`.** "Patricians", "plebeians", "the plebs".
Guicciardini names the two ranks where Machiavelli says nobles and plebs.

**`Ché se ... non nascevano`.** The imperfect in the result of a
contrary-to-fact condition is counterfactual (D47): "would not have arisen".
The period then runs on through `non arebbe` and `né sarebbe`, what the plebs
would not have desired and the magistracy would not have been.

**`comunicati gli onori, era comunicata la potenza`.** "The honours
communicated, the power was communicated": an entailment, stated as a rule —
to share the honours is to share the power. Kept as a plain past, not drawn
into the counterfactual.

**`manco virtuosa`.** "Less virtuous": the adjective of *virtù*. A republic
with less of it would have suffered more from the same divisions.

**`nocumento`, `noceva`.** "Harm", "did harm". `Danno` and `dannose` are
"damage" and "damaging"; the tumults' `nocivi` in Machiavelli are "harmful".

**`è certo ... forse`.** "It is certain that ... perhaps": the certainty is
that the tribunate was perhaps more damaging than useful. Kept as printed.

**`instrumento e colore`.** "Instrument and colour": the tribunate as the tool
and the pretext of those who would disturb the republic.

**`massime`.** "Above all", as in Machiavelli's `e massime quelle cittadi`.

**`lo intercedere`.** "Interceding": the tribune's veto, as in I.3.

**`laudare le disunioni ... la infermità`.** The simile's last word is the
sickness, and the English keeps it last. `Infermo` and `infermità` are "sick
man" and "sickness", one root. The `remedio` is Machiavelli's word for the
harangues (`il rimedio delle concioni`).

**`sotto e' re non noceva`.** "Under the kings it did no harm": the kings held
the authority, so the senate could not oppress the plebs, and the kings saw to
the plebs' conveniences (`comodi`, D59).

**Servius Tullius.** The sixth king, remembered as a friend of the plebs and
the author of the census; `più ambiziosamente che non si doveva`, "more
ambitiously than was due", is Guicciardini's judgment on his courting of it.

**`eleggere ... ne' patrizii`.** "Choose ... into the patricians": the kings'
enrolment of plebeian families among the patricians. `Eleggere` is "choose",
as throughout (I.1).

**`ancora`.** "Yet", in `al quale ancora speravano`: the plebeians bore the
patrician rank more readily because they might yet reach it. The 1933 edition
adds the subject, `loro`.

**`fastiditi`.** "Disdained": held in loathing, as ignoble.

**`forestieri`.** "Strangers" (D57).

**Appius Claudius.** The Sabine Attus Clausus, received with his clients into
the patriciate in the first years of the republic, in the tradition 504 BC;
ancestor of the Claudii.

**`il male presente`.** "The present ill": the kings. `Male` is "ill" (FG
I.2).

**`perizia`.** "Skill": the expertness of the practised.

**`ordinazione`.** "Ordering": with `ordine`, `ordinare`, `ordinatore`.

**`il non levare da principio le cause`.** "The not removing from the
beginning of the causes": the infinitive as a noun, kept, set against `il
rimedio` as the thing that was not useful.

**`non voglio ora discorrere`.** "I do not wish now to discourse": he uses
Machiavelli's verb, from the first line of the chapter, to decline the
discussion (D01).

**`non credo`.** "I do not believe": his act of assent withheld (`credo`,
glossary).

**`per esemplo`.** "For an example": as a model to copy.

**`la virtù sua ... tutti gli altri difetti`.** The military discipline's
*virtù* "sustained all the other defects": the opinion of the many that
Machiavelli set out to answer, that military *virtù* supplied Rome's defects,
is where Guicciardini ends.

**`si regge`, `si governano`.** Both "govern themselves" (D64).

**`girandole`.** "Whirligigs": the spinning wheels of a firework, and
figuratively shifts and schemes. It is set between `industria`, "industry", in
the older sense of ingenious application, and "the arts of peace"; the word is
not a neutral one.
