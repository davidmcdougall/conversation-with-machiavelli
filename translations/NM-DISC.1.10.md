# Discourses I.10

*How much the founders of a republic or of a kingdom are laudable, so much those of a tyranny are vituperable.*

## NM-DISC.1.10.01

Among all men praised, the most praised are those who have been heads and
orderers of religions. Next, afterwards, those who have founded either
republics or kingdoms. After these are renowned those who, appointed to
armies, have expanded either their kingdom, or that of the fatherland. To
these are added men of letters; and because these are of several kinds, each
of them is celebrated according to his degree. To any other man, the number
of whom is infinite, some part of praise is attributed, which his art and his
exercise bring him. On the contrary, infamous and detestable are the men
[who are] destroyers of religions, dissipaters of kingdoms and of republics,
enemies of *virtù*, of letters, and of every other art that brings utility
and honour to the human generation, such as the impious, and violent, the
ignorant, the idle, the vile, and the men of little account. And no one will
ever be so mad, or so wise, so wicked, or so good, that, the choice of the
two qualities of men being proposed to him, he will not praise that which is
to be praised, and blame that which is to be blamed.

## NM-DISC.1.10.02

Nevertheless afterwards almost all, deceived by a false good and by a false
glory, let themselves go, either voluntarily or ignorantly, into the degrees
of those who merit more blame than praise. And being able to make, with
perpetual honour to themselves, either a republic or a kingdom, they turn to
tyranny, nor do they perceive by this course how much fame, how much glory,
how much honour, security, quiet, with satisfaction of spirit, they flee,
and how much infamy, disgrace, blame, danger and disquiet they incur.

## NM-DISC.1.10.03

And it is impossible that those who live in private station in a republic,
or by fortune or *virtù* become princes of it, if they read the histories,
and set store by the memories of ancient things, that they should not wish,
those private men, to live in their fatherland rather as Scipios than as
Caesars; and those who are princes, rather as Agesilauses, Timoleons and
Dions than as Nabises, Phalarises and Dionysiuses; because they would see
these supremely vituperated, and those excessively praised. They would see
also how Timoleon and the others had in their fatherland no less authority
than Dionysius and Phalaris had; but they would see [them] to have had far
more security there.

## NM-DISC.1.10.04

Nor let there be anyone who deceives himself through the glory of Caesar,
hearing him above all celebrated by the writers: because those who praise
him are corrupted by his fortune, and frightened by the length of the rule,
which, governing itself under that name, did not permit the writers to speak
freely of him. But whoever wishes to know what free writers would say of
him, let him see what they say of Catiline. And by so much is Caesar the more
detestable, by how much more he who has done an ill is to be blamed than he
who has wished to do it. Let him see also with how many praises they
celebrate Brutus; so that, not being able to blame that man because of his
power, they celebrate his enemy.

## NM-DISC.1.10.05

Let him who has become prince in a republic consider also how many more
praises, after Rome had become an empire, those emperors merited who lived
under the laws, and as good princes, than those who lived to the contrary;
and he will see how to Titus, Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus and Marcus
the praetorian soldiers were not necessary, nor the multitude of the legions
to defend them, because their customs, the benevolence of the people, the
love of the senate defended them. He will see also how to Caligula, Nero,
Vitellius, and to so many other villainous emperors the eastern and western
armies did not suffice to save them against those enemies whom their evil
customs, their vicious life had generated for them. And if the history of
these were well considered, it would be very good instruction to any prince,
to show him the path of glory or of blame, and of his security or of his
fear. For of twenty-six emperors who were from Caesar to Maximinus, sixteen
were killed, ten died ordinarily; and if of those who were slain there was
some good one, like Galba and Pertinax, he was slain by that corruption that
his predecessor had left in the soldiers. And if among those who died
ordinarily there was some villainous one, like Severus, it arose from a very
great fortune and *virtù* of his, which two things few men accompany.

## NM-DISC.1.10.06

He will see also, by the reading of this history, how a good kingdom can be
ordered; for all the emperors who succeeded to the rule by inheritance,
except Titus, were bad; those who by adoption, were all good, as were those
five from Nerva to Marcus. And when the rule fell to the heirs, it returned
into its ruin. Let a prince, then, set before himself the times from Nerva to
Marcus, and compare them with those that had been before, and that were
after; and afterwards let him choose in which he would wish to have been
born, or to which he would wish to be appointed.

## NM-DISC.1.10.07

For in those governed by the good he will see a prince secure in the middle
of his secure citizens, filled with peace and with justice the world; he
will see the senate with its authority, the magistracies with their honours,
the rich citizens enjoying their riches, nobility and *virtù* exalted; he
will see every quiet and every good; and on the other hand every rancour,
every licence, corruption and ambition extinguished; he will see the golden
times, where each can hold and defend that opinion which he wishes. He will
see, in the end, the world triumph; full of reverence and of glory the
prince; of love and of security the peoples. If he afterwards considers
minutely the times of the other emperors, he will see them atrocious through
wars, discordant through seditions, in peace and in war cruel, so many princes slain by the sword, so many civil wars, so many external, Italy
afflicted, and full of new misfortunes, ruined and sacked the cities of it.
He will see Rome burned, the Capitol undone by its citizens, desolate the
ancient temples, corrupted the ceremonies, filled with adulteries the cities;
he will see the sea full of exiles, the rocks full of blood. He will see in
Rome innumerable cruelties follow; and nobility, riches, honours, and above
all *virtù* imputed as a capital sin. He will see accusers rewarded, servants
corrupted against their lord, freedmen against their master, and those who
had lacked enemies oppressed by their friends. And he will know then very
well how many debts Rome, Italy, and the world have to Caesar.

## NM-DISC.1.10.08

And without doubt, if he be born of man, he will be frightened away from
every imitation of the bad times, and will be kindled with an immense desire
to follow the good. And truly, a prince seeking the glory of the world ought
to desire to possess a corrupt city, not to spoil it wholly like Caesar, but
to reorder it like Romulus. And truly the heavens cannot give men greater
occasion of glory, nor can men desire a greater. And if, to wish to order a
city well, one were of necessity to lay down the principate, he who did not
order it, so as not to fall from that degree, would merit some excuse. But,
it being possible to keep the principate and order [the city], no excuse
whatever is merited. And in sum, let those to whom the heavens give such
occasion consider how two paths are proposed to them: the one, which makes
them live secure, and after death renders them glorious; the other makes
them live in continual straits, and after death leave behind them an
everlasting infamy.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Quanto ... tanto`, "How much ... so much": a proportion, as in
the rubric of I.8. The one verdict on Caesar in the chapter is the same
proportion turned round, `tanto ... quanto`, "by so much ... by how much"
(.04). Guicciardini's reply names the rubric "the title of this Discourse",
calls it most true, and restates it with kingdoms first (Guicciardini .01).

**`Capi e ordinatori delle Religioni`.** "Heads and orderers of religions":
`capo` and `ordinatore` as the glossary has them. The ranking runs founders of
religions, of republics and kingdoms, captains, men of letters, and then
everyone else, each praised for his art. `Sono celebri` (captains) is
"renowned", `sono celebrati` (men of letters) "celebrated".

**`delle virtù`.** *Virtù* in the plural, the capacities that the destroyers
are enemies of, set beside letters and the arts; the loan-word cannot show the
number. Elsewhere in the chapter *virtù* is a quality of men: the private man
who becomes prince `per fortuna o virtù` (.03), Severus' `fortuna e virtù`
(.05), and the *virtù* honoured in the good age and imputed as a capital sin in
the bad (.07).

**`i vili, e i da pochi`.** "The vile, and the men of little account": `vile` in
its older sense, base, wanting in spirit; men *da poco*, of little worth. The
1554 edition has no `e i da pochi` (NM-DISC-I10-D002).

**`sì tristo, o sì buono`.** "So wicked, or so good": `tristo` is "wicked"
(D03). `Scellerati`, twice of emperors (.05), is "villainous", and `malvagia
vita` "vicious life", so that the three words stay apart from `rei costumi`,
"evil customs".

**`dipoi`.** "Afterwards", in all four places (.01, .02, .06, .07), so that
"then" stays `adunque`'s (D74).

**`incorrono`.** "They incur": a *currere* compound, the running-into that
answers `fuggono`, "they flee", in the same period (D01, D171).

**`vituperio`.** "Disgrace" (D26). The adjective and the verb keep the root:
`vituperabili` "vituperable" in the rubric, `vituperati` "vituperated" (.03).

**`gradi`.** "Degrees" (glossary `grado`): each man of letters according to his
degree (.01), the degrees of the praised and the blamed (.02), and the
principate's height (`cadere di quel grado`, "fall from that degree", .08).

**`quelli tali privati`.** "Those private men": the subject is resumed after
the long conditional, as Machiavelli resumes it, with a second "that" (D136).

**`facessero capitale`.** "Set store by": `fare capitale di`, to count on, to
treat as a stock. "Make capital of" now means to exploit.

**Scipios and Caesars.** Scipio Africanus, who held no power beyond the
republic's, against Julius Caesar; among princes, Agesilaus II of Sparta,
Timoleon of Corinth (who freed Syracuse and laid down his command) and Dion
of Syracuse, against Nabis, tyrant of Sparta, Phalaris of Acragas and
Dionysius of Syracuse. The plurals are Machiavelli's (`Agesilai`, `Nabidi`).

**`Né sia alcuno che s'inganni`.** "Nor let there be anyone who deceives
himself": reflexive, against the true passive of .02 (`ingannati da`).

**`quanto più è da biasimare quello che ha fatto, che quello che ha voluto
fare un male`.** He who has done an ill is more to be blamed than he who
wished to do it: Caesar did what Catiline only attempted. The English moves
"an ill" to the first clause, where it is understood; the Italian sets it at
the end, after the second verb. Brutus is Marcus Junius Brutus.

**`Imperio`.** D124 occurrence by occurrence. "Rule" for the sovereignty of
the Caesars (`la lunghezza dell'Imperio, il quale reggendosi sotto quel
nome`, .04) and for what passes by inheritance (`succederono all'Imperio`,
`l'Imperio cadde negli eredi`, .06); "empire" where Rome changes its form
(`poi che Roma fu diventata Imperio`, .05), as I.5 `fare uno Imperio`.

**The emperors.** The good: Titus, Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius and
Marcus Aurelius; "those five from Nerva to Marcus" are the adoptive emperors,
and Titus the one good heir. The bad: Caligula, Nero, Vitellius. Galba and
Pertinax were killed by the soldiery; Severus is Septimius Severus. The count
of twenty-six from Caesar to Maximinus (Maximinus Thrax, 235–238) is
Machiavelli's own; the 1554 edition prints it in numerals.

**`morirono ordinariamente`.** "Died ordinarily": by the ordinary way, not by
the sword, as `ordinariamente` in I.7 (D151).

**`le quali due cose pochi uomini accompagnano`.** "Which two things few men
accompany". The Italian can be construed either way: few men join great
fortune to great *virtù*, or the two things accompany few men. The English
keeps the order and with it the first construction; the sense is close.

**`in quelli governati da' buoni`.** "In those governed by the good": the
times governed by good emperors.

**The two ages.** Machiavelli sets the vision out as absolutes with the
predicate first (`ripieno di pace e di giustizia il Mondo`, `desolati gli
antichi templi`); the English keeps that order throughout .07.

**`i Magistrati coi suoi onori`.** "The magistracies with their honours": the
offices, as D93.

**`spenta`.** "Extinguished": rancour, licence, corruption and ambition put
out, as a fire.

**`dove ciascuno può tenere e difendere quella opinione che vuole`.** "Where
each can hold and defend that opinion which he wishes". Cf. Tacitus,
*Histories* 1.1, of the times of Nerva and Trajan: *ubi sentire quae velis et
quae sentias dicere licet*, "when one may think what one wishes and say what
one thinks".

**`peccato capitale`.** "A capital sin": a crime punishable by death, in the
word for sin.

**`quanti obblighi Roma, Italia, e il Mondo abbia con Cesare`.** "How many
debts": `obbligo` is "debt" (D17), and ironic, as in I.12.

**`se e' sarà nato d'uomo`.** "If he be born of man": if he is a human being at
all. The future perfect is conjectural.

**`si sbigottirà da`.** "Will be frightened away from": the `da` separates.

**`ordinarla`.** The city, not the principate, which is masculine; the English
supplies "[the city]" in .08, where the verbs are impersonal.

**`continue angustie`.** "Continual straits". The 1554 edition reads `continoue
molestie`, "continual vexations" (NM-DISC-I10-D009).
