# Consideration on chapter VIII of Book I

*As much as accusations are useful to republics, so much are calumnies pernicious*

## FG-CONS.1.8.01

It is a true conclusion that calumnies are detestable, but so natural in a
free city that it is difficult and perhaps impossible to remove them; for when
a false charge arises against a citizen, which can arise from the malignity of
whoever is its author and also from error, how can one provide that it does
not spread in the multitude, which is more inclined to believe the ill than
the good? And also there is no lack of many who for hatred or for envy foment
these uproars, and therefore at Rome, in which the path of accusing was so easy
and broad, how many were the charges given falsely to citizens? For an example
there is Fabius Maximus, and many others, nor can one always accuse or punish
whoever calumniates wrongly, nor can one, otherwise than in writing, form a
way of republic that provides so promptly against all the disorders. Therefore
in every free people there was and will always be abundance of calumniators,
suffice it, that false calumnies, with time and with the truth, are often
extinguished of themselves. Nor will the indignation of being calumniated ever
carry a grave citizen away to make disorder against the republic, and although
he will have indignation against him who he thinks was the author of the
calumny, he has indignation also, and much greater, against him who has
accused him falsely. But these particular indignations never make
important disorder in a city that is otherwise well regulated, as neither do
calumnies, which, when they are scandalous, as was that of Manlius Capitolinus,
which tended to raise the plebs against the senate, are oppressed, if they are
not scandalous, are let go, for they fall of themselves. And the example of
Cosimo, figured in his Discourse without naming him, is a dream, for to him
the path to greatness was opened not by calumnies, but by prudence, and
principally by excessive wealth, with which, the government of Florence being most
disordered and full by its nature of sedition, it was easy for him to corrupt
the citizens, and, fomenting the divisions of the city, to walk, by making
himself head of a party, to tyranny. And because this matter, for
proving the conclusion of the Discourse, is narrow of examples, that of messer
Giovanni Guicciardini was begged, of whom it is true that he was calumniated
unjustly, and that, through the judgments' being disordered, he had no way by
means of them to justify his innocence, even though he made every effort, as
far as presenting himself voluntarily in prison, but from his calumny the division of the city did not
arise, nor from this were [they] augmented, but rather, on the contrary, the
discords of the citizens fomented this case of his and made it of more moment
than by the ordinary it would have been

## Notes

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply,
with lower-case `repubbliche` and without a full stop.

**The paragraph.** Canestrini prints one, and it is kept.

**`[È] vera conclusione`.** "It is a true conclusion": assent to Machiavelli's
`detestabili le calunnie` (.02), in his own word. The witness prints `E`
without the accent; as printed the period has no main verb, and it is
emended as his I.3 and I.7 openings were (D114, D144, D157). The assent is
to the conclusion; the reply is against the remedy and the examples.

**`tanto naturali in una città libera`.** "So natural in a free city": the
1933 edition reads the singular `naturale` (FG-CONS-I8-D003).

**`il levarle`.** "To remove them": a nominal infinitive in the Italian, which
the English does not show. Machiavelli
asked for the order "to take them away" (`torle via`, .02); Guicciardini
doubts it can be done.

**`uno carico falso`.** "A false charge": `carico`, a burden laid on a man's
name.

**`malignità`.** "Malignity" (glossary).

**`provedere`.** "Provide": take measures. `Proveda` returns of the way of
republic that "provides so promptly".

**`il male che il bene`.** "The ill than the good" (glossary, `male`).

**`romori`.** "Uproars" (D155): public clamour, here stirred up around a false
charge.

**`e però a Roma`.** "And therefore at Rome" (D22). The consequence is in the
argument, not the history: many foment false charges, and so even Rome, with
its easy path of accusing, had many.

**`la via dello accusare`.** "The path of accusing" (D131): Machiavelli's
open places for accusation (.02) were Rome's, and did not stop calumny there.

**`e carichi`.** "The charges": the plural article (D35).

**`Fabio Massimo`.** Quintus Fabius Maximus, the Delayer, whose caution
against Hannibal was decried at Rome as cowardice and worse; Hannibal spared
his estate to make him suspect, and his command was split with his master of
the horse in 217 BC (Livy 22.12–26).

**`a torto`.** "Wrongly".

**`altrimenti che per scrittura`.** "Otherwise than in writing": only on paper
can a republic be framed to provide against every disorder.

**`modo di republica`.** "Way of republic" (glossary, `modo`).

**`Però`.** "Therefore" (D22), again: the second consequence, that free
peoples always have calumniators.

**`basta, che`.** "Suffice it, that": it is enough. Canestrini's commas are
kept; the 1933 edition has `calunniatori; basta che`.

**`si spengono ... per sé stesse`.** "Are extinguished of themselves" (as
`spegnere`, I.7). The calumnies "fall of themselves" too (`da sé medesime
caggiono`).

**`trasporterà`.** "Will carry ... away": as a passion does. The 1933 edition
spells it `traporterá` (FG-CONS-I8-D004).

**`uno cittadino grave`.** "A grave citizen": of weight and judgment.

**`sdegno`, `sdegni particulari`.** "Indignation", "particular
indignations" (`particulare`, D93): private resentments, as against Machiavelli's
"indignation" of Giovanni's friends, which he made a public cause.

**`contro a chi l'ha accusato falsamente`.** "Against him who has accused him
falsely": two objects of indignation, the presumed author of the calumny and,
much more, whoever accused him falsely. The word is `accusato`, not
`calunniato`; whether the false accuser is the calumniator, or a formal
accuser under Machiavelli's remedy, the Italian does not say, and the English
does not either.

**`regolata`.** "Regulated": ordered.

**`come né anche fanno le calunnie`.** "As neither do calumnies". The 1933
edition prints `neanche` (FG-CONS-I8-D005).

**`si opprimono`.** "Are oppressed": put down (glossary, `opprimere`).
Manlius is Machiavelli's example and Guicciardini's: the scandalous calumny,
put down when the dictator imprisoned Manlius.

**`si lasciano andare`.** "Are let go": the sentence runs on by commas,
without a conjunction, as the Italian does.

**`lo esemplo di Cosimo figurato nel Discorso suo, sanza nominarlo`.** "The
example of Cosimo, figured in his Discourse without naming him": Machiavelli's
"some citizen" who rose by calumnies against the powerful (.03). Guicciardini
names him: Cosimo de' Medici. Canestrini's note, which is his and is not
carried, says the same.

**`è un sogno`.** "Is a dream": the 1933 edition prints `uno sogno`.

**`a lui aperse la via alla grandezza non le calunnie, ma la prudenza`.** "To
him the path to greatness was opened not by calumnies, but by prudence": the
subjects come last in the Italian, and the passive keeps them last (D18).

**`con le quali`.** "With which": the plural takes both prudence and wealth.

**`prudenza`.** "Prudence" (glossary): practical judgment, not caution.

**`disordinatissimo`.** "Most disordered", in the `ordine` family.

**`corrompere e' cittadini`.** "To corrupt the citizens": Machiavelli's
`corrotto` (.03, .04), the charge in the calumnies, is here what Cosimo did.

**`camminare, col farsi capo di una parte, alla tirannide`.** "To walk, by
making himself head of a party, to tyranny": the Italian puts the means
between the verb and its goal, and the English keeps it there. `Capo` as
in I.12 (glossary).

**`è stretta di esempli`.** "Is narrow of examples": short of them. The
purpose phrase stays between subject and predicate, as in the Italian. Rome's
path of accusing was "broad" (`larga`); Machiavelli's matter is narrow.

**`fu mendicato`.** "Was begged": sought for want of better. Giovanni
Guicciardini was of his own family.

**`il quale è vero che ..., e che`.** "Of whom it is true that ..., and
that": one concession governing two facts. He grants Machiavelli both, the
unjust calumny and the want of a way to clear himself, which is Machiavelli's
diagnosis (`per non essere modi in quella Repubblica`, .04).

**`per essere e' giudicii disordinati`.** "Through the judgments' being
disordered": the courts (glossary, `giudicii`). The 1933 edition reads
`giudici`, judges (FG-CONS-I8-D006).

**`insino a rapresentarsi volontariamente in carcere`.** "As far as presenting
himself voluntarily in prison": where Machiavelli has him willing to put
himself in the Captain's hands.

**`né da questo si augumentorono`.** "Nor from this were [they] augmented":
`da questo` repeats `dalla calunnia sua`, "from his calumny". The
verb is plural and has no subject until the next clause names the discords;
the English supplies "[they]" (D53). `Augumentare`, "augment" (D69).

**`anzi pel contrario`.** "But rather, on the contrary": `anzi` corrects the
negative (D90), and `pel contrario` reverses.

**`fomentorono e feciono di più momento questo caso suo`.** "Fomented this
case of his and made it of more moment": both verbs take `questo caso suo`.
The `e` prints a-shaped in Canestrini; read `e` (D156). `Fomentare` is the
verb of the many who "foment these uproars" and of Cosimo "fomenting the
divisions". `Di momento` is Machiavelli's phrase for Manlius' tumults (.01).
The causation runs the other way from Machiavelli's: the discords made the
case, not the case the discords.

**`per lo ordinario`.** "By the ordinary", as Machiavelli's `per l'ordinario`
in I.7 (D151). It can mean "in the ordinary course", as things would have
gone; it can also mean by the ordinary way of the orders, which would make
Guicciardini answer Machiavelli's argument in Machiavelli's phrase. The
English keeps the phrase so that both stay open.

**No full stop.** Canestrini prints none after `stato`; the 1933 edition
does. The English ends as the Italian does (D40).
