# Consideration on chapter X of Book I

*How much the founders of a republic or of a kingdom are laudable, so much those of a tyranny are vituperable*

## FG-CONS.1.10.01

The title of this Discourse is most true, for supreme praise the founders of
kingdoms and of republics merit, supreme blame the founders of tyranny. But
because the cases are various, and the author confounds the examples, it is required to consider that it seldom occurs that whoever occupies the tyranny in
a free fatherland has such necessity to do it, or, if he has necessity, that it
be caused without fault of his, so that there remain to him any colour of
justification. And this sort of men, among whom was Caesar, full of many other
*virtù*, but oppressed by the ambition of dominating, are certainly most
inhuman and detestable. It is true that at times the forms of the liberties
are so disordered, and the cities so full of civil discords, that necessity
leads some citizen, not being able to save himself otherwise, to seek the
tyranny, or to adhere to whoever seeks it. In which case he would be very
laudable who should put the love of the fatherland before his particular
safety, but because this love or this fortitude is desired in men rather than
it is found, he merits to be much excused who is moved by such a cause, and so
much the more if the government, against which he goes, is disordered, for
many are often called liberties that are not. The example can be set in our
city, where, after the change of the state of '26, some good and
well-qualified citizens have been persecuted and trampled, and at the last, at
the coming of the prince of Orange, necessitated either to disobey the
commands made by the Eight to stay in Florence under pain of rebellion, or to
remain in danger of being killed, and at the least with certainty of being
detained as suspects. Whom necessity has led either to desire the change of a
state that under the name of liberty is tyrannical and destroyer of the
fatherland, or tacitly to let themselves, with supreme injustice, have the
fatherland and their substance taken away. Whoever, then, is author in a free
fatherland of a tyranny, and does it from appetite of dominating, merits
supreme reproof; and of these was Caesar, Phalaris, Pisistratus and the like,
of whom one is more infamous than the other, according as they used it more
or less cruelly, and according as they were more or less adorned with other
*virtù*.

## FG-CONS.1.10.02

The other case is of those to whom the tyranny is left hereditary, who merit
less blame continuing in it than do those who have founded it from the
beginning, and leaving it would merit so much the more praise, as they are
less debtors to cancel the sin of having usurped it. Of these there are found
very few or perhaps none that without necessity have left it, nor is it a
marvel, for whoever is nourished in a tyranny has not eyes to know that glory
that is acquired by putting the fatherland in liberty, nor does he consider
this case with that taste that private men do, for, accustomed to that way of
living, he judges that the supreme good is in power; and not knowing the fruit
of that glory, no other reason can persuade him to leave the tyranny. Besides
that the danger can hold him back, even if he had the will to it, for it is
difficult that a tyranny should have been able to be acquired and conserved
without many enmities and without offence to many; therefore to reduce
oneself to private [station], or to leave after oneself one's sons private,
seems a dangerous thing, above all since peoples are ungrateful, and new
liberties are commonly full of disorders. And if Sulla did it, it is a most
rare example, and he was able to do it more securely, for the government
remained in the hands of the men of his faction, in such a way that not only
was he secure while he lived, but also, he being dead, his acts were conserved
and reverence had for his memory.

## FG-CONS.1.10.03

Other is the case of those who are kings and princes, either created
legitimately, as were the kings of Lacedaemon, as were the first Roman
kings, or who through the length of time are held legitimate. Of these, if
they have the authority unbound, there is found yet some one who governs
justly in such a way that he merits the name of being a good prince, but I do
not know which [of them] reduce the kingdom to that perfection of orders which
deservedly ought to be, that is, to order it in such a way that not the sons
or the nearest have the kingdom by inheritance, but that one succeed by
choice. And if in any kingdom there has been this institution, I believe some
necessity has conserved it there more than the will of those who have
reigned, for too great is the love that fathers bear to their sons, nor is
that small which bears them on to leave the memory of their house illustrious.

## FG-CONS.1.10.04

Therefore these thoughts, that tyrants should lay down tyrannies, and that
kings should order kingdoms well, depriving their posterity of the
succession, are painted more readily in books and in the imaginations of men
than they are executed in fact; indeed, as much as the reasonings of private
men about them are frequent, so much are the examples of them rare, and
therefore those merit lesser reproof who do not do the things, the like of
which there are found very few and perhaps none that has done [them].

## Notes

**`Il titulo di questo Discorso`.** "The title of this Discourse": Machiavelli's
rubric, which the reply names, grants and restates in its own words, with
kingdoms now before republics, and `biasimo`, blame, for `vituperabili`
(Machiavelli .01). The order matters, because .03 turns to kings. `Titulo` is
Guicciardini's Latin form; the 1933 edition prints `titolo`.

**`somma`, `sommo`.** "Supreme": supreme praise, blame, injustice, reproof, the
supreme good, as Machiavelli's `sommamente vituperati`, "supremely
vituperated" (Machiavelli .03).

**`e lo autore confonde gli esempli`.** "And the author confounds the
examples": the reply's charge. Machiavelli draws his examples indifferently
from a citizen who made a tyranny in a free city (Caesar), from tyrants (Nabis,
Phalaris, Dionysius), and from emperors who came to rule by inheritance or by
adoption. Guicciardini separates the man who makes a tyranny, the man who
inherits one, and the legitimate king.

**`rare volte occorre`.** "It seldom occurs": `rare volte` as D97, and
`occorrere` as D01.

**`talmente che gli resti colore alcuno`.** "So that there remain to him any
colour": `alcuno` under the `rare volte` of the governing clause, any colour at
all. Both readers were unsure of a letter in `resti`; it is read with the 1933
edition (D182).

**`inumanissimi`.** "Most inhuman". The 1933 edition reads `immanissimi`, "most
monstrous" (FG-CONS-I10-D002).

**`le forme delle libertà`.** "The forms of the liberties": the plural, of free
governments.

**`la salute sua particulare`.** "His particular safety": his own good against
the fatherland's, as FG I.8 `sdegni particulari`, "particular indignations"
(D93).

**`si desidera negli uomini più presto che la si truovi`.** "Is desired in men
rather than it is found": `più presto ... che` as D135.

**`merita essere assai scusato`.** "He merits to be much excused": excuse,
Machiavelli's word for the founder in I.9 and for the prince in the last lines
of I.10 (Machiavelli .08).

**`perché molte sono chiamate spesso libertà che non sono`.** "For many are
often called liberties that are not": governments called free.

**The example of Florence.** The Medici were driven out in May 1527 and the
popular government restored. "Of '26" is as both editions print it, and the
reading stands; Canestrini's note, which is his and is not carried, dates the
change to 1527. In 1529, after the peace between the pope and the emperor, the
prince of Orange (Philibert of Chalon) led the imperial army against the city.
The order to stay, Guicciardini says, was made by the Eight (the Otto di
Guardia, the magistracy of police and security). Guicciardini himself left
Florence in 1529 and was afterwards declared a rebel.

**`sotto pena di rebellione`.** "Under pain of rebellion": of being declared
rebels, which carried the confiscation of goods.

**`sostenuti come sospetti`.** "Detained as suspects": `sostenere` in its
period sense, to hold in custody.

**`e almanco con certezza`.** "And at the least with certainty": both readers
were unsure of the letter; it is read `e` with the 1933 edition (D183).

**`uno Stato che sotto nome di libertà è tirannico`.** The popular government
is the tyranny: the citizens are led to desire its change, not drawn towards
tyranny.

**`lasciarsi ... tòrre la patria e le facultà`.** "Let themselves ... have the
fatherland and their substance taken away": `tòrre`, take away (D131).
`Facultà` is here what one owns: "substance", not "capacity", which is the word's
other sense (D198).

**`[È] vero`, `[È] altro`.** Canestrini prints `E` without the accent at both;
as printed neither period has a finite verb, and the 1933 edition prints `È`.
Emended (D181); the English follows the emended text.

**`Pisistrato`.** Pisistratus, tyrant of Athens, whom Machiavelli does not name
here. Guicciardini adds him to Caesar and Phalaris.

**`la usorono`.** "Used it": the tyranny.

**`quanto manco sono debitori di cancellare il peccato d'averla usurpata`.** "As
they are less debtors to cancel the sin of having usurped it": the heir did not
commit the usurpation, so laying the tyranny down is more freely given.
"Debtors" has no Italian echo of Machiavelli's `obblighi`, "debts" (D17).

**`pochissimi o forse nessuno`.** "Very few or perhaps none": returns as
`pochissimi e forse nessuno` in the reply's last line, with `o` become `e`.

**`nessuna altra ragione`.** Read `nessuna` with the 1933 edition (D184).

**`Sanza che`.** "Besides that": a further cause, in a period left without a
main clause.

**`n'avessi volontà`.** "Had the will to it": `volontà`, will (D117). The
reply spells it `voluntà` in .03; both are kept as printed.

**`ridursi privato`.** "To reduce oneself to private [station]": Machiavelli's
`in istato privato`, "in private station" (Machiavelli .03).

**Sulla.** Lucius Cornelius Sulla, dictator from 82/81 BC, gave up the
dictatorship and retired to private life by 79 BC, and died in 78. Much of his
legislation outlived him; the tribunes' powers he had cut were restored in
70 BC.

**`e' re di Lacedemone`, `e' primi re romani`.** "The kings of Lacedaemon":
Sparta's two hereditary kings. "Lacedaemon" as FG I.2. The first Roman kings
were chosen, not born to the throne: of his two examples of kings created
legitimately, one is hereditary and one elective.

**`la autorità sciolta`.** "The authority unbound": not bound by laws or other
magistrates.

**`si truova pure qualcuno`.** "There is found yet some one": `pure`
concessive, granting before the `ma`.

**`ma io non so quali che riduchino`.** "But I do not know which [of them]
reduce": `non so`, the suspension of the four acts (problematic.md). He does
not know which kings do it, and the next period allows that some kingdom may
have had the institution.

**`per elezione`.** "By choice": `elezione` is "choice" throughout the edition,
including the choice of kings (glossary). It answers Machiavelli's emperors
`per adozione` (Machiavelli .06).

**`portano`, `porta`.** "Bear", "bears them on": the love fathers bear their
sons, and the love that bears them on to leave their house's memory
illustrious. The 1933 edition reads `che si porta` (FG-CONS-I10-D006).

**`anzi`.** "Indeed": correcting upward after an affirmative (D147).

**`quanto ... tanto`.** The rubric's proportion, turned to measure how often
private men reason about these things against how rarely they are done.

**`minore reprensione`.** "Lesser reproof": not excuse, and not praise. It
falls on those who do not lay down a tyranny or disinherit their posterity,
the case to which Machiavelli granted "some excuse"; his "no excuse whatever"
was for the prince who could keep the principate and order the city
(Machiavelli .08).

**`bisogna`.** "It is required": the edition's rendering of the impersonal `bisognare`, in every tense (D260); the reasons are in the note on `bisogni` at Machiavelli I.2.
