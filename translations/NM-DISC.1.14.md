# Discourses I.14

*The Romans interpreted the auspices according to necessity, and with prudence
made a show of observing religion when, forced, they did not observe it, and if
anyone rashly despised it, they punished him.*

## NM-DISC.1.14.01

Not only were the auguries, as has been discoursed above, in good part the
foundation of the ancient religion of the Gentiles, but they were also those
that were the cause of the well-being of the Roman republic. Whence the Romans
had more care of them than of any other order of it, and used them in the
consular comitia, in beginning enterprises, in leading out the armies, in giving
battle, and in every important action of theirs, whether civil or military; nor
would they ever have gone on an expedition without having persuaded the soldiers
that the gods promised them the victory. And among the other haruspicies they
had in the armies certain orders of auspices, which they called *pullarii*. And
whenever they ordered battle to be given with the enemy, they wished the
*pullarii* to make their auspices; and, the chickens pecking, they fought with
good augury; not pecking, they abstained from the engagement.

## NM-DISC.1.14.02

Nonetheless, when reason showed them a thing ought to be done, notwithstanding
that the auspices were adverse, they did it in any case; but they turned it
about with terms and modes so attentively that it should not appear they did it in
contempt of religion: which term was used by Papirius the consul in a most important engagement that he had with the Samnites, after which they remained
altogether weak and afflicted.

## NM-DISC.1.14.03

For, Papirius being in the field over against the Samnites, and it seeming to him that victory in the engagement was certain, and wishing for this reason to
give battle, he commanded the *pullarii* to make their auspices; but, the
chickens not pecking, and the prince of the *pullarii* seeing the great
disposition of the army to fight, and the opinion that was in the captain and in
all the soldiers of winning, so as not to take from that army the occasion of acting well, he reported to the consul that the auspices were proceeding well; so
that, Papirius ordering the squadrons, and it being said by some of the
*pullarii* to certain soldiers that the chickens had not pecked, they told it to
Spurius Papirius, nephew of the consul, and he reporting it to the consul, [the consul] answered at once that he should attend to doing his office well, and that
as for himself and the army the auspices were right, and if the *pullarius* had
told lies, they would turn back to his prejudice. And so that the effect should
correspond to the prognostic, he commanded the legates to station the *pullarii*
in the front line of the engagement. Whence it arose that, as they went against the
enemy, a dart thrown by a Roman soldier by chance killed the prince of the
*pullarii*; which thing being heard, the consul said that everything was
proceeding well, and with the favour of the gods, for the army by the death of
that liar had purged itself of every fault, and of every anger that they might
have taken against it. And so, by knowing well how to accommodate his designs to
the auspices, he took the decision to engage, without that army perceiving that
in any part he had neglected the orders of their religion.

## NM-DISC.1.14.04

Appius Pulcher did the contrary in Sicily in the first Punic war, who, wishing to engage the Carthaginian army, had the auspices made by the *pullarii*,
and, they reporting to him that the chickens were not pecking, said, let us see
whether they would drink, and had them thrown into the sea; whereupon, engaging, he lost the battle, for which he was condemned at Rome, and Papirius honoured,
not so much for the one having lost and the other won, as for the one having
acted against the auspices prudently, and the other rashly.

## NM-DISC.1.14.05

Nor did this mode of haruspicating tend to any other end than to make the
soldiers go confidently to the engagement, from which confidence victory almost
always arises. Which thing was used not only by the Romans but by foreigners; of
which I think fit to adduce an example in the following chapter.

## Notes

**`auspizj`, `augurj`, `aruspizj`.** Three different words, kept as three:
auspices, the taking of signs from birds; auguries, the art and its readings;
haruspicies, properly the reading of entrails. Machiavelli uses them loosely: he
counts the chicken-auspices among the "haruspicies" in .01, and calls the whole
practice "haruspicating" in .05. The English keeps his words rather than
correcting his Roman antiquities.

***pullarii*.** The keepers of the sacred chickens, whose feeding was read as an
auspice: if the birds ate eagerly, the gods approved. Left in Latin, as the
Italian keeps the office's name (`Pollarj`); Guicciardini calls them `Pullarii`.

**`il Principe de' Pollarj`.** "The prince of the *pullarii*": the chief of the
college. The word is kept because it is the word Machiavelli uses of rulers;
here it has its Latin sense of first man.

**`termine`.** "Term" in the older sense of a manner of proceeding, not a
word: `termini e modi` is close to "ways and means", and "which term" picks up
the device Papirius used, not anything he said.

**.03, `rispose`.** The Italian gives the verb no subject, and the last man
named is the nephew. It is the consul who answers; the English supplies him in
brackets.

**.03, `contro di lui`.** "Against him" could be the liar, but the sense is the
anger the gods might have taken against the army, which by the liar's death has
purged itself of it. The English says "it", for the army.

**`zuffa`, `azzuffarsi`, `combattere`, `giornata`.** Engagement, engage, fight,
battle: four words, kept apart. `Zuffa` is used of Aquilonia, a pitched battle,
so it is not a skirmish.

**.03, `in pregiudizio suo`.** "To his prejudice", in the old sense of harm: if
the chicken-keeper lied, the lie falls on his own head, as it does.

**The sources.** Livy 10.40, for Lucius Papirius Cursor, son of the dictator of the same name,
before Aquilonia (293 BC). The
chickens thrown into the sea belong to Publius Claudius Pulcher, before the
sea-fight off Drepana in Sicily (249 BC), in Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.7, and
Livy, *Periochae* 19. Machiavelli calls him Appius, his father's name.
