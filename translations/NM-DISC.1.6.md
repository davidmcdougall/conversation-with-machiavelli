# Discourses I.6

*Whether in Rome a state could be ordered that should take away the enmities
between the people and the senate.*

## NM-DISC.1.6.01

We have discoursed above of the effects that the controversies between the
people and the senate made. Now, these having continued up to the time of the
Gracchi, where they were the cause of the ruin of free living, someone might
desire that Rome had made the great effects that it made without there being
such enmities in it; therefore it has seemed to me a thing worthy of
consideration to see whether in Rome a state could be ordered that should take
away the said controversies. And if one wishes to examine this, it is
necessary to have recourse to those republics which, without so many enmities
and tumults, have long been free, and to see what their state was, and whether
it could be introduced into Rome.

## NM-DISC.1.6.02

For an example, among the ancients there is Sparta, among the moderns Venice,
named by me above. Sparta made a king with a small senate that should govern
it. Venice has not divided the government by names, but under one appellation
all those who can have administration are called gentlemen. Which way chance
gave, more than the prudence of whoever gave them the laws; for, many
inhabitants having withdrawn onto those rocks where that city is now, for the
causes stated above, when they had grown to such a number that, if they wished
to live together, it was needful for them to make laws, they ordered a form of
government; and, convening often together in the councils to deliberate on
the city, when it seemed to them that they were so many as to be sufficient
for a political living, they closed the path to all those others who should
come to live there anew, of being able to convene in their governments; and,
in time, there being found in that place many inhabitants outside the
government, to give reputation to those who governed they called them
gentlemen, and the others commoners. This way could arise and maintain itself
without tumult, because when it arose whoever then lived in Venice was made
[part] of the government, so that no one could complain; those who afterwards
came to live there, finding the state firm and bounded, had neither cause nor
convenience to make tumult. The cause was not there, because nothing had been
taken from them. The convenience was not there, because whoever governed held
them in rein, and did not employ them in anything where they might take
authority. Besides this, those who afterwards came to live in Venice have not
been many, or of such a number that there should be disproportion between
those who govern them and them who are governed; for the number of the
gentlemen is either equal to them or greater; so that for these causes Venice
could order that state and maintain it united.

## NM-DISC.1.6.03

Sparta, as I have said, being governed by a king and by a narrow senate, could
maintain itself so long a time; because, there being few inhabitants in
Sparta, and having taken away the path from whoever might come to live there,
and having taken up with reputation the laws of Lycurgus, observing which
[they] removed all the causes of the tumults, they could live united a long
time; for Lycurgus with his laws made in Sparta more equality of substance and
less equality of degree; for there was an equal poverty there, and the
plebeians were less ambitious, because the degrees of the city were spread
among few citizens and were kept at a distance from the plebs; nor did the
nobles, by treating them ill, ever give them the desire to have them. This
arose from the Spartan kings, who, being placed in that principate and set in
the middle of that nobility, had no greater remedy for keeping their dignity
firm than to keep the plebs defended from every injury; which made the plebs
not fear and not desire rule; and, not having rule nor fearing, the rivalry it
might have with the nobility was removed, and the cause of the tumults, and
they could live united a long time. But two principal things caused this
union: the one, that the inhabitants of Sparta were few, and for this they
could be governed by few; the other, that, not accepting strangers into their
republic, they had occasion neither to corrupt themselves nor to grow so much
that it would be insupportable to those few who governed it.

## NM-DISC.1.6.04

Considering, then, all these things, one sees how it was necessary for the
legislators of Rome to do one of the two things, if they wished Rome to stay
quiet like the above-said republics, either not employ the plebs in war, like
the Venetians; or not open the path to strangers, like the Spartans. And they
did the one and the other, which gave the plebs force and augmentation, and
infinite occasions to make tumult. And if the Roman state had come to be more
quiet, this inconvenience would have followed from it, that it would also
have been weaker, because the path would have been cut off for it of being
able to come to that greatness at which it arrived. In such a way that, Rome
wishing to remove the causes of the tumults, it would have removed also the
causes of expanding. And in all human things one sees this, whoever will
examine them well: that one can never cancel one inconvenience without another
springing up from it. On that account, if you wish to make a people numerous
and armed, so as to be able to make a great empire, you make it of such a
quality that you cannot afterwards handle it in your own way; if you keep it
either small or unarmed so as to be able to handle it, if it acquires dominion
you cannot hold it, or it becomes so base that you are prey to whoever assails
you. And therefore in every deliberation of ours one must consider where
there are fewer inconveniences, and take that for the better course; for [a
thing] wholly clean, wholly without suspicion, is never found.

## NM-DISC.1.6.05

Rome, then, could, after the likeness of Sparta, make a prince for life, make
a small senate, but it could not, like that one, not grow the number of its
citizens, wishing to make a great empire; which made [it] that the king for
life, and the small number of the senate, as to union, would have availed it
little. If anyone, on that account, wished to order a republic anew, he would
be to examine whether he wished it to expand, like Rome, in dominion and in
power, or else to stay within narrow bounds. In the first case it is necessary
to order it like Rome, and to give place to the tumults and to the universal
dissensions as best one can; for without a great number of men, and well
armed, a republic will never be able to grow, or, if it grows, to maintain
itself. In the second case you can order it like Sparta or like Venice; but
because expanding is the poison of like republics, whoever orders them must,
in all those ways that one can, forbid them acquiring, for such acquisitions,
founded upon a weak republic, are altogether its ruin; as befell Sparta and
Venice, of which the first, having subjected almost all Greece to itself,
showed on a very slight accident its weak foundation; for, the rebellion of
Thebes having followed, caused by Pelopidas, the other cities rebelling, that
republic went altogether to ruin. Likewise Venice, having occupied a great
part of Italy, and the greater part not with war but with money and with
industry, when it was to make proof of its forces, lost everything in one
battle.

## NM-DISC.1.6.06

I would well believe that, to make a republic that should last a long time,
the best way would be to order it within like Sparta or like Venice; to set it
in a strong place, and of such power that no one would believe he could
oppress it at once; and, on the other hand, that it be not so great that it
should be formidable to its neighbours; and so it could long enjoy its state.
For war is made on a republic for two causes: the one, to become its lord; the
other, for fear that it may occupy you. These two causes the above-said way
almost wholly takes away; for if it is difficult to storm, as I presuppose it,
being well ordered for defence, it will seldom happen, or never, that anyone
can make a design of acquiring it. If it will keep within its bounds, and it be
seen by experience that there is no ambition in it, it will never occur that
anyone should make war on it for fear for himself; and so much the more would
this be so if there were in it a constitution or law that forbade it to
expand. And without doubt I believe that, if the thing could be held balanced
in this way, that would be the true political living and the true quiet of a
city. But, all the things of men being in motion, and not being able to stand
firm, it is fitting that they rise or that they descend; and to many things to
which reason does not induce you, necessity induces you; so that, having
ordered a republic apt to maintain itself [by] not expanding, and necessity
should lead it to expand, one would come to take away its foundations and to
make it go to ruin the sooner. So, on the other hand, when heaven should be so
benign to it that it were not to make war, it would arise from this that
idleness would make it either effeminate or divided; which two things,
together or each by itself, would be the cause of its ruin. On that account,
since one cannot, as I believe, balance this thing, nor maintain this middle
path exactly, it is needful in ordering the republic to think of the more
honourable part, and to order it in such a way that, should necessity after
all induce it to expand, it could conserve what it had occupied.

## NM-DISC.1.6.07

And to return to the first reasoning, I believe it is necessary to follow the
Roman order and not that of the other republics, for to find a middle way
between the one and the other I do not believe can be done; and those enmities
that should arise between the people and the senate, to tolerate them, taking
them for an inconvenience necessary to arrive at the Roman greatness. For,
besides the other reasons alleged, where the tribunician authority is
demonstrated to have been necessary for the guard of liberty, one can readily
consider the benefit that the authority of accusing does in republics, which
was committed to the tribunes among the others, as will be discoursed in the
following chapter.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Si poteva ordinare` is "could be ordered": the question is
asked as a possibility, and the chapter's answer is that the possibility had a
price. Guicciardini's reply turns the same `si poteva` into `si poteva fare`,
"it could be done".

**`Noi abbiamo discorsi di sopra`.** "We have discoursed above": the first of
the chapter's *currere* verbs (D01). It ends on the same verb, `si
discorrerà`, "will be discoursed"; between them `ricorrere`, "have recourse"
(.01), and `non occorrerà mai`, "it will never occur" (.06). Machiavelli's
discoursing frames the chapter.

**`controversie`, `inimicizie`.** "Controversies", "enmities": two words, two
words (D128). The rubric and the conclusion say enmities; the opening and the
question say controversies. The 1554 edition has `contese` for the first.

**`facevano`, `fece`.** "Made": `fare` is "make" throughout the chapter, of
effects as of kings, empires and war, except where its object is a thing done
(`fare una delle due cose`, "do one of the two things").

**`però`.** "Therefore" (D22).

**`se in Roma si poteva ordinare uno Stato`.** `Stato` is "state", as in I.2:
a form of government, not the modern nation-state (glossary, [open]).

**`Gentiluomini`, `Popolani`.** "Gentlemen", "commoners": the Venetian names
for those within the government and those outside it (D134). `Popolani` is
"commoners", as in I.5 (D118).

**`Il qual modo lo dette il caso`.** "Which way chance gave": `modo` is "way"
and `via` is "path" throughout (D131). Chance more than prudence ordered Venice;
the same contrast as I.2, where Rome reaches its mixed state by accident.

**`sendosi ridotti in su quelli scogli`.** "Having withdrawn onto those
rocks": the lagoon islands, settled, as I.1 says, by those fleeing the
barbarian invasions ("the causes stated above").

**`un vivere politico`.** "A political living": the infinitive as a noun, as
`vivere libero` is "free living". In .06 `il vero vivere politico` is "the
true political living".

**`chiusono la via ..., di potere convenire ne' loro governi`.** "Closed the
path ..., of being able to convene in their governments": the infinitive
phrase depends on `la via`, displaced to the end. The English keeps it there.
`Convenire` is "convene" both times.

**`fu fatto del governo`.** "Was made [part] of the government": admitted to
it (D53).

**`comodità`.** "Convenience" (D59): the opportunity to make tumult.

**`li teneva in freno`.** "Held them in rein": the bridle image, as I.3 `a
freno`, "reined in" (D88).

**`non sono stati molti, e di tanto numero`.** "Have not been many, or of such
a number": both witnesses print `e`, "and", where the sense needs the negative
carried on. English carries it with "or" (D129).

**`Sparta ..., potette mantenersi così lungo tempo; perché`.** The period's
first ground is Sparta's few inhabitants and closed door and Lycurgus' laws,
and a second, third and fourth `perché` explain the laws and their effect. The English keeps each
`perché` attached to what it explains.

**`le leggi di Licurgo ..., le quali osservando, levavano via`.** "Observing
which [they] removed": read with the Spartans as subject, who observe the laws
and remove the causes of tumults; the Italian can also make the laws the
subject of `levavano`. `Levare via` is "remove", kept apart from `togliere via` ("take
away"), the rubric's verb (D131).

**`più egualità di sustanze, e meno egualità di grado`.** "More equality of
substance and less equality of degree": more equal in wealth, less equal in rank. `Grado` is
"degree", as in I.2 and I.5.

**`dettero mai loro desiderio d'averli`.** "Ever give them the desire to have
them": the plebeians, the degrees.

**`Imperio`.** "Rule" (D124): the plebs, defended from injury, did not fear,
and did not desire rule. `un grande Imperio` in .04–.05 is "a great empire":
dominion over others.

**`forestieri`.** "Strangers" (D57).

**`a' legislatori di Roma era necessario fare una delle due cose`.** "It was
necessary for the legislators of Rome to do one of the two things": the
choices are not employing the plebs in war (Venice) and not admitting
strangers (Sparta). `E loro fecero l'una e l'altra`: Rome did both of the
things the remedies forbid, armed the plebs and admitted strangers, and so
applied neither. The English keeps "the one and the other".

**`augumento`.** "Augmentation" (D69). The 1554 edition prints
`accrescimento`, the noun of `accrescere`, "increase".

**`E se lo Stato romano veniva ad essere più quieto, ne seguiva ...`** "If the
Roman state had come to be more quiet, this inconvenience would have
followed": the imperfect indicative in a contrary-to-fact condition, as D47.
Rome did not become quieter; the sentence says what would have followed if it
had. `In modo che ... levava ancora` continues the counterfactual (D130).

**`inconveniente`.** "Inconvenience", in the older sense: a drawback that
follows from a course (D133). Three times in .04, and the chapter's last word
on the enmities: "an inconvenience necessary to arrive at the Roman
greatness".

**`ampliare`, `crescere`.** "Expand", "grow": two verbs, two words (D132).
`Non crescere il numero dei cittadini suoi` is "not grow the number of its
citizens", the verb used transitively.

**`Pertanto`.** "On that account" (D98).

**`maneggiare`.** "Handle": to manage, as a horse or a weapon.

**`sì vile`.** "So base": abject, without spirit.

**`E però in ogni nostra deliberazione si debbe`.** "And therefore in every
deliberation of ours one must": `però` (D22); `debbe` "must" (D120).

**`tutto netto, tutto senza sospetto`.** "[A thing] wholly clean, wholly
without suspicion": the Italian has no noun.

**`il Re a vita, e il picciolo numero del Senato`.** "The king for life, and
the small number of the senate": Sparta's two institutions. Rome could have
copied them, but not the small citizen body that let them unite the city, so
the copy would have availed little.

**`arebbe a esaminare`.** "He would be to examine" (D61).

**`il veleno di simili Repubbliche`.** "The poison of like republics":
`simile` is "like" (glossary); `tali acquisti` in the same period is "such
acquisitions".

**`la ribellione di Tebe, causata da Pelopida`.** Thebes, garrisoned by
Sparta since 382 BC, was freed by Pelopidas and others in 379; Sparta's
defeat at Leuctra in 371 followed. Machiavelli compresses the sequence into
"a very slight accident".

**`Vinegia ... perdette in una giornata ogni cosa`.** "Lost everything in one
battle": Agnadello, 14 May 1509, against the French army of the League of Cambrai. `Giornata`
is "battle" (D49). `Industria` is "industry", skill and diligence; the 1554
edition prints `astutia`, cunning.

**`Crederei bene`.** "I would well believe": his judgment, in the
conditional, of an ideal he is about to withdraw.

**`subito opprimere`.** "Oppress it at once": take it at a stroke.

**`come io la presuppongo`.** "As I presuppose it": `presupporre` is the
founder's working assumption (D82).

**`rade volte`.** "Seldom" (D97).

**`non occorrerà mai`.** "It will never occur" (D01).

**`per paura di sé`.** "For fear for himself": out of fear on his own
account.

**`constituzione o legge`.** "Constitution or law": a fundamental ordinance.
The 1554 edition prints `ordine`.

**`conviene che le saglino, o che le scendano`.** "It is fitting that they
rise or that they descend": `conviene`, impersonal, is "it is fitting" (D85).

**`a molte cose che la ragione non t'induce, t'induce la necessità`.** "To
many things to which reason does not induce you, necessity induces you": the
verb repeated, as in the Italian. The 1554 edition has `ti astringe`,
"constrains you", for the second.

**`avendo ordinata ... e la necessità la conducesse`.** "Having ordered ...,
and necessity should lead it": the period changes construction midway, from a
gerund to a conditional subjunctive. The English keeps the break.

**`quando il cielo le fusse sì benigno`.** "When heaven should be so benign to
it": `quando` with the subjunctive, conditional.

**`l'ozio`.** "Idleness", as in I.1, where it is good for Venice and a vice
elsewhere.

**`questa via del mezzo` / `un modo mezzo`.** "This middle path" / "a middle
way": two phrases, kept apart (D131).

**`bisogna`.** "It is needful" (D75).

**`quelle inimicizie ..., tollerarle`.** "Those enmities ..., to tolerate
them": the infinitive depends on `credo che sia necessario`, and the object
is set before it and resumed by the pronoun. The 1554 edition supplies `si
deurebbe`, "one should".

**`l'autorità tribunizia`.** "The tribunician authority": the argument of I.4
and I.5, where the tribunes are the guard of liberty.

**`facilmente`.** "Readily" (glossary).

**`l'autorità dello accusare`.** "The authority of accusing": the subject of
I.7.
