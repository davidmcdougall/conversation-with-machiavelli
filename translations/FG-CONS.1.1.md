# Consideration on chapter I of Book I

*What have universally been the beginnings of any city whatever, and what was
that of Rome*

## FG-CONS.1.1.01

In the first Discourse the distinction is true that all cities are built either
by strangers or by men native to the place, and in this second member fall
Venice and Athens, Rome falls there too, but differently from Athens and from
Venice, for these were built by the dwellers from necessity of having either a
secure refuge or a common government, but Rome, without any of these
necessities, was rather built as a colony of Alba, that is by men either Albans
or subjects of the rule of Alba, for love of those places where they had been
nurtured, or from ambition to govern themselves by themselves; nor can Rome, in
respect of Aeneas, be assigned to the member of the strangers, for it is a
seeking of origins too far off, which are not to be referred to the first
ancestors of whoever has built.

## FG-CONS.1.1.02

As to the member of cities built by strangers, it is not simply true that the
colonies sent to relieve the countries of inhabitants always depend on others,
for many nations, as were the Gauls, the Cimbri and the like, sent for the said
cause part of their peoples to seek themselves new seats, which, once acquired,
had no dependence on or recognition of their native places, and therefore a truer and fuller distinction would have been that either the cities built by strangers
are built in such a condition that they are to govern themselves by themselves,
nor depend in anything even on the authors of their origin, or they are built in such a way that they are to recognise those as princes, and in these second ones it
is true that from the beginning they cannot make great progress, but in progress
of time many accidents can arise that free them from that subjection, or then it
can happen that they take notable augmentation. And of this species has been
Florence, and all the colonies of the Romans, that after the decline of Rome many of them have become magnificent and powerful cities, and perhaps whoever discoursed
of them one by one would find no fewer of these risen to notable power than of
those that have had a free beginning, for they have grown or not according to
the site, the institution and fortune they have had. It is true that ordinarily
these have been slower to begin to grow, having their beginning subject to
others, but if meanwhile, through the goodness of the site or through good
institution or other cause, they have had occasion to swell with riches and with
people, they have then had facility to become powerful.

## FG-CONS.1.1.03

The principal foundation of the power and riches of the city is to have a large
people, and a city placed in a sterile place can ill swell with people, unless
it has a very generative air, like Florence, or the opportunity of the sea, like Venice, and therefore it is better to place oneself in fertile country, for the
inhabitants concur there more readily, but if it were possible to fix many inhabitants in a site, I do not say altogether sterile, but not fat,
there is no doubt that the necessity of providing for oneself would do more to
make it virtuous than good laws, for those can be varied by the will of men, but
necessity is a law and a continual stimulus. And this directed Rome well, which,
although placed in fertile country, all the same, through not having a
countryside and being ringed by powerful peoples, was forced to enlarge itself
with the *virtù* of arms and with concord, and this is discoursed not of a city
that wishes to live in the philosophical manner, but of those that wish to
govern themselves according to the common use of the world, as is necessary to do, otherwise they would be, being weak, oppressed and trampled by their
neighbours

## Notes

**The two editions.** This edition prints the text of the first edition of
the *Considerazioni*, Giuseppe Canestrini's (Florence, 1857), and compares it
with Roberto Palmarocchi's of 1933. Where they differ, the variants list gives
Palmarocchi's reading.

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply, in
his own spelling and without a full stop.

**The paragraphs.** Canestrini prints this reply in three paragraphs, and they
are kept. The edition's paragraphs are otherwise editorial.

**`concorrono`.** "The inhabitants concur there more readily": *concorrere* in
its first sense, to run together, of people flocking to a fertile place. It is
the verb Guicciardini uses elsewhere for assent — "I do not readily concur" —
and the English keeps it here, with `facilmente` as "readily", so that the
word's two senses stay one word.

**`discorressi`, `si discorre`.** Guicciardini answers in Machiavelli's own
verb: whoever "discoursed" of the colonies one by one; "this is discoursed" of
cities that live as the world does.

**Alba.** Alba Longa, the Latin city from which, in the legend, Romulus and
Remus came.

**Emendations.** Three words in Canestrini's printing are errors rather than
readings, and the Italian here corrects them from Palmarocchi, with the
corrected letters in brackets: `o paesi`, "or countries", to `[e'] paesi`, "the
countries"; `la colonie`, an article that does not agree, to `l[e] colonie`;
`fartile`, not a word, to `f[e]rtile`. Readings that make sense, even weaker
sense, are left as Canestrini prints them.

**`o allora`.** Canestrini's "or then"; Palmarocchi has "and then", which the
sense prefers. The English follows Canestrini.

**`tanto per non avere contado`.** "All the same, through not having a
countryside": `tanto` appears to carry the concession that `se bene` opened.
Palmarocchi reads the Latin `tamen`, nevertheless, to the same effect.

**`virtuoso` and `virtù`.** As in the facing chapter: *virtù* is capacity, and
"virtuous" is its adjective.

**`contado`.** The countryside a city rules and feeds on. Guicciardini says
Rome had none, and was hemmed in by powerful neighbours.

**The close.** The reply ends without a full stop in Canestrini's printing, and
the English ends without one.
