# Consideration on chapter II of Book I

*Of how many species republics are, and of which was the Roman republic*

## FG-CONS.1.2.01

And there is no doubt that the mixed government of the three species, princes,
optimates and people, is better and more stable than a simple government of any
of the three species, and especially when it is mixed in such a way that from
each species the good is taken and the bad left behind; which is the point it is
needful to take heed of, or where the fallacy of whoever orders them can lie.
And to discourse this article minutely, I say that the fruit of royal government
is that much better, with more order, with more celerity, with more secrecy,
with more resolution, the public things are governed when they depend on the
will of one alone than when they are at the discretion of several. The ill it
has is that if it falls upon a bad person, he having the power loosed to do ill,
all that authority which is given him to do good effects makes them very bad;
likewise, if he is good but insufficient, infinite disorders arise through his
sloth. And even though the king were made by choice, not by succession, there is
not entire security against these dangers, for whoever chooses can many times
deceive himself, reckoning good or prudent one who is of another sort, and the
greatness of the power and of the licence often changes the nature of whoever is
chosen, and especially if he has sons, it is difficult for him not to desire to
have them as successors, which, when he is king with absolute power, can with
difficulty be forbidden him, even though it be against the constitutions of the
kingdom, but he cannot indeed bring it about except with art and means not
laudable.

## FG-CONS.1.2.02

Wishing then to order a government that should partake as much as can be of the
good of royal government, and should not partake of the ill, it is impossible
that it partake of all the good and flee all the ill, and it is needful to be
content that it should rather have less of the good than, through wanting too
much of it, partake also of the ill. And therefore it is necessary to make it
perpetual, but to limit its authority, by making it so that by itself alone it
cannot dispose of anything, or at least only of those things that are of lesser
importance, and, ordering it so, one would get from it the good of having an eye
that should watch continually over the public things, a head to whom they might
be reported, a procurator who should propose them, press them and put them in
mind. One would lack that good which one alone's being able to deliberate and
execute carries with it, but because this cannot be had without the danger that
it be in his power to turn the kingdom to tyranny, it is a lesser ill to have
little good and secure than much and with so grave a danger. Let the king then,
that is, the head that is to represent that prince, be with authority limited in
such a way that by himself alone he cannot deliberate the important things, and
let him be by choice, not by succession, and when it is so, it is better that he
be perpetual than temporary, and if temporary all the same, better for a long
time than for a short. In which the Venetians have done better than did the
Romans and the Lacedaemonians, for the kings of the Lacedaemonians were always
of one same family and by succession, the Roman kings, although they had the
senate and some image of a republic, yet had so much authority that it was easy
for them to turn the kingdom to tyranny, as one sees some beginning in Servius
Tullius, and then openly in Tarquinius Superbus. And if we wish to call the
authority of the consuls royal, it was not perpetual but annual, whereas the
Venetian prince is perpetual, is chosen, and has a most limited authority.

## FG-CONS.1.2.03

In the government of the optimates there is this good, that, being several,
they cannot so readily make a tyranny as one alone, being the most qualified men
of the city, they govern it with more intellect and with more prudence than a
multitude would, and being honoured, they have less cause to trouble it, as,
being discontented, they could readily do. The ill is that, finding themselves
with great authority, they favour those things that are useful to them and that
oppress the people, and, the ambition of men having no bound for increasing
their condition, they break with one another and make sedition, whence arises,
either by way of tyranny or in another way, the ruin of cities; and if they are
optimates by succession and not by choice, from prudent and good men things soon
come into the hands of imprudent and bad.

## FG-CONS.1.2.04

It is needful to draw from this species of government what good one can, and to
flee the ill, that the optimates be not always the same lines and families, but
that from the whole body of the city, that is, from all those who according to
the laws are able to partake of the magistracies, a senate be chosen that is to
treat the arduous things, that is, that it be the flower of the prudent, noble or
rich men of the city, that it be perpetual, or at least that they last for a
very long time, that they be many in number, so that they may be more readily
tolerated by the others, who will have continual hope that they or their houses
may succeed in place of those who from day to day should fail, and also because,
the number being large, one will be able to hope that everyone who merits it may
enter it, and although some unfit man will enter it, it is a lesser
inconvenience than if some sufficient one were excluded from it, that they have
not absolute power over all the public things, so that they do not arrogate too
much authority to themselves, especially of creating magistrates, specially
those that have mere and mixed *imperium*, or that are magistrates of utility, nor of
making law without the consent of the people, so that they cannot either alter
the form of the government, or bring the orders of the city to the benefit of
the powerful and the diminution of the lesser, but that to them belong the
consulting and deliberating of those things for which the prudence of men is
most necessary, that is, wars, peaces, dealings with princes, and all the things
substantial to the conservation and augmentation of the dominion. The
Lacedaemonians had the optimates in this way, that is, not of a particular sort
of men, but of the whole body of the city; the Romans had them, but with a
distinction, for among them the patricians were from the beginnings the
optimates, the others were plebeians, which was the cause of all their
seditions.

## FG-CONS.1.2.05

In the government of the people there is, of good, that while it lasts there is
no tyranny, the laws can do more than men, and the end of all deliberations is
to look to the universal good. Of ill there is that the people through its
ignorance is not capable of deliberating the important things; and therefore a
republic that remits things to the consultation of the people is soon in peril;
it is unstable and ever desirous of new things, and therefore easy to be moved
and deceived by ambitious and seditious men; it willingly strikes at the
qualified citizens, which necessitates them to seek novelty and disturbance. To
flee these things, it is needful not to remit to the people any important
thing, except those which, if they were in the hands of others, liberty would
not be secure, as is the choice of magistrates, the creation of laws, which it
is not well should come to the people unless first digested and approved by the
supreme magistrates and by the senate, but those ordered by them should not
indeed have force unless they are confirmed by the people, not to leave the
harangues free, which is a great instrument of seditions, but that in the
council of the people none may speak except him to whom it is committed by the
magistrates, and on that matter which is committed to him. And ordering this
government so, one will have the mixture of which mention is made in the
Discourse

## Notes

**The two editions.** This edition prints the text of the first edition of
the *Considerazioni*, Giuseppe Canestrini's (Florence, 1857), and compares it
with Roberto Palmarocchi's of 1933. Where they differ, the variants list gives
Palmarocchi's reading.

**The rubric.** Canestrini sets Machiavelli's chapter title over the reply, in
his own spelling (`spezie`) and without a full stop.

**The paragraphs.** Canestrini prints this reply in five paragraphs, and they
are kept: one on the mixture and royal government, one on how to limit the
head, one each on the good and ill of the optimates and on how to build a
senate, and one on the people.

**`E non è dubio`.** The reply opens on "And", as if continuing a conversation:
Canestrini's `E`. Palmarocchi prints the pronoun `E'`, "It is not doubtful",
which is no conjunction.

**`discorrere`, `Volendo adunque`.** "To discourse this article minutely":
Machiavelli's verb, from his turn to Rome (NM .03, `Volendo adunque
discorrere`). The reply's next paragraph opens on the same construction,
`Volendo adunque ordinare`, and both are "Wishing then to ...".

**`principi`, `ottimati`, `popolo`.** The three species named by their
holders, princes, optimates and people, where Machiavelli names the states
(principate, optimates, popular). "Optimates" is kept for both.

**`bene` and `male`.** "Good" and "ill": the reply is built on them, the good
and ill of each simple government, the good to draw and the ill to flee. "Ill"
keeps `male` apart from `cattivo`, "bad", and `reo`, "evil". `Il buono`
(.01) and `di buono` (.05) are also "good": English cannot keep `buono` apart
from `bene` as it keeps `cattivo` from `male`.

**`riputando`.** "Reckoning" good or prudent: the verb of Guicciardini's
`reputo`, "I reckon", used here of the voter who reckons wrongly.

**`potestà`.** "Power": the power an office carries, `potestà sciolta`
(loosed), `assoluta`. `Potenza`, power as might, does not occur in the reply.

**`il potere uno solo deliberare e eseguire`.** "One alone's being able to
deliberate and execute": `potere` here is the verb as a noun, a capacity, not
the power of an office.

**`uno procuratore`.** The head as the public business's agent: one who puts
matters forward (`proponessi`), presses them (`sollecitassi`) and keeps them
before the others' minds (`ricordassi`). Matters are "reported" to him
(`referire`), the edition's word for `riferire` in both authors.

**The Venetian doge.** Elected for life and bound by the councils. Canestrini's
note adds that the doge could undertake nothing without the Great Council, the
*Pregadi* (the senate) and the *Collegio*.

**`Bisogna ... che`.** "It is needful": `bisogna` governs the two
infinitives and then every `che` with a subjunctive in the long .04 period
(that the optimates be not always the same families, that a senate be chosen,
that it be perpetual, that they be many, that they have not absolute power, that
to them belong ...), and again in .05. Canestrini prints no mark after `male`,
and a reader could take `che gli ottimati` as a relative on it; the English
reads it as depending on `Bisogna`. Palmarocchi's `Bisogna, a trarre ...` makes
that explicit; see the variants. `È necessario` (.02) keeps "it is necessary".

**`abili a partecipare de' magistrati`.** "Able to partake of the
magistracies": eligible for office. The English keeps "partake", the verb of
the mixed government in .02 and of Machiavelli's mixed state ("one that should
partake of all"). `Partecipi tutto il bene` in .02 is transitive but means to
share in, not to impart, as `participare` does in I.12.

**`mero e misto imperio`.** "Mere and mixed *imperium*": the jurists'
*merum et mixtum imperium*, from Roman law (Ulpian, *Digest* 2.1.3), kept in
Latin as a term of art, as English writers on Italian law keep it. *Merum*,
"pure", is the power of the sword, criminal jurisdiction to death or exile;
*mixtum* is civil jurisdiction joined to a lesser power of coercion. Together,
full criminal and civil jurisdiction: the senate is not to appoint those who
judge and punish. Elsewhere `imperio` is "empire", "rule" or "command" by sense
(D124).

**`magistrati di utilità`.** "Magistrates of utility": Canestrini's note glosses
them as the offices that apportioned taxes and administered the public revenues
and debt.

**`concioni`.** "Harangues": speeches to the assembled people, from the Latin
*contio*. Guicciardini would have no one speak in the council unless
commissioned, and only on the matter commissioned.

**`ridurre`.** "Bring": to bring into a state, not to diminish, which is
`diminuzione` in the same clause.

**`temporale`.** "Temporary", of a head's tenure; in I.12 the same word is
"temporal", of the Church's rule.

**`s'arà la mistizia`.** "One will have the mixture": `s'arà` is *si avrà*.
Canestrini prints a space where the apostrophe fell out; the apostrophe is
supplied. Palmarocchi reads `mistura` for `mistizia`, the same thing.

**The Romans.** Guicciardini's Lacedaemonian senate was drawn
from the whole city, Rome's from the patricians alone, and that, he says, was
the cause of all its seditions. Machiavelli's next chapters argue that Rome's
perfection came through the same disunion.

**The close.** The reply ends without a full stop in Canestrini's printing, and
the English ends without one.
