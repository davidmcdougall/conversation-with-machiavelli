# Discourses III.19

## NM-DISC.3.19.01

The Roman republic was stirred up by the enmities of the nobles and of the plebeians, nonetheless, war impending over them, they sent out with the armies Quinctius and Appius Claudius. Appius, through being cruel and rude in commanding, was ill obeyed by his own, so that, almost routed, he fled from his province. Quinctius, through being benign and of humane mind, had his soldiers obedient, and carried away the victory from it.

## NM-DISC.3.19.02

Whence it seems that, to govern a multitude, it is better to be humane than proud, merciful than cruel. Nonetheless Cornelius Tacitus, to whom many other writers consent, in a sentence of his concludes the contrary, when he says: *In multitudine regenda plus poena, quam obsequium valet.* And considering how one can save the one and the other of these opinions, I say: either you are to govern men who are, by the ordinary, companions to you, or men who are always subject to you.

## NM-DISC.3.19.03

When they are companions to you, one cannot wholly use penalty, nor that severity of which Cornelius reasons; and because the Roman plebs had in Rome equal rule with the nobility, one who became prince of it for a time could not handle it with cruelty and rudeness. And many times it was seen that the Roman captains who made themselves loved by the armies, and who handled them with compliance, made better fruit than those who made themselves extraordinarily feared, unless they were accompanied by an excessive *virtù*, as was Manlius Torquatus.

## NM-DISC.3.19.04

But whoever commands the subjects, of whom Cornelius reasons, must, in order that they not become insolent, and that through your too great facility they not trample on you, turn rather to penalty than to compliance. But this also must be moderated in such a way that one flee hatred; because to make oneself hated never turns out well for any prince. The way of fleeing it is to leave alone the property of the subjects, because, of blood, when rapine is not hidden under it, no prince is desirous unless necessitated, and this necessity comes seldom; but rapine being mixed in, it comes always, nor do the causes and the desire to shed it ever fail, as has been discoursed at large on this matter in another treatise.

## NM-DISC.3.19.05

Quinctius, then, merited more praise than Appius; and the sentence of Cornelius, within its terms, and not in the cases observed by Appius, merits being approved. And because we have spoken of penalty and of compliance, it does not seem to me superfluous to show how an example of humanity could [do] more with the Faliscans than arms.

## Notes

**The rubric.** `Se a reggere una moltitudine è più necessario l'ossequio che la pena.` "Whether, to govern a multitude, compliance is more necessary than penalty": `reggere`, govern (D64, a recorded collapse with `governare`, .02); `moltitudine`, multitude (D402), kept apart from `Plebe`, plebs, and `Popolo`, people; `ossequio`, compliance, and `pena`, penalty (D504), the two Italian words for Tacitus' *obsequium* and *poena* (.02), kept as one pair through the chapter and in Guicciardini's rubric. In the Latin *obsequium* is the governed's, compliance with the ruler; Machiavelli makes `ossequio` the commander's way of handling men (.03) and the ruler's resort (.04), and the one English word carries both, as the Italian does; "penalty" leaves "punish" to Guicciardini's `punire`. The rubric's question is answered by the distinction of .02, not by a verdict. The 1554 sets the same sentence as its heading; Canestrini sets it over Guicciardini's reply with `lo ossequio` (NM-DISC-III19-D007; D503).

**`Era la Repubblica romana sollevata per le inimicizie de' Nobili e de' Plebei, nondimeno soprastando loro la guerra, mandarono fuori con gli eserciti Quinzio ed Appio Claudio`.** "The Roman republic was stirred up by the enmities of the nobles and of the plebeians, nonetheless, war impending over them, they sent out with the armies Quinctius and Appius Claudius": the fronted verb of the first clause set in plain order (D263); `sollevato`, stirred up (D496); `inimicizie`, enmities (D128); `Nobili`, nobles, `Plebei`, plebeians (glossary), lower case as the glossary sets them; `nondimeno`, nonetheless (D45); `soprastare`, impend over (D504); the subject passes without notice from the republic to an unnamed "they", as printed, and the comma before `nondimeno` is kept. Titus Quinctius Capitolinus and Appius Claudius, consuls in 471 BC (names.md; Livy 2.56-60, the editor's reference); this Appius is neither the decemvir of I.40 nor the Attus Clausus of FG I.4.

**`Appio per essere crudele e rozzo nel comandare, fu male ubbidito dai suoi, tanto che quasi rotto si fuggì della sua Provincia`.** "Appius, through being cruel and rude in commanding, was ill obeyed by his own, so that, almost routed, he fled from his province": `per essere`, through being, causal, as `per essere stato` (FG III.17); `crudele`, cruel, with `crudeltà` cruelty (.03); `rozzo`, rude (D207), with `rozzezza` rudeness (.03); `ubbidire`, obey (D504); `rotto`, routed (D431); `Provincia`, province (glossary), here in the Roman sense, the field of war assigned to a consul.

**`Quinzio per essere benigno e di umano ingegno, ebbe i suoi soldati ubbidienti, e riportonne la vittoria`.** "Quinctius, through being benign and of humane mind, had his soldiers obedient, and carried away the victory from it": `benigno`, benign (D504); `umano`, humane, of disposition, kept apart from `umanità`, humanity (.05); `ingegno`, mind (D178); `riportare`, carry away (glossary), `ne` "from it", the war.

**`Donde e' pare che sia meglio a governare una moltitudine essere umano che superbo, pietoso che crudele`.** "Whence it seems that, to govern a multitude, it is better to be humane than proud, merciful than cruel": `Donde`, whence; the 1554 reads `Onde`, the same sense (NM-DISC-III19-D001; D501); `pare`, it seems, the appearance the next sentence contests; `governare`, govern (D64); `superbo`, proud (glossary `superbia`); `pietoso`, merciful (D504), not "pitiful".

**`Nondimeno Cornelio Tacito, al quale molti altri Scrittori acconsentono`.** "Nonetheless Cornelius Tacitus, to whom many other writers consent": `nondimeno`, nonetheless (D45); `al quale`, to whom, the dative kept; `acconsentire`, consent (D504), not "concur", which is `concorrere`'s (SCOPE §3); `Scrittori`, writers. The 1554 reads `conſentono` (NM-DISC-III19-D002; D501).

**`in una sua sentenza conchiude il contrario, quando dice: *In multitudine regenda plus poena, quam obsequium valet.*`** "In a sentence of his concludes the contrary, when he says": `sentenza`, sentence (glossary); `conchiudere`, conclude. The Latin, as the 1824 prints it, means "in governing a multitude, penalty avails more than compliance". No sentence in these words has been found in Tacitus (the editor's reference); the nearest is *Annals* 3.55, where Tacitus says the reverse, that compliance towards the prince and the love of emulating him availed more than the penalty of the laws and fear (`obsequium inde in principem et aemulandi amor validior quam poena ex legibus et metus`; checked against the Latin text at thelatinlibrary.com). The 1554 has `Quando ait` and adds an Italian rendering, which the 1824 does not print and the edition does not carry (NM-DISC-III19-D002; D501).

**`E considerando come si possa salvare l'una e l'altra di queste opinioni, dico`.** "And considering how one can save the one and the other of these opinions, I say": `salvare`, save, to keep both opinions true; `opinioni`, opinions (D02); `dico`, I say, Machiavelli's own distinction.

**`o che tu hai a reggere uomini che ti sono per l'ordinario compagni, o uomini che ti sono sempre soggetti`.** "Either you are to govern men who are, by the ordinary, companions to you, or men who are always subject to you": `avere a`, be to (D261), of the case one has in hand; `per l'ordinario`, by the ordinary (D151), set against `sempre`, always, in the second member, which is why the prince of .03 is only `a tempo`; `compagni`, companions (D452), extended here from Rome's allies to equals in the city (D504); `soggetti`, subject (D488, a recorded collapse with `sudditi`, subjects, .04).

**`Quando ti sono compagni, non si può interamente usare la pena, né quella severità di che ragiona Cornelio`.** "When they are companions to you, one cannot wholly use penalty, nor that severity of which Cornelius reasons": `interamente`, wholly; `severità`, severity (D504), the word Guicciardini opens with; `ragionare di`, reason of (D504), to treat of.

**`e perché la Plebe romana aveva in Roma eguale imperio con la Nobiltà, non poteva uno che ne diventava Principe a tempo, con crudeltà e rozzezza maneggiarla`.** "And because the Roman plebs had in Rome equal rule with the nobility, one who became prince of it for a time could not handle it with cruelty and rudeness": `Plebe`, plebs, `Nobiltà`, nobility (glossary); `imperio`, rule (D124), sovereignty shared, not empire over others; `Principe a tempo`, prince for a time, a consul or other magistrate set over the plebs for a term (the editor's reading); `ne`, of it, Rome or the plebs, left open as in the Italian, the editor reading the plebs; the 1554 omits it (NM-DISC-III19-D003; D501); `maneggiare`, handle (glossary).

**`E molte volte si vide che miglior frutto fecero i Capitani romani, che si facevano amare dagli eserciti, e che con ossequio li maneggiavano, che quelli che si facevano straordinariamente temere`.** "And many times it was seen that the Roman captains who made themselves loved by the armies, and who handled them with compliance, made better fruit than those who made themselves extraordinarily feared": the fronted object `miglior frutto` set in plain order (D263); `frutto`, fruit (D504), with `fare`, make (D410); `Capitani`, captains (glossary); `con ossequio`, with compliance, here the commander's towards his soldiers; `amare`, love, and `temere`, fear (glossary `timore`); `straordinariamente`, extraordinarily (D151), beyond the ordinary.

**`se già e' non erano accompagnati da una eccessiva virtù, come fu Manlio Torquato`.** "Unless they were accompanied by an excessive *virtù*, as was Manlius Torquatus": `se già ... non`, unless, the exception; `già` with the negative is not rendered (D374, D423), "indeed" staying `anzi`'s (D147); `eccessivo`, excessive (D504); *virtù* untranslated (glossary). Titus Manlius Torquatus (names.md, as I.11), whose severity is the subject of III.22 (the editor's cross-reference).

**`Ma chi comanda ai sudditi, de' quali ragiona Cornelio, acciocché non diventino insolenti, e che per troppa tua facilità non ti calpestino, debbe volgersi piuttosto alla pena che all'ossequio`.** "But whoever commands the subjects, of whom Cornelius reasons, must, in order that they not become insolent, and that through your too great facility they not trample on you, turn rather to penalty than to compliance": in the Italian the two purposes stand between the subject and `debbe`; the English moves "must" before them, so that they attach to the turning and not to the commanding, no inversion being involved; `ai sudditi`, the subjects, with the article, the second member of .02; `sudditi`, subjects (D488); `acciocché`, in order that (D423), its subjunctives without "should" (D348); `insolente`, insolent (D464); `facilità`, facility (glossary), ease of temper, readiness to give way; the person shifts to "you", as printed; `calpestare`, trample on (D504); `debbe`, must (D274); `volgersi`, turn (glossary). The 1554 reads `felicità`, happiness or good fortune, so that the subjects trample on the commander through his too great good fortune and not through his facility (NM-DISC-III19-D004; D501).

**`Ma questa ancora debbe essere in modo moderata, che si fugga l'odio`.** "But this also must be moderated in such a way that one flee hatred": `ancora`, also (D472), a sense beside D496's "still"; `in modo ... che`, in such a way that (glossary); `moderato`, moderated; `fuggire`, flee (D325); `odio`, hatred (D504).

**`perché farsi odiare non torna mai bene ad alcun Principe`.** "Because to make oneself hated never turns out well for any prince": `tornare bene`, turn out well (D504).

**`Il modo del fuggirlo è lasciare stare la roba de' sudditi`.** "The way of fleeing it is to leave alone the property of the subjects": `modo`, way (glossary); `lasciare stare`, leave alone (`lasciare`, leave, D464; "let alone" is D226's); `roba`, property (D504), goods and possessions.

**`perché del sangue, quando non vi sia sotto ascosa la rapina, nessun Principe ne è desideroso se non necessitato`.** "Because, of blood, when rapine is not hidden under it, no prince is desirous unless necessitated": the fronted genitive `del sangue` kept first, answered by `ne` (D263, a contrast with `la roba`); `sangue`, blood (D410); `ascoso`, hidden; `rapina`, rapine (D504); `desideroso`, desirous (glossary `desiderare`); `se non necessitato`, unless necessitated; the 1554 reads `non neceſsitato`, not being necessitated (NM-DISC-III19-D005; D501).

**`e questa necessità viene rare volte; ma sendovi mescolata la rapina, viene sempre`.** "And this necessity comes seldom; but rapine being mixed in, it comes always": `rare volte`, seldom (D97); `mescolare`, mix.

**`né mancano mai le cagioni e il desiderio di spargerlo`.** "Nor do the causes and the desire to shed it ever fail": `mancare`, fail (D206); `cagioni`, causes (glossary, a recorded collapse with Guicciardini's `causa`); `spargere`, shed; `lo`, it, the blood. The 1554 reads `ſpargerla`, a feminine whose only sensible antecedent is `rapina` (NM-DISC-III19-D006; D501).

**`come in altro Trattato sopra questa materia s'è largamente discorso`.** "As has been discoursed at large on this matter in another treatise": `discorrere`, discourse (SCOPE §3, the *currere* family); `largamente`, at large (D464). The other treatise is *The Prince*, chapter 17, on being feared without being hated and abstaining from the property of subjects (the editor's reference).

**`Meritò dunque più laude Quinzio che Appio`.** "Quinctius, then, merited more praise than Appius": `dunque`, then, as `adunque` (D74), which the 1554 prints; `laude`, praise (glossary); the fronted verb set in plain order (D263).

**`e la sentenza di Cornelio dentro ai termini suoi, e non ne' casi osservati da Appio, merita d'essere approvata`.** "And the sentence of Cornelius, within its terms, and not in the cases observed by Appius, merits being approved": `termini`, terms (D401), the bounds of the sentence's case, subjects; `osservare`, observe, here of cases met with in practice. The sentence holds within its terms; Appius' case, of companions, is outside them.

**`E perché noi abbiamo parlato della pena e dell'ossequio, non mi pare superfluo mostrare, come un esempio d'umanità poté appresso ai Falisci più che l'armi`.** "And because we have spoken of penalty and of compliance, it does not seem to me superfluous to show how an example of humanity could [do] more with the Faliscans than arms": `umanità`, humanity (D504); `potere più`, can [do] more (D53), as at III.17; `appresso a`, with, among; `Falisci`, Faliscans (names.md); `armi`, arms. The sentence announces III.20, whose rubric it anticipates.
